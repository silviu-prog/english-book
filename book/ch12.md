# 12. Yeshua in Today's World

## The false prophets of the last days

"In the last days many false prophets will arise" (Matthew 24:11). Few of Yeshua's statements have been so frequently invoked to instill fear, justify exclusions, and reinforce religious authorities. Read, however, in His own logic rather than in the institutional logic that developed later, this sentence does not describe an apocalyptic scenario populated by mysterious figures, but a condition of human consciousness.

The "last days" are not merely a historical moment, but those recurring periods in which a person loses inner discernment and begins to seek truth on the outside: in the spiritual leader, in doctrine, in the promise of salvation offered by someone else. That is why Yeshua's immediate warning is surprisingly personal: "See to it that no one deceives you" (Matthew 24:4). The emphasis does not fall on identifying others, but on inner vigilance.

He does not say who will come, but what can happen to you. The problem is not the appearance of false prophets, but the loss of your own capacity to see. In His vision, the false prophet is not defined primarily by doctrinal error, but by the absence of living fruit. The criterion shifts radically from "what he says" to "what he produces." This shift becomes explicit when Yeshua says: "Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves. You will know them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:15–16). A false prophet may speak impeccably, may quote Scripture, may invoke the name of God, but his fruit is fear, separation, and dependence. He does not bring you closer to truth, but to himself. He does not make you alive, but compliant. Precisely for this reason, Yeshua warns against seeking truth on the outside, as an exclusive presence or a sacralized place: "If they say to you, 'Look, here is the Christ' or 'There he is,' do not believe it" (Matthew 24:23).

Truth does not come in order to be followed blindly, but in order to be recognized inwardly. It does not create attachment to a person, but clarity within you. In Paul's writings, however, an important shift of emphasis appears. His context is different: young, fragile communities, exposed to multiple influences. The danger is no longer authority without love, but the wrong message, which can destabilize the shared faith. Discernment is no longer predominantly inner, but doctrinal.

That is why he writes: "The time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching, but will gather to themselves teachers to suit their own desires" (2 Timothy 4:3). For Paul, the false prophet is the one who preaches "another gospel," and truth becomes something that must be guarded, delimited, and defended: "Even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed" (Galatians 1:8).

This shift would decisively influence the way institutional Christianity would understand, over the centuries, the idea of the false prophet. In many contemporary contexts, Yeshua's warning is applied almost exclusively to those outside the accepted boundaries: other denominations, other religions, other interpretations. Discernment turns into suspicion, and truth becomes property.

Thus we arrive at the painful paradox that Yeshua Himself was considered a false prophet by the religious authority of His own time, precisely because He did not align Himself with the dominant interpretation. Of Him it was said: "He has a demon and is leading the people astray" (John 7:12). Read in the spirit of Yeshua, the warning about false prophets is not an invitation to hunt down others, but to recognize any voice that interposes itself between a person and the truth.

Any teaching that produces fear, dependence, and separation, no matter how theologically correct it sounds, does not come from truth. Because, as He says clearly: "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32). If it does not make you free, if it does not bring you closer to yourself, to life, and to love, then it is not truth.

This, in the end, is the true "last days": the moment in which a person is placed before a subtle but decisive choice, between authority and presence, between fear and living truth, between following someone and seeing with one's own eyes.

## What Yeshua would say about today's Christianity

The Gospels show clearly that Yeshua was never a validator of religious systems, but a discerner of the heart. That is why His reaction would not be a global one ("good" or "bad"), but a differentiated one: He would separate life from ideology, love from fear, truth from power. Yeshua's first question would not be about dogma, morality, or ritual, but about the Kingdom. "The Kingdom of God is near… it is in your midst" (Mark 1:15; Luke 17:21).

Today's Christianity speaks much about salvation, but relatively little about the Kingdom as life lived now. For Yeshua, the observation would be quiet but radical: the Kingdom has been moved from the present into the future, from life lived into doctrine formulated, from real transformation into a deferred promise. What He proclaimed as a living reality, accessible here and now, has gradually become a distant horizon, conditioned by the correctness of one's belief and by belonging to a system.

The Kingdom, which for Yeshua was encounter, awakening, and a change of inner position, has been transformed into an explanation about what will come later. Thus life has been replaced by waiting, presence by doctrine, and the call to transformation by a promise that no longer asks for living, but only for acceptance.

## Yeshua would be troubled by the centrality of fear

For Yeshua, one of the most troubling mutations would be the centrality of fear. Yeshua constantly says "do not be afraid" and ties this call to trust in life and in the Father who provides (Matthew 6:25–34; Luke 12:32). By contrast, today's Christianity, in many of its forms, ends up being structured on fear: fear of sin, fear of punishment, fear of hell, fear of doctrinal error. Yeshua would immediately recognize this dynamic, because He already confronted it during His own life: "You tie up heavy burdens and lay them on people's shoulders" (Matthew 23:4). His critique is not aimed at moral care or responsibility, but at the use of fear as a spiritual instrument, as an engine of conformity.

For Yeshua, fear is not proof of holiness, but a sign that a person is not living from relationship with the Father, but from insecurity, from distrust, and from a distorted image of God.

## Yeshua would distinguish between faith and faith-ism

For Yeshua, faith is never confused with intellectual assent to a doctrine. Yeshua does not ask people to believe something correct about Him in order to be accepted; He calls them into a way of life. "Come, follow Me" (Mark 1:17) is not an invitation to theological definition, but to existential following. The emphasis falls on walking, not on formulating; on living, not on declaring. Faith is inner movement and a change of perspective, not ideological positioning.

Today's Christianity, however, has shifted the emphasis from following to adherence. To be "a believer" has come to mean upholding a set of correct statements, defending a doctrine, and identifying with a camp. Yeshua would probably observe much talk about Him, much defense of Him, and very little imitation of His way of living. His question would remain the same, just as uncomfortable: "Why do you call Me 'Lord, Lord,' and not do what I tell you?" (Luke 6:46).

Yeshua does not seek admirers, nor apologists, but people who live from the same perspective of love, truth, and freedom.

## Yeshua would be critical of excessive institutionalization

Yeshua did not build an institution. He set in motion a movement of life. He went from place to place, touched people, healed, disturbed consciences, and called to awakening. Today's Christianity, however, is largely a consolidated institution, with hierarchies, rules, clear boundaries, and mechanisms of exclusion. The structure has become stable, but life has often been moved to the background.

Yeshua would immediately recognize the signs He already denounced: authority that legitimizes itself, administered truth, conditioned access to God, spiritual power confused with office. "You have taken away the key of knowledge" (Luke 11:52) is not a critique of organization, but of monopolization. For Yeshua, the problem is not the existence of institutions, but the moment when structure replaces life. Wherever form becomes more important than the person, meaning is lost.

## Yeshua would be surprised by the obsession with sexuality

One of the greatest distortions would be, for Yeshua, the centrality of sexuality in contemporary Christianity. He speaks relatively little about sexuality and a great deal about money, power, judgment, hypocrisy, and fear. Today's Christianity often does exactly the opposite. Sexuality has become a major criterion of spiritual evaluation, while the lack of compassion, greed, or the abuse of power are treated as secondary.

Yeshua would observe how sexuality has become a criterion of exclusion, how shame has been sacralized, and how relationships are judged more harshly than the absence of love. His words would remain just as scandalously current: "The tax collectors and the prostitutes go into the Kingdom of God ahead of you" (Matthew 21:31). Not because sin is negligible, but because moral rigidity without love blocks life.

## Yeshua would affirm what is alive

At the same time, Yeshua would not reject all of Christianity. He would immediately recognize what is alive. He would see the communities that heal, the people who live love without conditions, those who draw near to the excluded, and faith lived in silence rather than displayed. "Do not stop him" (Mark 9:39–40) remains His criterion. Yeshua is not jealous of religious forms, nor of "incorrect" language. He is attentive to life.

Wherever someone loves, forgives, heals, and lives from truth, Yeshua would say that the Kingdom is already there. For Yeshua, the Kingdom comes before doctrine, life before the system, love before fear, and truth is that which sets free. In many forms of today's Christianity, the order has been reversed: doctrine has come before life, the institution before the person, fear before freedom, and truth has become conditioning rather than healing.

If Yeshua were to look at today's Christianity, He probably would not ask whether it is doctrinally correct, but whether it is alive. He would not ask whether it observes the rules, but whether it sets people free. And, most likely, He would repeat exactly the same words that troubled religion from the beginning: the Kingdom of God is here, now, in life. But you have grown accustomed to speaking about it without living it anymore.

## How would Yeshua react if He walked into a church today?

Yeshua does not judge by denominational labels, rituals, or doctrinal correctness, but by life. Where there is healing, freedom, truth, and love, there He recognizes the Kingdom (Matthew 11:4–6; Luke 17:21). Yeshua would not be impressed by architecture, symbols, or antiquity. "Not one stone here will be left upon another" (Matthew 24:2). His gaze would rest upon the people: fear or freedom, shame or dignity, life or constraint.

Just as He entered the Temple and observed what was being done in the name of God (Matthew 21:12–13), He would assess whether the religious space eases burdens (Matthew 11:28) or multiplies them (Matthew 23:4), whether it brings people closer to the Father or makes them dependent on intermediation. Yeshua would recognize a profound tension: much talk about Him and relatively little living like Him.

He would see emphasis on correct beliefs more than on transformed hearts, an obsession with sin more than with healing, fear of error more than trust in the Father. He would repeat the uncomfortable question: "Why do you call Me 'Lord, Lord,' and not do what I tell you?" (Luke 6:46). He would not reject Christianity as a whole, but He would separate living faith from ideology, the communities that heal from those that control, love from fear.

## Yeshua in a Catholic church

If Yeshua were to enter a Catholic church, He would immediately recognize the weight of historical memory. He would sense the continuity of the centuries, the awareness of a tradition that has crossed empires, collapses, and rebirths. He would see the institutionalized care for the poor, for the sick, for those forgotten by the world, and He would recognize in this an authentic echo of His own words: "I was hungry and you gave Me food; I was a stranger and you welcomed Me" (Matthew 25:35–40).

The sense of universality, the desire to embrace all of humanity, would not be foreign to Him. At the same time, Yeshua would be troubled by the excessive hierarchy and the great distance between the clergy and the people. He would observe how access to God is often strictly mediated, administered, and conditioned, how grace risks becoming a good distributed from the top down. The theology of structural guilt — the idea that a person starts from a position of culpability that must be managed sacramentally — would come into tension with His way of forgiving directly, through encounter. The words "You have taken away the key of knowledge" (Luke 11:52) would resound not as a condemnation of the sacraments in themselves, but as a radical question: do these symbols set free or condition? Do they bring a person closer to the Father or make him dependent on intermediation?

For Yeshua, the problem would not be the richness of ritual, but the risk that structure might take the place of life.

## Yeshua in an Orthodox church

In an Orthodox church, Yeshua would immediately recognize the silence. He would recognize the inner space, the mysticism, the deep breathing of a prayer that does not explain but contemplates. The emphasis placed on the healing of one's being, on the transformation of a person from within, would be in agreement with His call to wholeness. The liturgical continuity and the living bond with the past would not seem foreign to Him. But Yeshua would also observe the risks.

He would see how ritual can become formalism, how tradition can be confused with truth itself, how the gesture can become separated from life. He would sense the sacralization of fear in expressions such as "you are not worthy," which shift the center of gravity from the love of the Father to the assessment of human unworthiness. He would observe the distance between the liturgy and concrete life, between the beauty of the service and the way people treat one another.

"This people honors Me with their lips, but their heart is far from Me" (Matthew 15:8) would not be said as rejection, but as a gentle and firm call for the return of the heart. Yeshua would love the silence, but He would ask whether it gives birth to compassion. He would love the mystery, but He would ask whether it heals fear or hides it beneath the sacred.

## Yeshua in a neo-Protestant church

In a neo-Protestant church, Yeshua would immediately recognize the desire for a direct relationship with God. He would see the emphasis on Scripture, on personal reading, on the living community, and on the simplicity of forms. He would recognize the hunger for authenticity and the desire to live the faith without ritual intermediaries. At the same time, He would be attentive to other risks.

He would observe the tendency to reduce faith to correct doctrine, to absolutize "the correct decision" as the saving moment, to turn spiritual life into a continuous test of conformity. Moral pressure and the fear of error would appear as constant tensions, and the Gospel would risk becoming a system of evaluation rather than a space of healing. "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13) would remain His criterion.

He would not reject the zeal, nor the seriousness, nor the desire for truth, but He would ask whether these produce life or tension, freedom or anxiety. For Yeshua, Scripture is not a weapon of correctness, but a window toward truth, and the community is not a tribunal, but a place for lifting up those who have fallen. In all these forms, Yeshua would not seek institutional perfection. He would seek the signs of life.

Where people are freer, more whole, more capable of loving, there He would say the Kingdom is present. And where fear, control, and conditioning take the place of truth, there He would call again — not to a change of label, but to a change of heart. Most likely, Yeshua would not deliver a theological discourse, would not make denominational comparisons, and would not validate or invalidate institutions. He would stand beside the one who is shamed, the one who is excluded, the one who is tired of religion, the one who feels "unworthy."

And He would say what He always said: "Do not be afraid." "Take courage." "The Kingdom of God is near." Yeshua would not ask which denomination is right, but where life is. He would not seek the perfect church, but the open heart. And He would probably leave the building, walking toward those who no longer enter churches but still long for truth. "The Kingdom of God is not coming in ways that can be observed" (Luke 17:20).

Yeshua does not preach a doctrine, but a state of being. His words are not explanations about God, but invitations to awaken. He does not say "believe this," but "follow Me." He does not build a system of ideas, but opens a path of inner transformation. His preaching is performative: what He says happens in the one who listens. "Repent, for the Kingdom of Heaven is near" (Matthew 4:17). For Yeshua, repentance does not mean guilt, but a change of mind, a shift of the center of consciousness. Yeshua speaks in parables not to be poetic, but to avoid the hardening of truth. A parable cannot be turned into dogma without being killed. "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Matthew 11:15). His truth is not meant to be repeated, but to be recognized. He does not explain the Kingdom; He points to it.

"The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). Yeshua's preaching is always about now. He does not promise future salvation in exchange for a correct belief, but present life through truth lived. "Not everyone who says to Me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the Kingdom of Heaven, but the one who does the will of My Father" (Matthew 7:21). The emphasis is on being, not on affirming. On living, not on belonging.

Paul radically changes the register. He does not preach the Kingdom, but salvation. He does not preach a state of consciousness, but the central event: the cross. "We preach Christ crucified" (1 Corinthians 1:23). The message becomes explanatory, theological, soteriological. Instead of "follow Me," there appears "believe that." Instead of immediate inner transformation, there appears justification by faith. For Paul, truth is no longer recognized inwardly, but accepted intellectually.

"A person is justified by faith, apart from the works of the law" (Romans 3:28). Here the subtle rupture appears: life lived is no longer the primary criterion, but adherence to a correct formula. Faith becomes a substitute for transformation rather than its natural expression. This shift makes the message transmissible on a large scale. Paul builds the language, the structure, the theology that allow a global religion to emerge. But at the same time, the message can become separated from life.

You can believe without changing. You can confess without living. You can be "in Christ" theologically without being alive existentially. Today's preachers carry this shift even further. In many contexts, preaching is no longer either an invitation to awaken or a profound theological reflection, but emotional management, moralizing, or spiritual marketing. The sermon becomes either a list of rules, or a promise of prosperity, or a motivational speech dressed in religious clothing.

Truth is no longer something that changes you, but something that soothes you or validates you. In contemporary preaching, the emphasis often falls on sin, fear, belonging, and authority. "Believe correctly in order to be safe." "Belong in order to be saved." "Observe the doctrine in order to be accepted." Within this framework, Yeshua is often presented not as a model of life, but as a legal solution to a theological problem. The cross becomes a contract rather than a revelation.

Yeshua, however, does not preach fear, but freedom. "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32). Paul preaches liberation from the Law through faith, but leaves room for a new law: that of doctrine. Today's preachers, in many cases, preach psychological safety rather than freedom. Order rather than life. Conformity rather than awakening. The essential difference is not one of style, but of meaning. Yeshua preaches from being to being. Paul preaches from interpretation to belief.

Today's preachers often preach from the institution toward behavior. Yeshua awakens. Paul explains. The preachers administer. "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). For Yeshua, truth is alive. For Paul, truth is formulated. For today's preachers, truth is often defended. But the truth that must be defended no longer sets free. The truth that is lived, does. Yeshua did not ask to be preached, but to be embodied.

"You are the light of the world" (Matthew 5:14). Not "speak about the light," but be light. Here the circle closes. Authentic preaching is not the discourse from the pulpit, but the life that becomes visible. The rest are echoes — some useful, others distorting.

## How Yeshua would speak with the Pope

With Yeshua, the dialogue would never begin in a ceremonial register. Regardless of the person, the office, or the era, Yeshua would speak directly, personally, and without detour. That is how He spoke with the high priest, with the Pharisees, and with the rulers of the synagogues: without hostility, but also without flattery. His respect is not formal, but a respect for truth. He does not flatter roles and does not attack people; He asks questions that open or unmask.

Yeshua would recognize the care for the poor and the messages of peace, because these touch the heart of the Gospel. "I was hungry and you gave Me food; I was a stranger and you welcomed Me" (Matthew 25:35–40). But precisely because He recognizes what is alive, He would ask clearly, without ambiguity. Do you bring people closer to the Father or to the institution? "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6) does not indicate a bureaucratic route, but a living relationship. Then He would ask about burdens.

Do you ease burdens or organize them better? Yeshua was consistently critical of systems that make weight more efficient instead of lifting it. "They tie up heavy burdens and lay them on people's shoulders" (Matthew 23:4). For Him, authentic service does not refine control, but reduces the weight that presses on the conscience. The question about authority would be just as clear. Does your authority heal or condition?

When Yeshua forgives and heals, He does not do it through procedure, but through encounter: "Son, your sins are forgiven" (Mark 2:5–12). Authority that comes from truth sets free at once; authority that comes from a role demands steps, approvals, and proofs. Yeshua would not contest the pastoral role nor the need for organization, but He would relativize mediation as an end in itself. "The Father seeks worshipers in spirit and in truth" (John 4:23). Here the criterion is clear: worship does not necessarily pass through a channel, but through an open heart. He would honor the service that sets free, not the one that administers access. In this dialogue, Yeshua would not demand structural reforms as the ultimate goal. He would demand clarity of perspective. Where authority heals, there it is from God. Where it conditions, there fear has crept in.

And there, Yeshua would speak not as an adversary, but as a witness of the truth who calls again to life.

## How Yeshua would speak with the Orthodox Patriarch

The encounter with an Orthodox Patriarch would have a point of deep resonance. Yeshua would immediately recognize the value of silence, of inner withdrawal, and of prayer that descends into the heart. "When you pray, go into your room, shut the door, and pray to your Father in secret" (Matthew 6:6). This call to interiority is in full continuity with Orthodox mysticism and with the emphasis placed on the healing of one's being, not merely on the correctness of behavior.

Yeshua would recognize the beauty of the tradition and the living memory of a faith preserved through the centuries. He would see in the liturgy not merely a ritual, but a lived story of the community, a language of the heart passed on from generation to generation. Continuity would not be foreign to Him, because He Himself is inscribed in a living history, not in an arbitrary rupture. But precisely because He would resonate with the depth, Yeshua would ask clearly. Does the ritual lead you toward the heart or replace it?

"This people honors Me with their lips, but their heart is far from Me" (Matthew 15:8). When the gesture remains without life, the form risks taking the place of the encounter. Does tradition preserve life or freeze it? "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27). Where form becomes the goal, life is sacrificed in its name. Yeshua would love the liturgical silence, but He would refuse formalism. He would honor the symbol that opens, not the one that closes. He would probably say the same truth He spoke to the Samaritan woman: "The hour is coming when you will worship the Father neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem… the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth" (John 4:21–24). Yeshua would not abolish the Liturgy, nor would He minimize its sacredness. But He would ask that it go out into life, that it continue in compassion, in forgiveness, in presence.

For Him, the true liturgy does not end with "Amen," but begins where a person leaves healed and more alive than when he entered.

## How Yeshua would speak with famous evangelical leaders

With charismatic evangelical leaders, Yeshua would recognize and appreciate the authentic desire for a direct relationship with God, the accessibility of Scripture, and the vitality of a living community, there where "two or three are gathered" in His name (Matthew 18:20). But beyond this openness, He would ask questions that cannot be avoided: whether the Gospel is preached as a path of transformation or reduced to a simple verbal decision — because "not everyone who says to Me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the Kingdom" (Matthew 7:21); whether the message and the structure of the community give birth to inner freedom or, on the contrary, create emotional dependence on the leader, on the atmosphere, or on collective validation, in contrast to His call to rest and to real freedom (Matthew 11:28–30).

Yeshua would warn firmly against turning faith into a religious spectacle: "When you pray, go into your room" (Matthew 6:6). He would not reject enthusiasm and the living expression of faith, but He would draw a clear distinction between the inner fire that comes from truth lived and the group emotional pressure that can mimic presence without giving birth to it.

## Would Yeshua go to church often? Would He preach in church?

Yeshua would enter churches, but He would not settle there. He entered synagogues (Luke 4:16), He preached in temples (John 7), but the greater part of His message was spoken outside them: on mountains, on the road, at the table, in homes. He would preach where there is openness, not necessarily where there is a program. When He sensed that the religious space was blocking life, He would do what He always did: He would go out. "Foxes have holes… but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay His head" (Matthew 8:20).

## Would Yeshua observe the Sabbath?

Yeshua would observe the meaning of the Sabbath, not its letter. "The Sabbath was made for man" (Mark 2:27). He would heal, He would comfort, He would loosen burdens. He would refuse a Sabbath that forbids the good. If the modern "Sabbath" is Sunday, Yeshua would ask: does it rest the person, or only the program? Where the Sabbath no longer brings life, Yeshua reorients it. Repeatedly, Yeshua heals on the Sabbath day (Mark 3:1–6; Luke 13:10–17).

Each time, His justification is the same: the good cannot be postponed. "Is it lawful to do good on the Sabbath?" (Mark 3:4). The implicit answer is yes — because the Sabbath exists for man, not man for the Sabbath (Mark 2:27). If Sunday is the Christian "Sabbath," then Yeshua's logic would favor healing, not the liturgy. Where there is suffering, there is urgency. To postpone life for the sake of the program would be, in His terms, a reversal of meaning.

## If Yeshua were not to go to church: where life moves

If Yeshua were not to go to church, He would do nothing "new." He would do exactly what He did back then, because His perspective has never changed. He would stay with the excluded, there where shame has closed doors and where religion has raised walls. "It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick" (Luke 5:29–32). He would heal, not as a demonstration of power, but as a restoration of life: "the blind see, the lame walk" (Matthew 11:5).

He would eat with people, because the table is the place of real encounter, not of moral evaluation: "He entered the house of a Pharisee to eat" (Luke 7:36). Yeshua would walk along the road, because the road is the space where life moves and where hearts open. "He walked with them along the way" (Luke 24:15). He would listen, because truth is not imposed but is born of attention. "What do you want Me to do for you?" (Mark 10:46–52). In all of this, Yeshua would transform "religious" time into time for life. For Him, the Kingdom is not tied to a place or an hour, to a building or a program. "The hour is coming when you will worship the Father neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem" (John 4:21).

## How Yeshua would relate to worship songs

Yeshua would not reject singing. He Himself sang psalms (Matthew 26:30). But He would be attentive to intention and to effect. He would ask whether the song opens or merely excites, whether it draws closer to truth or maintains an emotional state that does not transform. His criterion remains the same: "in spirit and truth" (John 4:23). Singing that gives birth to presence, humility, and love would be received without reservation. Singing that produces self-validation, emotion without change, or collective pressure would be relativized.

For Yeshua, the sound is not the problem, but the direction of the heart. Worship is not evaluated by intensity, but by fruit. Yeshua would not take the side of any denomination. He would take the side of life. He would not fight with religious leaders, but He would call each one to truth. He would not flee from churches, but He would not remain where life is replaced by form. And He would probably say the same thing as back then: "The Kingdom of God is near."

## Where Yeshua would be on a Sunday morning

The question of whether Yeshua would choose, on a Sunday morning, to preach from a church or to be in a park, healing, listening, or doing something seemingly "non-religious," is not a speculative one. The Gospels offer a coherent pattern of His priorities. When this pattern is followed carefully, the answer becomes surprisingly clear. Yeshua enters the synagogue "as was His custom" (Luke 4:16), but He does not center His mission on the religious space.

He does not turn the place of assembly into the center of His work. He spends the greater part of His time on the roads, in motion, in unscheduled encounters: "as they were walking along the way" (Luke 24:15), on the mountain where He delivers the Sermon (Matthew 5:1), on the seashore where He teaches the crowds (Mark 4:1), in homes where He stops unexpectedly ("Zacchaeus, hurry and come down, for today I must stay at your house," Luke 19:5), and at tables with people considered religiously unsuitable ("He sat at table with the tax collectors and sinners," Luke 5:29–32).

His preaching appears where the person is, not where the program is. In Yeshua's logic, the place does not sacralize the encounter; the encounter sacralizes the place. When He is asked about the right place of worship, His answer shifts the emphasis from geography to the interior: "The hour is coming when you will worship neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem… the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and in truth" (John 4:21–23).

This statement relativizes any religious space as being absolutely necessary. God is no more present in a building than in a park, on a road, or beside a person who is suffering. Yeshua constantly refuses to let the religious program take priority over life. "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27) shows clearly that the religious order cannot become more important than concrete human need.

He heals on the Sabbath (Mark 3:1–6), not in order to provoke, but because life cannot wait for the program's permission. By the same logic, if on a "Sunday morning" there were a wounded person, a person searching, a person weeping, Yeshua would be there. Not because He would despise the assembly, but because He would not sacrifice life on the altar of form.

The pattern of the Gospels shows a Yeshua constantly drawn to the spaces where life unfolds freely, unfiltered by ritual: fields, roads, homes, tables, shores of waters. There the most important conversations are born, the deepest healings, and the most living revelations. "He taught the people on the seashore" (Mark 4:1) and "He went through the towns and villages, proclaiming and announcing the Kingdom of God" (Luke 8:1) show a God in motion, not one fixed within a program.

If there were a Sunday in the modern sense, the Gospel pattern suggests that Yeshua would be drawn rather to the non-institutionalized space, where the encounter is not scheduled, where people do not come "religiously prepared," but come as they are. Because the Kingdom is not announced by a schedule, but recognized through presence. "The Kingdom of God is not coming in ways that can be observed" (Luke 17:20).

Yeshua is not against the assembly, but He is constantly against the idea that God is more accessible in one setting than in another. He shifts the center of gravity from place to relationship, from program to encounter, from form to life. That is why the answer is not that He would choose "the park" instead of "the church," but that He would choose the place where life is more alive, where the person is more present, where the need is more real.

Most likely, on such a morning, Yeshua would be there where someone needs Him: on a bench, on a road, at a table, beside someone searching for meaning or weeping in silence. Because, in His pattern, the Kingdom does not come through an announcement, but through presence.

## If Yeshua appeared today in the churches — welcomed as Messiah or rejected as a heretic?

Imagining this scenario is not a provocative game, but a test of spiritual honesty. If Yeshua Christ were to appear today without saying who He is, without demonstrative miracles, without institutional authority — only with His teachings, with His way of seeing the person, sin, truth, and love — how would He be received? Not by the world, but by the Christian churches. The answer is not comfortable.

Yeshua, as He appears in the Gospels, is not a defender of religious institutions, but a disturber of them. He does not begin by affirming dogmas, but by relativizing them in favor of life. He says things such as: "The Sabbath is for man," "You have heard that it was said… but I say to you," "I desire mercy, not sacrifice." These statements are not theological errors, but they are structural threats to any system that draws its authority from rules, boundaries, and moral control.

If Yeshua were to speak today in the churches about judgment as blindness, about religious hypocrisy as a major sin, about empathy before correction, about the fact that "the tax collectors and prostitutes enter the Kingdom ahead of you," He would quickly be regarded as a dangerous preacher. Not because He would deny Scripture, but because He would refuse to use it as a weapon.

## How Yeshua treats those who think differently from Him

One of the clearest indications of the profoundly different nature of Yeshua's message compared with the Christianity that would be built after Him is the way He relates to those who think differently. Not to those who are "sinful" in a moral sense, but to those who do not understand, do not agree, misinterpret Him, or reject Him.

Here one sees not merely a difference of strategy, but a difference of spiritual ontology: Yeshua does not defend a doctrine; Paul and institutional Christianity will come to defend a formulated truth. In the Gospels, Yeshua lives constantly in the midst of misunderstanding. The disciples do not grasp the parables, they misinterpret His mission, they argue about power, they are afraid, they flee, they deny Him. And yet, Yeshua does not exclude them, does not threaten them, and does not demand intellectual alignment.

"To you it has been given to know the secrets of the Kingdom, but to them it has not been given" (Matthew 13:11) is not a condemnation of those outside, but an observation about the openness of the heart. The difference is not between "correct" and "wrong," but between open and closed. Yeshua is in no hurry to correct different thinking. Many times, He lets it run its course on its own. When He is rejected in a Samaritan village, He refuses the punishment proposed by the disciples: "You do not know what spirit you are of" (Luke 9:55).

The refusal is not interpreted as an offense against truth, but as a limit of receptivity. Yeshua does not feel threatened by disagreement. For Him, truth does not need to be defended by force. Even in the tense dialogues with the Pharisees, Yeshua does not exclude them because they think differently, but because they use thinking as an instrument of superiority. His critique is not aimed at wrong interpretation, but at the rigidity that kills life (Matthew 23).

He does not say: "You think wrongly and you are lost," but: "You shut the Kingdom in people's faces" (Matthew 23:13). The problem is not the difference of opinion, but the refusal to see. Before those who do not agree with Him, Yeshua often responds with silence. Before Herod, He does not defend Himself (Luke 23:9). Before Pilate, He does not enter into a theological debate (John 18:36–38). The silence is not weakness, but a sign that truth is not a thesis to be demonstrated.

Yeshua does not try to win arguments; He remains in the truth. This attitude is possible because Yeshua does not tie truth to its acceptance. He says explicitly: "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Mark 4:9). Truth is not imposed; it is recognized. That is why Yeshua can sit at table with those who do not share His vision, without conditioning or correcting them beforehand (Mark 2:15–17). Difference is not a problem; the fear of difference is the problem.

With Paul, the meaning shifts visibly. Paul does not operate in a context of open encounter, but in a context of community building. The community must be protected, delimited, kept coherent. Thus, different thinking begins to be treated not as a stage of the journey, but as a danger. "If anyone preaches to you another gospel… let him be accursed" (Galatians 1:8). A difference of interpretation becomes a threat to salvation.

With Paul there appears for the first time the idea of "sound teaching" versus "wrong teaching" (Titus 1:9; 2 Timothy 4:3). Truth begins to be formulated, fixed, defended. From here, exclusion inevitably appears as well: "keep away from them" (Romans 16:17). Paul does not invite dialogue with those who think differently, but separation, in order to protect the community from contamination. This shift would be amplified by the Christianity that came after Yeshua and continues to this day. Difference of thought becomes heresy.

The question becomes dangerous. Doubt becomes a lack of faith. Institutional Christianity would develop clear mechanisms of delimitation: dogmas, creeds, anathemas, excommunications. Truth is no longer something that sets free, but something that must be guarded. Thus, while Yeshua can say, "Whoever is not against us is for us" (Mark 9:40), Christianity often comes to operate on the reverse logic: whoever is not with us is against us.

Difference of thought is no longer a space of growth, but a test of loyalty. Truth is no longer lived, but declared. Yeshua treats difference as a limit of sight, not as a fault. Paul and institutional Christianity treat difference as a threat to the formulated truth. Yeshua is not afraid that truth might be lost if someone thinks differently. Christianity is afraid that, without control, truth becomes diluted.

In the end, Yeshua never demands uniformity of thought. He demands presence. He demands sincerity. He demands openness. He does not say, "Think like Me," but "Come and see" (John 1:39). Christianity will often say: "Believe correctly and you will be accepted." This is perhaps the clearest rupture: Yeshua created space for difference. Christianity created borders around the truth.

## Sharp with those who possess the truth, not with those who seek it

For Yeshua, difference of thought is not a danger to be eliminated, but a space of encounter. He does not enter into disputes in order to win ideas, but into relationships in order to awaken consciences. That is why His way of responding does not depend on the doctrinal correctness of the other, but on the state of heart with which that person comes. With those who are different and open — Samaritans, Romans, sinners, women religiously excluded — Yeshua is present, curious, and non-defensive.

In the parable of the Samaritan, it is precisely the one considered a heretic who becomes the model of humanity: "A Samaritan… had compassion on him" (Luke 10:33). Before the Roman centurion, Yeshua is not scandalized by his belonging, but recognizes his authenticity: "Not even in Israel have I found such faith" (Matthew 8:10). In the dialogue with the Samaritan woman, He does not begin by correcting her theology, but by opening a living relationship: "If you knew the gift of God…" (John 4:10).

The relationship comes first; truth is born from within the relationship, not from coercion. By contrast, with those who believe they already possess the truth and use it as a weapon, Yeshua becomes unusually firm. Not because they think differently, but because they use truth to close rather than to open. "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!" (Matthew 23:27) is not a condemnation of their doctrine, but of their lack of heart.

When He says, "You are wrong, because you know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God" (Matthew 22:29), He does not reproach them for intellectual ignorance, but for the fact that they have turned Scripture into a system that excludes love. The difference is not the problem; the rigidity is. In the way He handles conflict of ideas, Yeshua does not impose truth through external authority. He asks questions that compel reflection: "What is written in the Law? How do you read it?" (Luke 10:26).

He tells parables that bypass confrontation and work from within: "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Matthew 13:9). When He is rejected, He does not coerce and does not insist: "If they will not receive you… shake the dust from your feet" (Matthew 10:14). For Yeshua, truth is not defended; it is offered. He accepts that some are not ready — and He does not force their ripening. With Paul, the approach changes visibly, not because love is absent, but because the context is different.

Paul is a missionary, an organizer, a theologian; he works in young communities that need coherence and stability. He has a real openness toward difference: "I have become all things to all people, that I might save some" (1 Corinthians 9:22) and "Welcome one another" (Romans 15:7).

But when difference becomes a threat to the message and to cohesion, Paul is corrective and firm: "If anyone preaches another gospel… let him be accursed" (Galatians 1:8) and "Remove the evil person from among you" (1 Corinthians 5:13). He treats difference functionally, asking what impact it has on the community, not only on the individual. The fundamental difference is clear.

Yeshua works with the individual and with the heart: truth is lived, not imposed, and difference is tolerated as long as life remains alive. Paul works with the system: truth must be protected, and difference becomes dangerous if it fragments. Yeshua awakens; Paul organizes. Today's institutional Christianity often operates from a combination of a hardened Pauline theology, identity-based fear, and doctrinal control. The result is the rapid labeling — "heretic," "lost" — the exclusion and the moralizing without relationship. Thus, without intention, the words Yeshua spoke about the religious leaders of His time are fulfilled: "They tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear, but they themselves are not willing to lift a finger to move them" (Matthew 23:4). The truth about Yeshua is defended, but the way of Yeshua is lost. There are, however, things that Yeshua never does.

He does not cancel anyone's dignity because they err. He does not force the acceptance of truth. He does not confuse intellectual opposition with moral malice. He affirms clearly: "I did not come to judge the world, but to save the world" (John 12:47). This is not a weakness, but a strength that comes from the security of truth lived. Thus there remain three distinct ways of treating difference. Yeshua remains in relationship even amid disagreement, because truth flows from presence.

Paul corrects in order to protect the message and the community. Today's Christianity often excludes in order to protect its identity. The question that remains, inspired by Yeshua's rebuke to the disciples, is an essential one: "What spirit are you speaking from?" (cf. Luke 9:55). Because it is not which truth you uphold that defines you most deeply, but how you treat those who do not share it. For Yeshua, "evil" is neither a list of deeds nor a label placed on people.

Evil is a state of the heart that produces blindness, separation, and a lack of love. He shifts the emphasis from the outside to the inside when He says: "The good person brings good things out of the good treasure of his heart, and the evil person brings evil things out of the evil treasure of his heart" (Luke 6:45). In the same direction, He explains clearly: "Out of the heart come evil thoughts" (Matthew 15:19).

Evil is not an identity, but a disconnection; it is not a definitive label, but a state born of a heart torn from truth and from love. Yeshua's harshest words are not addressed to those considered "sinners" by society, but to those convinced that they see clearly and judge in the name of this conviction. "If you were blind, you would have no sin; but now that you say, 'We see,' your sin remains" (John 9:41).

In another place, He calls them without hesitation "blind guides" (Matthew 15:14). Supreme evil thus becomes the moral arrogance that justifies the lack of love. When a person believes himself to be light, he can no longer receive the light. Blindness that believes itself to be sight is, for Yeshua, one of the deepest forms of evil. Yeshua names evil also where religion becomes an instrument of oppression, shaming, and control.

He says of the religious leaders of His time: "You shut the Kingdom of Heaven in people's faces" (Matthew 23:13). And to this attitude He opposes a simple and decisive principle: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13). Evil is not the law itself, but the use of the law against life. When the rule replaces compassion, when form replaces the heart, evil takes hold under the appearance of righteousness. Equally important is what Yeshua does not call evil.

He does not call evil those who are different, those who are socially stigmatized, those who are searching. He says clearly: "I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners" (Luke 5:32). Before the woman caught in adultery, He shifts the attention from guilt to conscience: "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone" (John 8:7). For Yeshua, evil is not the fall, but the refusal to see and to love. The central problem is not the error, but the closing toward truth.

For the Apostle Paul, evil is described rather as an active force, an inner principle in conflict with a person's desire to do good. "I find it to be a law that when I want to do good, evil lies close at hand" (Romans 7:21). Paul describes this tension with honesty: "I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want" (Romans 7:19).

The solution, in his vision, is radical: "Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh" (Galatians 5:24). The key difference from Yeshua is that Paul speaks of evil as a power that demands discipline, struggle, and constant grace, while Yeshua speaks of evil as the absence of love lived, a state that dissolves through seeing and unity. In contemporary Christianity, evil is often externalized and simplified.

It becomes different behavior, a "wrong" opinion, "worldly" belonging. The label replaces discernment, and fear is justified religiously. Exclusion comes to seem a virtue. It is precisely here that Yeshua warns: "Why do you see the speck in your brother's eye and not the log in your own eye?" (Matthew 7:3). Moral projection — the tendency to see evil only in others — is, in His logic, the sign that evil is already at work within. Seen together, three ways of defining evil appear.

For Yeshua, evil is the closed heart that believes itself to be light. For Paul, evil is the flesh in permanent conflict with grace. For today's Christianity, evil often comes to be the other person. But Yeshua shifts the question once more toward the interior: from what source does your life flow? His criterion remains the same: "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:16). Where the fruits are fear, rigidity, and a lack of compassion, evil is already present, even if the language is religious. Where the fruits are life, freedom, and love, evil loses its power, even if the forms are not the expected ones.

## How Yeshua treated other religions

The way a tradition relates to "others" says more about its nature than any doctrinal declaration. In this sense, the difference between Yeshua and the Christianity that would later be built is one of the most visible and least discussed ruptures. Yeshua never built a theology of religious exclusivity. Paul would take the first steps toward doctrinal delimitation. Today's institutional Christianity would turn delimitation into a system.

An essential fact is this: Yeshua nowhere speaks of "false religions." He does not attack other religious traditions as competing systems and does not define His message through doctrinal opposition. His critique is not directed at "others," but at His own tradition when it becomes rigid, moralistic, and lifeless (Matthew 23).

Yeshua does not position Himself as the founder of a new religion that must replace the old one; He moves within Judaism, but lives it from a different perspective. His attitude toward people of other religious traditions is consistently relational, not polemical. The Samaritans, considered heretical and impure from a religious standpoint, are not treated as doctrinally lost, but as people capable of truth and compassion.

The parable of the Good Samaritan is scandalously clear: true nearness to God is shown by someone from a religious tradition other than the "correct" one (Luke 10:33–37). Yeshua does not relativize truth; He relativizes belonging. In the dialogue with the Samaritan woman, Yeshua acknowledges the real religious difference — "you worship what you do not know" — but immediately shifts the discussion: "the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and in truth" (John 4:22–23).

This is not a condemnation of another religion, but a transcending of religious boundaries as such. Worship is no longer tied to the mountain, the temple, or tradition, but to a quality of consciousness. Yeshua explicitly praises the faith of people from outside His own people and religion. The Roman centurion — a representative of a pagan power — is praised for his faith more than anyone in Israel: "Not even in Israel have I found such great faith" (Matthew 8:10).

Yeshua does not demand religious conversion, does not impose belonging, does not require the abandonment of one's tradition. He recognizes truth wherever it is alive. This freedom is possible because Yeshua does not identify truth with a religious system. For Him, the Kingdom of God does not belong to a tradition: "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). That is why Yeshua does not build a religious "we" in opposition to a "they." He builds a way of seeing that makes the boundaries secondary.

With Paul, the situation changes. Paul operates in a missionary and apologetic context. The message about Christ must be proclaimed in a religiously plural world: Judaism, Greco-Roman cults, philosophy. In this context, Paul begins to draw boundaries. He speaks of "false gods" (1 Corinthians 8:5–6) and of "idolatry" as spiritual error (Romans 1:21–25). Other religions are no longer possible spaces of truth, but expressions of ignorance or of error.

The discourse in Athens is emblematic. Paul acknowledges that the Athenians are "very religious" and begins from the altar to "the unknown God" (Acts 17:22–23). But this openness is strategic, not relational. The goal is not the recognition of a truth already present, but its correction through the proclamation of Christ as the only valid framework.

Truth begins to be formulated exclusively: "God now commands all people everywhere to repent" (Acts 17:30). From here onward, religious pluralism is no longer a context of encounter, but a field of mission. Other religions are no longer seen as partial expressions of the human search, but as wrong alternatives that must be abandoned. This shift would be taken up and amplified by institutional Christianity.

The Christianity that came after Yeshua and continues to this day would build a theology of exclusivity: one single way, one single formulated truth, one single valid salvation. "There is salvation in no one else" (Acts 4:12) becomes the foundation of a competitive relationship with other religions. Throughout history, this exclusivity would justify not only mission, but also condemnation, marginalization, and, at times, symbolic or real violence.

In contemporary Christianity, other religions are often treated either as "deceptions" or as "sincere but incomplete searches" that must be completed through conversion. Interreligious dialogue is tolerated, but rarely lived in the logic of Yeshua. The truth is already decided; the encounter is no longer open.

While Yeshua meets people of other religions without asking them to renounce their identity in order to be seen, Paul begins the process by which truth is tied to a specific proclamation. And institutional Christianity completes the process by turning truth into a criterion of belonging. Yeshua did not treat other religions as errors to be corrected, but as spaces in which a person may or may not see.

Paul treated other religions as incomplete frameworks that must be transcended. Today's Christianity often treats them as spiritual rivals. Yeshua did not come to replace religions. He came to show what remains true when religions can no longer sustain life.

## Why Yeshua is not against religions

A question that inevitably arises when we read the Gospels without the filter of later Christianity is this: is Yeshua against other religions or not? The answer is not simple if we use the modern categories of "true religions" and "false religions." Yeshua does not operate with this map. He does not enter into a religious competition and does not define His message through doctrinal opposition. At the same time, Yeshua speaks clearly and radically about idolatry. Only that idolatry, for Him, is not what it would later become in Christian theology.

An essential starting point is this: Yeshua never uses the language of "other religions" as a theological problem. He does not say that the Samaritans have a wrong religion, that the Romans are lost because they worship other gods, or that the nations must first abandon their traditions in order to be seen by God. His critique is not directed outward, but inward, into His own tradition, there where religion becomes rigid, moralistic, and separated from life (Matthew 23). This positioning is evident in the way Yeshua interacts with people outside Judaism. The Roman centurion, a representative of a pagan power, is not corrected doctrinally, but praised for his faith: "Not even in Israel have I found such great faith" (Matthew 8:10).

Yeshua does not ask him to renounce his religious or cultural identity. He recognizes truth wherever it is alive. Likewise, the dialogue with the Samaritan woman shows clearly that Yeshua acknowledges real religious differences, but refuses to turn them into ultimate criteria. "You worship what you do not know" (John 4:22) is not a condemnation, but a contextual observation.

Immediately, however, Yeshua shifts the meaning: "the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and in truth" (John 4:23). Worship is no longer tied to the mountain, the temple, or tradition, but to a state of consciousness. Thus, Yeshua does not attack the Samaritan religion; He relativizes religion as such. The parable of the Good Samaritan goes even further. Here, the exemplary figure of nearness to God is not the priest or the Levite — representatives of the "correct" religion — but the Samaritan, considered a heretic (Luke 10:33–37). Yeshua does not say that the Samaritan is saved because he belongs to another religion, but shows that truth is recognized through love, not through belonging. This is a profoundly non-exclusivist gesture, yet not a relativist one.

At this point the major confusion appears: if Yeshua is not against other religions, what about idolatry? The answer is decisive. Yeshua speaks about idols, but radically redefines the meaning of idolatry. For Him, the idol is not primarily a foreign god or a statue. The idol is anything that takes the place of God as the source of security, identity, and meaning.

The statement "you cannot serve two masters… you cannot serve God and Mammon" (Matthew 6:24) is central. Yeshua does not oppose to God a pagan deity, but money, material security, control. Idolatry is the displacement of the inner center. It is not about what name you bear, but about what you live from. The same logic appears in the temptations in the wilderness. Yeshua refuses not only political power, but also the temptation to instrumentalize God for security and spectacle (Matthew 4:1–10). Idolatry is not only the worship of another god, but the use of God Himself as an instrument of fear and control. That is why Yeshua is far harder on the religion that uses God than on the religions that seek God imperfectly.

In the Gospels, the principal idols are not "the gods of the nations," but: money, power, the absolutized Law, religious status, self-image, fear. "This people honors Me with their lips, but their heart is far from Me" (Matthew 15:8). Here, idolatry is internal, not external. It can exist even in the name of God. That is why Yeshua does not attack other religions as systems. He attacks any form of religious or non-religious life that shifts the center from trust to fear. For Him, the problem is not religious pluralism, but wrong worship. This can appear in Judaism, in paganism, in what would become Christianity, or outside any religion. This vision would change significantly with Paul and with later Christianity, which would identify idolatry primarily with other religions and with "false gods" (Romans 1:21–25), turning pluralism into a doctrinal problem.

Yeshua, however, does not start from a map of religions, but from the human heart. The conclusion is clear: Yeshua is not against other religions. He is against any worship that moves a person from truth into fear. He did not come to create a competing religion, but to reveal what remains true when all religions are set in parentheses.

## How Yeshua would see Muslims

If we look at the way Yeshua evaluates people in the Gospels, we observe a constant pattern: He never begins with theological labels, but with the state of the heart. Yeshua does not use the term "heretic" to exclude; He speaks of blindness, fear, formalism, or hypocrisy as inner states, not as religious belonging. "It is not what enters the mouth that defiles a person, but what comes out of the mouth" (Matthew 15:11) immediately shifts the emphasis from doctrine to the heart.

And on the cross, His prayer — "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34) — shows that ignorance is not treated as absolute guilt, but as a need for light. Yeshua rebukes most harshly the religious people who are convinced that they are already "in the truth." From this perspective, Muslims — who respect Yeshua, who pray, fast, seek submission to God, and practice mercy — would not be treated as "outside" the truth, but as people on the way, not yet at the destination. For Yeshua, the decisive question is not "do you have the correct religion?" but "are you alive in the heart?"

Islam recognizes Yeshua as a prophet, born of a virgin and a worker of miracles, but rejects Yeshua's central claim about Himself: "I am the Way, the Truth, and the Life" (John 14:6). Yeshua would regard this position as an incomplete recognition — truth without existential consequence. "You call Me Teacher and Lord, and you are right, for so I am" (John 13:13) ties the title to the following of His life, not merely to verbal respect. That is why Yeshua would not force belief, but would ask the same question He asked the disciples: "Who do people say that I am?" and, more personally, "But you, who do you say that I am?" (Matthew 16:13–15). To call Him a prophet without following His life and taking up the cross is a form of respect that avoids transformation.

Islam denies the crucifixion of Yeshua, holding that God would not have permitted such a humiliation. For Yeshua, however, the cross is not an accident, but the core of His mission: "The Son of Man must suffer many things… and be killed" (Mark 8:31). "For this purpose I have come" (John 12:27) shows that the embrace of suffering is part of the revelation of love. Yeshua would not engage in historical polemic, but would point to the meaning: the refusal of the cross is the refusal of vulnerability and of the love that gives itself to the very end. His call remains the same for all: "Whoever wants to come after Me must deny himself, take up his cross, and follow Me" (Mark 8:34). Honoring without following to the end remains, for Yeshua, incomplete. "Allah" literally means "God." Yeshua would not be scandalized by the language, but by the inner image of God.

"God is Spirit, and those who worship Him must worship in spirit and in truth" (John 4:24) shifts the discussion from the name to the face. The tension arises between a God of submission through fear and a God-Father who calls to love and sonship: "Whoever has seen Me has seen the Father" (John 14:9). Yeshua would say that Muslims pray to God, but do not yet know Him in the revealed face of radical love. His call would not be to a change of name, but to a change of relationship.

## Closer to the truth than some Christians?

Yeshua's answer would be surprisingly direct: sometimes, yes. He said clearly that mercy and sincerity can precede doctrinal correctness: "The tax collectors and the prostitutes go into the Kingdom of God ahead of you" (Matthew 21:31). And He warned: "Not everyone who says to Me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the Kingdom" (Matthew 7:21). A sincere Muslim who prays, fasts, helps the poor, and lives with the fear of God may be closer to the Kingdom than a Christian who proclaims the dogma but lives in hatred and contempt. For Yeshua, the fruit of one's life weighs more than the label.

## About Muhammad

Yeshua would not speak of Muhammad as a rival, but as a man who sought to orient people toward God in a violent and divided context. Nevertheless, He would make a clear distinction of role: "I am the Good Shepherd" (John 10:11). Yeshua would not validate a revelation that reduces Him to an inferior status, annuls the cross, or shifts the relationship with God from sonship into legal submission. He would not condemn the person, but He would not confirm the message as being complete.

Yeshua would not begin with "You are wrong," but with "Come and see" (John 1:39). In His eyes, Muslims are not enemies, are not rejected, and are not "outside." They are invited further — not to a new religion, but to a Person. "I came that the sheep may have life, and have it abundantly" (John 10:10). Yeshua would not ask them to deny God; He would ask them to know Him.

## What Yeshua would say to atheists

Yeshua would not begin by proving the existence of God. There is no sermon of His in the Gospels constructed as a philosophical argument about the existence of the divine. He does not seek to win debates, but to create encounters. Before atheists, Yeshua would not say "you are wrong," but would look at what they live, what they seek, and what causes them pain. Yeshua does not divide people into "believers" and "unbelievers" as definitive labels. He divides between those who see and those who do not yet see.

"I have not come to call the righteous to repentance, but sinners" (Luke 5:32). Here "sinner" does not mean atheist, but a person torn from life, from meaning, from inner truth. In His dialogues, Yeshua does not force conclusions. With Nicodemus He speaks at night, without pressure. With the Samaritan woman He begins from water, not from theology. With the rich young man He leaves freedom intact, even though the man walks away. Yeshua accepts refusal without turning it into a threat.

"Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Matthew 11:15). Of atheists, Yeshua would not ask that they believe in Him, but would invite them to observe life. "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:16). He would say: look at what love produces, what fear produces, what the ego produces. If truth is alive, it will recognize itself. Yeshua does not seek assent, but resonance. Most likely, Yeshua would sit at table with atheists without opening the subject of faith.

He would speak about life, about loss, about fear, about meaning. He would love without conditions. For Him, love is already a revelation of God, even if a person does not name it as such. "By this everyone will know that you are My disciples: if you have love for one another" (John 13:35). Paul, by contrast, would try to convince. Not necessarily aggressively, but systematically. In the Acts of the Apostles, Paul debates with the philosophers in Athens, argues, quotes Greek poets, and builds an intellectual bridge: "What you worship without knowing, this I proclaim to you" (Acts 17:23). For Paul, atheism is a problem of wrong or incomplete knowledge. He believes that truth must be explained, preached, articulated doctrinally. "Faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ" (Romans 10:17).

Here appears the idea that the correctly transmitted message will produce faith. Paul is willing to debate, to defend, to argue. He sees conversion as an explicit goal. Not in order to control, but in order to save. But this approach shifts the center from experience to explanation, from living to intellectual conviction. Today's Christianity, in many of its forms, has lost both Yeshua's delicacy and Paul's depth. Instead of an encounter, it offers labels.

Instead of dialogue, it offers conclusions. The atheist is often seen as "blind," "rebellious," or "threatened by hell." John 3:16 is used as an instrument of pressure rather than as a revelation of love. The message becomes: "Believe or you will perish." Thus, God is presented as a condition rather than as a presence. Instead of manifesting the fruits of the Spirit, Christianity comes to defend ideas about God, sometimes with emotional aggression. Rarely does contemporary Christianity sit with atheists without the aim of conversion.

Rarely does it listen. Rarely does it accept that an atheist can live love, compassion, and truth without religious language. And yet, Yeshua says clearly: "Whoever does the will of My Father… is My brother" (Matthew 12:50). He does not say: whoever believes correctly.

## What Yeshua would say about Buddhism and the Buddha

Yeshua would not begin by delimiting doctrinal territories. He would not first ask what you believe, but how you live. In Buddhism, Yeshua would immediately recognize an authentic search for liberation from suffering, a discipline of consciousness, and a profound attention to the nature of the mind. "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven" (Matthew 5:3) would resonate naturally with the practice of emptying oneself of attachments.

Yeshua would see in the Buddha a man who saw suffering, refused the superficial, and sought the truth to the very end. He would recognize the discernment of desire as the source of suffering and would say, in His own language: "Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21). Attachment, for Yeshua, as for the Buddha, binds the heart and darkens sight. The difference would appear not as opposition, but as emphasis.

Yeshua proposes not only the exit from suffering through detachment, but the entry into life through love. "Abide in Me, and I in you" (John 15:4). Where Buddhism speaks of non-attachment and non-self, Yeshua speaks of relationship, communion, and Father. He does not dissolve the person, but transfigures it through love. Yeshua would not say that the Buddha erred. He would say, rather, that he saw a part of the truth. "Whoever is not against us is for us" (Mark 9:40).

He would recognize the stillness, the compassion, and the lucidity as real fruits. But He would add that the ultimate truth is not merely a state of consciousness, but a living relationship: "This is eternal life: that they may know You" (John 17:3). Paul would have a far more cautious and exclusivist approach. For him, any path that does not pass explicitly through Christ is incomplete or wrong. "Even if we ourselves or an angel from heaven should preach another gospel, let him be accursed" (Galatians 1:8).

Paul might appreciate the moral discipline and the ethic of compassion, but he would reject the absence of a personal God. For Paul, the central problem of the human being is not ignorance, but sin. He would not see enlightenment as a sufficient solution, because it does not resolve the broken relationship with God. "The wages of sin is death" (Romans 6:23). Within this framework, the Buddha would be perceived as a moral teacher or philosopher, not as a bearer of salvation.

Paul would say that Buddhism can calm the mind, but cannot save the soul. He might recognize the "zeal," but "without full knowledge" (Romans 10:2). His emphasis would be on the uniqueness of Christ as mediator: "There is one God and one mediator between God and men, the man Yeshua Christ" (1 Timothy 2:5). Today's Christianity, in many of its expressions, has gone even further than Paul.

Most often, Buddhism is quickly labeled as a "false religion," a "deception," or a "demonic work," without any real effort at understanding. The Buddha is reduced to the status of an idol, and the practice of meditation is regarded with suspicion. In this discourse, no distinction is made between experience and doctrine. Everything that is not explicitly Christian is considered dangerous. It is forgotten, however, that Yeshua says: "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:16).

The real fruits of many Buddhist practitioners — peace, compassion, non-violence — are often ignored because they do not fit the dogma. Contemporary Christianity rarely speaks with Buddhists; it speaks about them. And that says everything. It rarely recognizes partial truth as truth. It rarely accepts that God can work even beyond the boundaries of Christian language. And yet, Yeshua says: "I have other sheep that are not of this fold" (John 10:16).

## How Yeshua would relate to the Jews of today

Yeshua cannot be separated from His Jewish identity. He lives within Judaism, speaks the language of the Hebrew Scripture, and breathes the same spiritual world. We find Him in the synagogue "as was His custom" (Luke 4:16), going up to Jerusalem for the feasts (John 7; John 10:22), and relating constantly to the Law and the Prophets. His statement is clear: "I have not come to abolish the Law or the Prophets, but to fulfill them" (Matthew 5:17).

But this "fulfillment" does not mean rigidity, but the restoration of the Law to its living core. When He is asked which is the great commandment, Yeshua concentrates the whole of Scripture into love: "You shall love the Lord your God… and your neighbor as yourself" (Matthew 22:37–40), taking up exactly the heart of the Torah from Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18. In the same prophetic spirit, He reaffirms: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13). Yeshua does not abandon the tradition, but deepens it. He remains in continuity with the prophets, who always said that the purpose of the Law is life, not control.

## Would Yeshua be an "Orthodox Jew"?

If by "Orthodox" one means the absolutizing of the norm as a marker of identity, delimitation through rules, and the defense of form as an end in itself, the Gospel pattern clearly suggests that Yeshua would not align Himself with this model. He heals on the Sabbath (Mark 3:1–6), relativizes ritual purity when it becomes an obstacle to truth (Mark 7:14–23), and formulates the principle that sums up His entire position: "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27).

And yet, if "Orthodox" means a profound anchoring in Scripture, prayer, ethics, and the living memory of faith, then Yeshua is profoundly within this tradition. The difference is not one of belonging, but of perspective: for Yeshua, tradition is the means by which life is preserved, not the criterion by which a person is measured.

## Would Yeshua be a Zionist?

Zionism is a modern political project, born in a historical context entirely different from that of Yeshua. The Gospels, however, show clearly that Yeshua refuses any form of political messianism. When the people "wanted to make Him king," He withdraws (John 6:15). Before Pilate, He says without equivocation: "My Kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36).

Yeshua deeply loves His people — He weeps over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41) — but He does not sacralize the land, the border, or power. His fidelity is to justice and life, not to an ideological project. He would reject equally antisemitism and the confusion of God with the state, with political force, or with borders.

## How Yeshua would speak with the Jews of today

Yeshua would not speak as an adversary, nor as an outsider. He would speak from within, using the shared Scripture and the prophetic language. He would recognize the continuity: "salvation is from the Jews" (John 4:22). His tone would be both familiar and uncomfortable — honoring living faith and confronting formalism.

He would not ask for conversion to Christianity, but a return to the core of the Law: "You shall love the Lord your God" (Deuteronomy 6:5) and "you shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18), just as He Himself unites them (Matthew 22:37–40). His call would be one of the heart, not of polemic.

## Between Jews and Christians

Yeshua never places Himself within camps. He does not belong to "the Jews" against "the Christians," nor the reverse. His criterion is life lived: "Whoever does the will of My Father is My brother and sister and mother" (Matthew 12:50). That is why Yeshua would be closer to the Jews who live mercy and justice, closer to the Christians who live love and freedom, and equally far from any religion that defends itself instead of serving life.

Yeshua remains a Jew in depth, anchored in the prophetic core of His tradition. He refuses the absolutizing of tradition, rejects political messianism, and gathers people around what is alive. He would be neither "Zionist" nor "anti-Zionist." He would be neither "Orthodox" in the legalistic sense nor "liberal" in the relativist sense. He would not ask who is right, but where living justice is. And He would stand exactly there — among people — not between camps.

## How Yeshua would relate to "MAGA Christianity"

Exactly the same tensions existed in Yeshua's own time: religion allied with political power, sacralized national identity, fear of "others," selective moralism, and the use of God as an instrument of legitimation. Seen through this lens, the reactions and words of Yeshua take on a surprising clarity for today's context. Yeshua never aligns Himself with a political project, a national identity, or a movement of power.

He says without equivocation: "My Kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36). When the crowds want to make Him king, He withdraws (John 6:15). Not because He is indifferent to social suffering, but because His perspective is the Kingdom as life lived, not as a program of governance. The Kingdom, for Yeshua, is not installed through power, but through the transformation of the heart.

In this sense, any form of Christianity that mixes the Gospel with the political project, the cross with the flag, the Kingdom with the state, would represent for Yeshua exactly the confusion that He constantly avoids. For Him, this shift is not a legitimate strategy, but a clear spiritual sign that trust has moved from God to power.

When Yeshua says, "Give to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's" (Mark 12:17), He does not bless political power, but delimits it. The political does not become theological. Power remains power, and God remains God. Yeshua refuses to turn governance into an expression of faith. In political Christianity, by contrast, the political is theologized: leaders become "anointed," policies become "biblical," and opposition is labeled "anti-God." Yeshua would immediately recognize this dynamic, because it is the same alliance of religion and power that condemned Him, when the religious leaders said: "We have no king but Caesar" (John 19:15). For Yeshua, the moral criterion is not "mine first," but "the vulnerable first."

He says clearly: "I was a stranger and you welcomed Me" (Matthew 25:35). When He is asked, "Who is my neighbor?" He answers with the parable of the Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37), in which the foreigner becomes the model of humanity. The neighbor is not defined by a border, but by need. The community is not protected through moralized exclusion, but through active compassion. Yeshua does not deny the existence of administrative borders, but He does not sacralize them.

He crosses cultural and religious borders: He enters Samaria (John 4), praises the faith of a Roman (Matthew 8:10), heals those considered "outsiders." For Him, security is not built through walls, but through justice and truth. That is why one of His most repeated calls remains: "Do not be afraid" (Luke 12:32). A faith that lives from fear has already departed from the center of the Gospel.

His tone toward those who use religion as a weapon would be firm, just as in the Gospels. "Why do you see the speck in your brother's eye?" (Matthew 7:3). "Not everyone who says to Me, 'Lord, Lord'…" (Matthew 7:21). As back then, the rebuke would be harsher toward those who consider themselves righteous than toward those considered sinners. The problem is not moral weakness, but the use of God to justify fear, exclusion, and domination.

Constantly, Yeshua calls to the welcoming of the other: "What you have done to one of these least brothers of Mine, you have done to Me" (Matthew 25:40). The stranger, the migrant, the marginalized are not threats, but places of encounter with God. This is not naivety, but discernment without fear. The difference of perspective thus becomes clear.

Yeshua refuses an alliance with power, does not sacralize the nation, does not live from fear, places mercy before identity, and sees the person before the category. Any form of Christianity that sacralizes politics, absolutizes the nation, operates from fear, moralizes selectively, and excludes in the name of God is in profound tension with the Gospel pattern. Yeshua would not be captive to any ideology. Not because He would be "left-wing" or "right-wing," but because He would not be aligned with any logic of power. He would say what He always said: the Kingdom of God is not about gaining power, but about losing fear. And, as back then, those who use religion in order to feel "righteous" would be the ones most scandalized by Him.

## Why Yeshua never confirmed conspiracy theories

A surprising fact becomes visible when we read the Gospels carefully: Yeshua lived in a world full of real conspiracies, but He never built His message around them. The historical context was extremely tense. Judea was under Roman occupation, there was a religious class allied with political power, and various Jewish groups believed that Rome, together with the religious elites, was conspiring against the people.

In this context, it would have been very easy for Yeshua to mobilize the masses through a rhetoric of exposing the system. And yet, He chose a completely different path. Instead of confirming narratives about political or religious plots, He constantly shifts the attention toward the interior of the person. That is why He says: "The Kingdom of God is not coming in ways that can be observed… for behold, the Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:20–21).

Instead of offering a conspiratorial explanation of the world, Yeshua points to another level of reality: the transformation of consciousness. This choice becomes even more evident when we consider that the people around Him expected exactly the opposite. Many hoped for a political Messiah who would explain the evil of the world through Roman oppression and lead a revolt. But Yeshua refuses this simplistic interpretation of evil.

When He is asked about paying taxes to the empire — an extremely politically sensitive subject — He answers in a way that completely disarms the logic of conflict: "Give therefore to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's" (Matthew 22:21). Through this statement, He avoids both imperial propaganda and revolutionary rhetoric. He does not confirm any global conspiracy against the people, but separates the planes: there are political structures of the world, and there is the spiritual reality of the person. His message is not a geopolitical analysis, but an invitation to inner transformation. Another reason Yeshua does not confirm conspiracies is that He sees evil in a light different from that of conspiratorial narratives.

Conspiracy theories usually presuppose the existence of conscious actors who control the system deliberately and malevolently. Yeshua, however, describes evil rather as spiritual unconsciousness. On the cross, at the culminating moment of historical injustice — when He is executed by the political and religious authorities — He does not say that He is the victim of a conspiracy. Instead He says: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34).

This statement completely changes the perspective on evil. There is no omnipotent cabal controlling the world; there are people who act out of spiritual blindness. The Gospels show that Yeshua was aware of the manipulations and hypocrisy of the religious leaders. He harshly criticizes the religious structures that exploit people's faith. For example, He says: "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you shut the Kingdom of Heaven in people's faces" (Matthew 23:13).

Yet, even when He denounces these behaviors, He does not build a total theory about a global plot. His critique is moral and spiritual, not conspiratorial. The problem is not a secret organization that controls everything, but the state of the human heart. In many places, Yeshua even warns against the obsession with spectacular signs or secret revelations about the world.

He says: "An evil and adulterous generation seeks a sign, but no sign will be given to it except the sign of Jonah" (Matthew 12:39). This statement is important, because conspiracy theories often function precisely through the promise of a "hidden truth" that explains everything. Yeshua refuses this logic. He does not offer total explanations about the mechanisms of the world, but invites a person to see something much closer: his own heart. That is why the theme of spiritual sight appears constantly in the Gospels.

Yeshua says: "If your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light" (Matthew 6:22). This sight is not about detecting political or economic conspiracies. It is about the inner clarity that allows a person to recognize the ego, fear, pride, and the need for control. In this sense, true blindness is not the lack of information about the world, but the inability to see one's own inner mechanisms.

Another reason Yeshua does not confirm conspiracies is that they can become a subtle form of collective victimhood. If the evil of the world is explained exclusively through external plots, personal responsibility disappears. By contrast, Yeshua places responsibility at the very center of His message. He says: "Why do you see the speck in your brother's eye and not notice the log in your own eye?" (Matthew 7:3).

This question shifts the attention from the analysis of the system's flaws to the transformation of one's own consciousness. Therefore, Yeshua's silence toward conspiracies is not political naivety. It is a profound spiritual choice. He knew that explaining evil through plots does not truly change the world. People can replace an official story with an anti-official story and still remain in the same state of consciousness. That is why He does not offer an alternative ideology.

Instead, He says: "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32). The freedom He speaks of is not the political freedom obtained by exposing a plot, but the inner freedom that appears when a person sees reality without fear and without identification with his own mental stories. Thus, the Gospels suggest that the true revolution Yeshua brings is not a political or conspiratorial one, but a revolution of consciousness. He does not promise that the world will become transparent and fully explainable. Instead, He promises that a person can become enlightened within it. And this light does not come from the discovery of a hidden plot, but from the transformation of the heart.

## How Yeshua would react to "sexual immorality"

The way sexuality is treated says a great deal about the level of consciousness from which a religious community operates. We are not speaking only about rules, but about what you see when you look at a person. The response of Yeshua Christ, of the Apostle Paul, and of contemporary Christianity differs radically — not in the degree of "tolerance," but in the vantage point from which they view the problem.

If Yeshua were to learn that a member of the religious community was living in sexual relationships considered "immoral," there is no indication in the Gospels that His first gesture would be exclusion, public exposure, or moral correction. Yeshua never treated sexuality as an isolated problem, but as a symptom of the heart. He would begin with an encounter — real, personal, free of any tribunal. The model is constant: Yeshua draws near to people before they change.

With the tax collectors, with the prostitutes, with the Samaritan woman, with the woman caught in adultery — presence comes before transformation. Yeshua does not normalize the behavior, but neither does He absolutize it. For Him, sin is not the sexual act in itself, but the rupture from truth and from oneself that sometimes expresses itself sexually as well. If He were to say anything, it would be said in private, not as a sanction, but as an invitation: "What are you searching for here?", "What is unfulfilled in you?", "What are you hungry for?"

For Yeshua, "disordered" sexuality is a sign of thirst, not of rebellion. His correction — "go and sin no more" — comes after the person is seen, not before. For Yeshua, to remove someone from the community because of sexuality would be a nonsense: it is precisely the wounded who need community. Paul reacts differently, because his mission is different. He does not go from village to village meeting individuals, but builds fragile communities, under external and internal pressure.

That is why, in his letters, "immoral" sexuality becomes a serious problem, not merely personal but communal. In the case at Corinth, Paul explicitly asks for the temporary removal of the one living in a relationship considered gravely immoral. His motivation is not shaming, but contamination: "a little leaven leavens the whole lump." For Paul, sexual sin is not merely a personal weakness, but a threat to the identity of the community and of its message.

And yet, even here, the goal is not final. Paul speaks of discipline so that the person may awaken, not so that he may be lost. Empathy exists, but it is subordinated to truth and order. The person is seen through the prism of the impact of his behavior, not only through his inner suffering. It is a functional logic, but already colder than that of Yeshua. Contemporary Christianity, in many contexts, has lost both the heart of Yeshua and the discernment of Paul.

It has remained with the rule, but without the relationship. Thus, when a case of "sexual immorality" appears, the typical reaction is stigmatization: whispers, labels, public shame, rapid exclusion, or a "call to repentance" under pressure. Sexuality becomes a marker of identity: the one who errs here is "more sinful" than the one who is proud, cold, devoid of empathy, or manipulative — sins that Yeshua condemned far more harshly.

Today's Christianity often treats sexuality as the gravest sin, because it is visible, easy to judge, and offers a false sense of moral superiority. Within this framework, a person is no longer asked "what are you living?" but "why do you not observe the rule?" The moral analysis excludes empathy, exactly as we discussed earlier. Correction comes without relationship, truth without presence, and the result is not healing, but hiding. People do not change; they only learn to lie better.

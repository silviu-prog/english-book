# 1. Who Is Yeshua

## Yeshua or Jesus

At the heart of this book lies a distinction that, though it may seem merely formal, profoundly changes the way the message of Jesus is understood: the difference between Yeshua and Jesus. Yeshua is the Aramaic form of the name borne by the Jewish man from Galilee. It was a common name in the first century, derived from Yehoshua, and it means "God saves" or "God delivers." This is how his family, his friends, his disciples, and ordinary people called him.

Yeshua belongs to a living context: a culture, a language, a concrete history. He is the man who lived, spoke, healed, provoked, and loved. Jesus, by contrast, is the Greco-Latin form of the same name (Iesous in Greek, Iesus in Latin), through which the figure of Yeshua entered the theological, imperial, and later dogmatic world.

"Jesus" is already a name laden with centuries of interpretation, doctrines, councils, and definitions concerning divine nature, sin, salvation, and religious authority. This book does not claim that one is "true" and the other "false."

## Isus or Iisus?

In the Romanian language, a question often arises: which form is correct — "Isus" or "Iisus"? Both are correct, but they come from different traditions. The form *Iisus* is specific to the Orthodox tradition and more faithfully reflects the transliteration from ancient Greek (Iēsous), where the initial vowel is lengthened. For this reason, Orthodox writings and translations use "Iisus Hristos."

The form *Isus* is common in modern Romanian, having been adopted in more recent translations of the Bible and in academic, theological, and secular usage. It is the form most widespread in current use, and it does not represent a "mistaken simplification" but rather a natural linguistic adaptation. This book uses the form *Yeshua*, not for confessional reasons, but for the sake of readability and clarity for a contemporary readership.

The choice does not imply a theological stance and does not deny the validity of the form "Jesus." In essence, the form of the name is not what is at stake. What is at stake is whether we are speaking of a religious figure being defended or a living message, lived and embraced. This book seeks the Yeshua behind the name.

## Why Yeshua Did Not Found a Religion

One of the most striking things, when you read Yeshua without the lenses of the institution, is that he does not seem interested in building a system. And, honestly, that is hard to accept if you come from within a system. He does not establish an organization with hierarchies, procedures, doctrinal manuals, and mechanisms of belonging. He does not demand adherence to a formula; he invites a transformation of one's way of being. Instead of saying "believe in my system," he says "follow me" (Mark 1:17).

But "follow me" is not an invitation to conformity; it is a call to a way of life that is recognized by its fruit, not by its slogan. Yeshua speaks obsessively about the Kingdom, but the Kingdom is not an institution. It is a lived reality. He says that the Kingdom "does not come in a way that can be observed" and that "the Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:20–21). If you take this seriously, almost all the religion we know disappears.

It is the perfect antibody against institutionalization: if the Kingdom is within, no one can possess, administer, or control it. Institutions live by monopoly. Yeshua's Kingdom lives by freedom. Yeshua harshly criticizes religion as a system of purity and status. Not faith as a living relationship with God, but religion as a mechanism of separation and superiority.

When he says, "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites," his criticism is not aimed at prayer or at Scripture, but at a type of "holiness" that produces proud, closed, rigid people, and that uses the law in order to feel above others (Matthew 23). The problem is not morality, but moralism: the use of morality as a weapon of identity. And here it becomes uncomfortable, because moralism is far easier than transformation. Moreover, Yeshua constantly breaks the boundaries of belonging.

He speaks with Samaritans, he praises the faith of a Roman centurion, he allows himself to be touched by those considered "unclean," he eats with sinners (John 4:7–10; Matthew 8:10–11; Mark 2:16–17). Within a religious system, the "unclean" person is excluded in order to protect the purity of the group. In Yeshua's logic, that is precisely where the work lies: not to relativize the truth, but to demonstrate that God is not the property of those who are "clean."

If Yeshua had founded a religion, he would have created clear criteria of belonging: formulas, rituals, signs. Instead, he speaks of fruit: "by their fruit you will know them" (Matthew 7:16). Fruit cannot be falsified indefinitely, whereas belonging can. Religion needs external identifiers. The Kingdom needs internal transformation. This is where the historical tension arises: after Yeshua's death, the community must survive.

And this is where the compromise begins, perhaps inevitably. Any community, in order to survive, becomes an organization. An organization needs boundaries, rules, doctrine. And so a religion *about* Yeshua is born. But the religion *of* Yeshua remains something else: a way of being that cannot be fully captured by an institution. That is why, in every age, Yeshua remains uncomfortable even for those who "defend" him.

## Did Yeshua Intend to Create Christianity?

In the words and deeds of Yeshua, there never appears any intention to found a new religion, an institution, or a separate doctrinal system. Yeshua does not use the term "Christian," does not define dogmas, does not build hierarchical structures, and does not draw confessional boundaries. The very idea of "Christian" would probably have sounded strange even to his disciples. He speaks constantly about the Kingdom of God, not about a religion.

"The Kingdom of God is near." (Mark 1:15) "The Kingdom of God is within you." (Luke 17:21) Yeshua does not call people to adhere to a system, but to enter into a state of life. His call is existential, not institutional. He does not say "create an organization," but: "Follow me." (Matthew 4:19) This following is not a formal belonging, but a transformation of one's way of being. Yeshua does not propose a new religion, but a new humanity.

## Where Does Christianity Appear as a Religion?

Christianity, as a theological, doctrinal, and institutional system, appears historically after Yeshua. It is not born directly from his life and message, but above all from the interpretation, formulation, and systematization carried out afterward, especially by the Apostle Paul. In this process, the emphasis gradually shifts from the Kingdom to salvation, from living to doctrinally formulated belief, from a living example to a sacrifice centralized in a single event.

The Pauline formula "We preach Christ crucified" (1 Corinthians 1:23) becomes the nucleus of the new religious construction. This shift has an obvious advantage: the message becomes easier to transmit on a large scale, easier to preach, easier to organize. At the same time, however, it also becomes easier to separate from lived experience. The emphasis shifts from "follow me" to "believe about me," from inner transformation to the acceptance of a doctrine.

Here Christianity appears as a religion of the creed, not merely as a path of becoming. Not as a life lived, but as a truth declared.

## Why the Institution of the Church Nonetheless Appeared

After Yeshua's death, the community had to survive. Any community that endures through time inevitably becomes an institution. Rules, roles, buildings, and doctrine appear. This process is historical and, to a certain extent, inevitable. But to confuse this evolution with Yeshua's original intention is to project back onto him a logic that he constantly contested.

Yeshua explicitly warns against hierarchies of a religious type: "you are not to be called rabbi… for you have one Teacher" (Matthew 23:8). This warning is hard to reconcile with the idea that he would have intended a rigid clerical structure. So Yeshua did not want to have churches in the institutional sense. He wanted there to be a living community, built on recognition, not on power.

When he says, "on this rock I will build my Church," he is referring not to buildings, not to offices, not to a succession of authority, but to a consciousness that sees and to a community that lives by this seeing. The Church, in Yeshua's sense, is not a place where you go. It is a way of being together.

## Yeshua and the Religious Institution

The relationship between Yeshua and the religious institution is one of the most tension-filled and revealing themes of the Gospels. Not because Yeshua was against faith, but because he constantly refused to allow faith to be reduced to a system that controls life. By contrast, Paul would lay the foundations of an organization without which the message would not have survived historically, but this organization would profoundly change the perspective.

The Christianity that came after them would gradually transform this organization into an institution of power, sometimes in direct opposition to Yeshua's original spirit. Yeshua is not anti-religious. He lives within Judaism, goes to the synagogue "as was his custom" (Luke 4:16), takes part in the feasts (John 7), and quotes the Scriptures with profound naturalness. His criticism is not directed against faith, but against the moment when faith becomes a mechanism of control.

Yeshua's major conflict is not with sinners, but with the religious leaders: "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!" (Matthew 23:27). The reason is repeated: the institution had come to defend the rule more than life itself. "They tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear, and lay them on people's shoulders" (Matthew 23:4).

In Yeshua's view, the institution becomes dangerous when it absolutizes the rule, legitimizes itself through fear, excludes in the name of God, and protects its own authority more than the truth. Instead of demanding the reform of the institution, Yeshua relativizes it through a statement that moves the center of the relationship with God from the outside to the inside: "The hour is coming when you will worship neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem" (John 4:21).

This is not a geographical criticism, but a theological one. The relationship with God will no longer depend on a place, a temple, or a religious caste, but on a state of the human being: "The true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and in truth" (John 4:23). Here the rupture occurs: the institution is no longer the center. Yeshua refuses to ground his authority on office or on institutional validation.

He forgives without ritual: "Son, your sins are forgiven" (Mark 2:5). He heals without permission, even on the Sabbath, provoking harsh reactions: "They watched him to see whether he would heal him on the Sabbath" (Mark 3:2). He speaks directly in the name of God, without asking anyone's approval. That is why the Gospels note people's astonishment: "He taught as one who had authority, not as the scribes" (Matthew 7:29). The institution functions through the delegation of authority; Yeshua functions through presence. This is the essential rupture.

With Paul, the problem is different. He does not know Yeshua historically and does not enter into direct conflict with the Jewish institution in the same way. His mission is to build stable communities in a hostile and fragmented world. For this, organization becomes inevitable. Paul introduces structures of leadership — "overseers" and "elders" (1 Timothy 3) — communal rules, criteria of belonging, and internal discipline.

He warns against "strange teachings" (Galatians 1:6–9) and insists on order: "God is not a God of disorder but of peace" (1 Corinthians 14:33). For Paul, the institution is not the enemy of life, but the condition of the message's survival. The emphasis begins to shift from Yeshua's radical freedom to doctrinal unity and communal stability. This shift is pragmatic and, in a sense, necessary.

But it changes the perspective: faith begins to be administered. What in Yeshua was a direct encounter with God gradually becomes belonging to an organized community that guards the message. The Christianity that came after Paul would carry this logic further. With the alliance between the Church and political power, the religious institution becomes the supreme moral authority, the administrator of salvation, the arbiter of truth, and the guardian of social order.

The relationship with God begins to be mediated by hierarchies, obligatory rituals, fixed dogmas, and sanctions. What in Paul was organization for protection becomes institution for control. The fear of error replaces trust in discernment. The question becomes dangerous. Direct experience becomes suspect.

It was precisely this dynamic that Yeshua had criticized when he said: "You have taken away the key of knowledge; you yourselves did not enter, and you hindered those who were entering" (Luke 11:52). The difference in perspective becomes clear when we look at the relationship between life, authority, and institution. In Yeshua, the institution is always relative. Life is the measure of truth, not the framework that contains it. Authority springs from the coherence between word and life, not from position.

That is why Yeshua can say: "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). He does not send people toward an institution in order to encounter God; he sends them back into their own awakened consciousness. In Paul, the institution becomes necessary for coherence and continuity. Authority also comes from fidelity to the message preached, and faith begins to be structured and protected from chaos.

In later Christianity, this displacement reaches the point of inversion: the institution becomes central, and life is called to conform to it. Truth no longer flows from life toward the institution, but is defined by the institution and transmitted to life. Yeshua did not come to build an institution. He came to free the relationship with God from dependence on institutions. Paul built structures so that the message would survive.

Later Christianity often transformed the structures into an end in themselves. Whenever the institution serves life, it remains legitimate. Whenever it begins to serve itself, the words of Yeshua resound once more: what truly matters has been forgotten.

## Did Yeshua Want to Have Churches?

The question of whether Yeshua intended to create "churches" in the sense in which we know them today is one of the most important and most uncomfortable questions of Christianity. For if the answer is "no," then a great part of what we today call religious life must be reread not as the fulfillment of Yeshua's will, but as a later historical development.

In the Gospels, Yeshua does not speak about buildings, hierarchies, standardized rituals, or administrative structures. He does not sketch out an institutional plan, does not designate offices, does not create a formal organization. His message constantly revolves around the Kingdom of God — a living, dynamic, present reality, not a fixed institution.

That is why, when we come to the sentence "On this rock I will build my Church" (Matthew 16:18), it is essential to pause and ask: what did Yeshua mean by "church"? The word translated as "church" is *ekklesia*. In the context of the first century, *ekklesia* did not denote a sacred edifice, nor a religious institution. It meant, quite simply, an assembly, a calling together, a convened community.

It was a term used in civic life to describe a gathering of people called to take part in a common reality. Therefore, when Yeshua says "I will build my Church," he is not speaking of walls, altars, or structures, but of a living community, formed of people who live out of a different consciousness. The "building" is not architectural, but relational and existential.

This interpretation is confirmed by the fact that Yeshua never associates *ekklesia* with any particular sacred space. On the contrary, he constantly relativizes space: "The hour is coming when you will worship the Father neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem… the true worshipers will worship in spirit and in truth" (John 4:21–23). Such a statement is incompatible with the idea that Yeshua sought to build a religion centered on sacred locations.

The statement to Peter appears at a key moment: the disciples are asked who they believe Yeshua is. After various answers, Peter says: "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" (Matthew 16:16). Yeshua's reply is famous: "You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my Church" (Matthew 16:18). Institutional Christian tradition would interpret this "rock" as the person of Peter, from whom a line of authority, succession, and power would derive.

But the text, read carefully, allows a far deeper understanding, one more coherent with the rest of Yeshua's message. Yeshua does not say: "on you, Peter, as an individual, I will build an institution." He makes a play on words between Peter's name (Petros — stone) and petra — rock, a term that indicates a foundation, not an office.

The "rock" is not the man Peter with his weaknesses — whom Yeshua will rebuke just a few verses later ("Get behind me, Satan!" — Matthew 16:23) — but the revelation that Peter has just spoken. Yeshua clarifies this immediately: "flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father" (Matthew 16:17). So the "rock" is not a personal quality of Peter, but the capacity to see.

It is the recognition of Yeshua beyond appearances, a seeing that does not come from reason, tradition, or status, but from an inner openness. On this "rock" — that is, on this awakened consciousness — Yeshua says he will "build" his community. The Church, in Yeshua's sense, is formed of people who see, not of people who hold offices. Of people who recognize, not of people who administer.

This interpretation is confirmed by the fact that Yeshua does not grant Peter a role of domination. On the contrary, Peter will fail gravely, he will deny Yeshua (Matthew 26:69–75), and his restoration will not come through institutional reinstatement, but through a relational question: "Do you love me?" (John 21:15–17). Authority, in Yeshua, does not come from office, but from love.

## What "I Will Build" Means

The "building" of which Yeshua speaks is not an act of rapid construction, but a living process. Yeshua does not say "I will establish," "I will organize," or "I will administer." He says "I will build" — an organic, slow, relational verb. This building happens through transformed relationships, people who learn to live without fear, communities that do not operate on domination. That is why Yeshua adds: "the gates of hell will not prevail against it" (Matthew 16:18).

"Hell" (Hades) is not a place of fire, but the symbol of death, fear, and separation. A community built on seeing and relationship cannot be destroyed by fear, because it is not based on control.

## How Yeshua Wants Us to Relate to Him

From his own words, Yeshua never presents himself as an inaccessible exception, severed from the human condition, but as a model of living that can be followed. He does not speak of himself as an unattainable ideal, but as an open path. His language is constantly one of imitation, of participation, and of becoming: not "look at me," but "follow me."

This perspective is clear in his gestures and teachings, when he says: "I have given you an example, that you also should do as I have done" (John 13:15). Here, Yeshua directly links truth to life, not to passive admiration. The same perspective appears even more clearly when he affirms: "Whoever believes in me will also do the works that I do" (John 14:12).

Faith, in his sense, is not intellectual assent, but entry into a state of consciousness from which the same deeds spring. Yeshua does not ask to be venerated from a distance, but to be followed from within. To follow him does not mean to repeat external gestures, but to enter into the same quality of being: love without conditions, truth without a mask, full presence, and the abandonment of the ego as the center of life.

When Yeshua says: "Be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect" (Matthew 5:48), he is not proposing a rigid and impossible moral perfection, but is speaking of the fullness of love. This call would make no sense if it were fundamentally impossible. In his logic, perfection is not ethical performance, but the maturing of the heart. That is why he also affirms that "the Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21).

Yeshua does not position himself as a substitute for the maturing of the human being, but as its living direction. He does not say, "You will never be able to be like me," but constantly utters the simple and radical call: "Follow me."

## Would Today's Christianity Recognize Yeshua?

The Gospels offer an uncomfortable precedent that cannot be avoided: Yeshua was not recognized by the religious elites of his time. Not because they were less devout or less concerned with God, but because they were deeply dependent on a system through which they administered the truth. It was precisely this dependence that made it impossible to recognize life when it appeared outside the framework.

The same risk exists today, in different forms, but with the same underlying structure. Yeshua had no recognized credentials. "How does this man know the Scriptures, when he has never been taught?" (John 7:15) expresses not curiosity, but suspicion. He relativized the form whenever it blocked life: "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27). He forgave without institutional permission: "Son, your sins are forgiven" (Mark 2:5–7).

He healed on the Sabbath, provoking the system at its sensitive point (Mark 3:1–6). He ate with the excluded, undermining the moral boundaries that defined belonging (Luke 5:29–32). All these were not accidental deviations, but signs of a different axis. The verdict that Yeshua pronounces remains one of the harshest in the Gospels: "You have taken away the key of knowledge; you yourselves did not enter, and you hindered those who were entering" (Luke 11:52).

The problem was not a lack of religious zeal, but the transformation of religion into a system for controlling access to God. If Yeshua were to appear today in his real form, the reactions would be surprisingly familiar. There would be institutional suspicion: "Who authorized you?" — perhaps phrased more elegantly, but with the same meaning (Matthew 21:23).

Doctrinal anxiety would arise, because Yeshua would once again preach the Kingdom as a life lived, not as a system of correct ideas: "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord'…" (Matthew 7:21). There would also be a profound discomfort with his freedom. Yeshua puts love before the rule: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Matthew 12:7). He puts freedom before belonging: "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32).

For a Christianity centered on control, demarcation, and doctrinal security, this freedom would sound dangerous. History repeats itself not out of malice, but out of blindness. Yeshua names this blindness as not moral, but perceptual: "Blind guides" (Matthew 23:16). It appears wherever authority is confused with office, truth with doctrine, fidelity with conformity. When the system becomes the goal, life becomes a threat.

Yeshua remains uncomfortable for any structure that defends itself, because he does not negotiate the truth in order to protect the form. As then, not everyone would be blind. Some "from within" would sense the truth without being able to control it. Nicodemus comes by night, with fear and sincerity (John 3). Joseph of Arimathea takes the risk when everything seems lost (John 19:38). They would be few, but decisive.

People who put life before role, conscience before position, truth before security. It is plausible that a part of today's Christianity would not recognize the real Yeshua, not because he would be too little religious, but because he would be too alive for a system that prefers control over transformation.

And the question would repeat itself, almost identically: "By what authority are you doing these things?" The answer would be, as then, not a theoretical justification, but life itself — a life that heals, liberates, and tells the truth without asking permission.

## Today's Churches Would Reject Yeshua

Looked at closely, many contemporary Christian churches operate according to the Pauline perspective rather than that of Yeshua. They rest on clearly delimited dogmas, on firm boundaries between "us" and "them," on morality transformed into an identity marker, and on a well-established hierarchical authority. These elements offer coherence and predictability, but they come at a cost: the reduction of life to conformity.

"You hold to the tradition of men" (Mark 7:8) remains the warning that hovers over any system that sustains itself through rules. Within this framework, Yeshua would quickly appear as an uncomfortable presence. His refusal to judge quickly, his constant closeness to those labeled "sinners," his criticism of religious hypocrites, and his silence before trap questions would be read as relativism or a lack of firmness.

"Why do you eat and drink with tax collectors and sinners?" (Luke 5:30) is the question that repeats itself, in other forms, in every age. Yeshua would be perceived as too soft on sin, subversive toward order, and lacking in "doctrinal clarity." Most likely, he would be invited to a "clarifying" discussion. He would be asked to formulate his positions, to define his limits, to fall in line. If he refused — as he did then — he would become problematic.

Not excluded immediately, but neutralized through labeling: "he has good intentions, but he creates confusion." "Whoever is not with us is against us" (cf. Mark 9:38–40) is the logic that reappears whenever freedom threatens the framework.

## Why Yeshua Refuses Political Power

The refusal of political power is one of the most constant and most difficult-to-understand traits of Yeshua. It appears from the beginning of his public activity and remains unchanged until the end. Yeshua not only does not seek political power, but actively avoids it, even when it is offered to him indirectly or when it seems to be the "natural" solution for imposing his message. This refusal is neither naivety nor a lack of historical realism.

It is a position deeply coherent with the way Yeshua understands the nature of evil, of change, and of human freedom. The episode of the temptations in the wilderness sets the framework for this refusal from the very beginning. When he is offered "all the kingdoms of the world" and their glory (Matthew 4:8–10), the temptation is not an abstract one. It is the invitation to produce good through control, to impose justice from the outside, to accelerate change through force.

Yeshua refuses, not because power is evil in itself, but because political power operates on a logic incompatible with the Kingdom of God. The political changes behaviors through coercion; the Kingdom changes consciousnesses through truth. Throughout the Gospels, Yeshua systematically avoids any association with messianic political projects. After the multiplication of the loaves, the crowd wants to make him king, and he withdraws alone to the mountain (John 6:15).

The gesture is revealing: at the very moment when he would have had popular legitimacy, Yeshua refuses. For him, power obtained through the enthusiasm of the crowd is just as dangerous as power obtained through force. Both replace inner freedom with dependence on a leader. Yeshua knows that political power, even when it begins with good intentions, tends to perpetuate itself. It quickly creates mechanisms of exclusion, hierarchies, and justifications for "necessary" violence.

That is why, when he is asked about political authority — "Should we pay taxes to Caesar or not?" — he refuses the trap (Mark 12:13–17). He does not position himself as an anti-imperial revolutionary, nor as a supporter of the existing order. His answer does not legitimize the system; it relativizes it. Caesar has a limited sphere. God does not compete with him for control of the world, because the Kingdom is not of the same category.

Before Pilate, Yeshua formulates this difference explicitly: "My Kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36). The statement does not mean that the Kingdom has no impact in the world, but that it does not function according to the logic of worldly power. If it had been "of this world," Yeshua says, his servants would have fought. But it is precisely the absence of armed struggle that is the distinguishing sign of this Kingdom. The refusal of political power is therefore an act of profound lucidity.

Yeshua understands that change imposed from the outside produces conformity, not transformation. The political can create order, but it cannot create love. It can regulate behaviors, but it cannot heal fear. The Kingdom of God begins precisely where fear loses its authority. And fear cannot be eliminated through greater fear. By refusing political power, Yeshua refuses to become what he criticizes. He does not want to replace one domination with another. He does not want to be "a good Caesar."

He wants to reveal another meaning of existence, in which truth is not imposed but recognized, and freedom is not negotiated but embraced.

## Why Yeshua Is Always Dangerous to Religion

Institutional religion needs predictability, norms, hierarchies, and control of meaning. Yeshua, however, brings presence, freedom, confrontation with oneself, and personal responsibility. He moves the center from the system to the heart. "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21) disarms any mechanism that promises salvation through belonging. That is why Yeshua was not killed by "sinners," but by the defenders of the truth.

Not by the world, but by religious leaders convinced that they were defending God. "It is better that one man die for the people" (John 11:50) shows how quickly truth becomes a danger when it cannot be controlled. The same pattern remains valid today. A Yeshua without a title, without institutional confirmation, without "verifiable" miracles, but with the same cutting lucidity and uncomfortable love, would be perceived as a spiritual threat, not as good news.

The chances that Yeshua would be immediately recognized as authentic are surprisingly small. Not because he would say something different, but because he would say the same thing — and that would expose how far we have moved away from him. The Apostle Paul might be cautious or suspicious, out of concern for the community. Today's churches, in many contexts, would become defensive or rejecting.

The supreme truth of this exercise is simple and painful: Yeshua is not dangerous to sin, but to religion without a heart. Wherever religion chooses certainty over presence, control over love, Yeshua will always be… suspect. "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32) remains the promise that cannot be tamed by any system.

## Why Christianity Moved the Resurrection from Consciousness to the Tomb

One of the most subtle changes that occurred in the history of Christianity is the shift of the emphasis of the resurrection from within the human being to events outside of history. In the Gospels, the resurrection often appears as a present reality, an awakening of consciousness, a passage from inner death to spiritual life. But in the later theological development, the emphasis was shifted almost exclusively onto the physical event of Christ's resurrection and onto the resurrection of bodies at the end of the world.

So what could have been lived as a transformative experience in a person's life became largely a doctrine about the future. In the Gospels, Yeshua speaks of eternal life not as a post-mortem reward, but as a reality that begins now. He says: "Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life and does not come into judgment, but has passed from death to life" (John 5:24).

We note the present tense: *has* eternal life and *has passed*. It is not only a future promise, but a present transformation. Death and life are not only biological states, but states of consciousness. This idea appears again a few verses later: "The hour is coming, and is now here, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live" (John 5:25). If we read it literally, it seems absurd: how can the dead hear? But it is precisely here that the spiritual meaning is hidden.

The "dead" are those who live without consciousness, without love, without an inner life. The voice of Yeshua is the call to awakening. This perspective is confirmed by the way Yeshua speaks about the Kingdom of God. He says: "The Kingdom of God does not come in a way that can be observed… for behold, the Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:20–21).

If the Kingdom is within, then the resurrection too — which is the entry into this new life — must be, at least in part, an inner transformation. Over time, however, the message began to be understood more and more in external terms. Instead of the resurrection being seen as a spiritual experience accessible to every person, it became primarily a unique historical event and an eschatological promise.

In this shift of emphasis, the human being becomes a spectator rather than a participant. Christ has risen — and we wait. But the language of the New Testament suggests an active participation. Paul says: "We were buried with him through baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4). Christ's resurrection now becomes the model of a personal transformation.

It is not merely a miracle to be contemplated, but a path to be followed. In the same spirit, Paul affirms: "If then you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above" (Colossians 3:1). Again, the verb is in the past tense: *if you have been raised*. This suggests that the resurrection can already be lived as a change in the way of seeing the world, the way of loving, the way of being. The danger of externalizing the resurrection is that inner transformation becomes secondary.

A person may remain in the same consciousness dominated by fear, resentment, and separation, yet believe that the resurrection nonetheless concerns him — because it is a doctrine he accepts. In this case, the resurrection is no longer an awakening of consciousness, but merely a theological affirmation. But if we return to the spirit of the Gospels, the resurrection appears as a change of perspective on life. It is the passage from fear to trust, from judgment to compassion, from ego to love.

The Apostle John expresses this transformation in a simple sentence: "We know that we have passed from death to life, because we love the brothers" (1 John 3:14). Here the criterion of the resurrection is not an external event, but the appearance of love. In this sense, the resurrection can be understood as a passage from a fragmented consciousness to an integrated one. From separation to unity. From reaction to presence.

This is what spiritual traditions sometimes call transcendence: the surpassing of the previous level of consciousness. That is why the resurrection should not be thought of only as a single moment, but as a continuous process. Each time a false identity dies and a deeper understanding is born, a resurrection takes place. Each time fear gives way to love, a resurrection takes place. Each time a person renounces separation and discovers unity, a resurrection takes place.

Yeshua himself describes this process through the metaphor of the seed: "Unless a grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). The death of the seed is not destruction, but transformation. This is precisely the logic of the resurrection. So the resurrection is not merely emerging from a tomb. It is emerging from a limited consciousness. It is the passage from a life lived in fear to a life lived in love.

It is the moment when death — in its inner form — loses its power. That is why Paul says of Christ: "Christ, being raised from the dead, will never die again; death no longer has dominion over him" (Romans 6:9). But this affirmation also becomes an invitation. For when a person passes through his own inner resurrection, death begins to lose that same power over him as well. In this profound sense, Christ's resurrection is not merely an event to be celebrated. It is a map of the transformation of human consciousness. And the central question is no longer only whether Christ rose, but whether his resurrection produces a resurrection in us as well.

# 3. How Yeshua's Teaching Works

## Why Yeshua Speaks in Parables and Not in Doctrines

When you go looking for "doctrine" in Yeshua, you run into a problem: there isn't much to find. What you find instead are parables. And parables are not didactic lessons in the classical sense. They are devices that break the linear mind and force it to see. Doctrine tells you what to believe. A parable provokes your perception. Doctrine closes; a parable opens. This is why Yeshua prefers the parable: because he does not want mental assent, he wants awakening.

Yeshua says outright that he speaks in parables precisely because there is a difference between hearing and understanding: "This is why I speak to them in parables: because seeing they do not see, and hearing they do not hear, nor do they understand" (Matthew 13:13). This is not elitism but a diagnosis: there are people who have religion but no sight. They have information but no transformation. The parable slips past the defense mechanism of the religious ego. If you told a Pharisee directly, "You are a hypocrite," he would defend himself.

But if you tell him a story about two men who go up to pray, one boasting and the other humbling himself, and God justifies the second, the story gets past the guards and settles into the conscience (Luke 18:9–14). Then the inner work begins. The parable is an instrument of freedom. A doctrine can be memorized and used as an identity. A parable, by contrast, demands participation. It does not hand you the answer to wear as a badge; it sets you in front of a mirror.

"Who is my neighbor?" asks the expert in the law, searching for a definition. Yeshua gives no definition. He tells the story of the Samaritan. And at the end he asks, "Which of these became a neighbor?" (Luke 10:29–37). Notice the inversion: not "who deserves my love?" but "into what do I transform myself?" Doctrine asks about a category. The parable asks about being. Yeshua avoids doctrines because doctrines are easily confiscated by power.

A doctrinal system quickly creates a correctness police: who is "inside," who is "outside." Parables, by contrast, cannot be fully administered. They remain living provocations. This is why the Kingdom is described through fluid images: seed, leaven, treasure, a net (Matthew 13). You cannot build a tribunal out of leaven. You cannot control people with a seed. What you can do is wake them. There is another reason as well: Yeshua wants to speak on many levels.

A parable can be heard by a child, a fisherman, an intellectual, a Pharisee. Each takes what he can. Doctrine standardizes. The parable respects differences of maturity without humiliating them. "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" is not an insult. It is an invitation toward maturing (Mark 4:9). In the end, the parable preserves the mystery. And preserving the mystery is essential, because the reality of God is not a mental object.

Any doctrine, even when true, can become an idol if it replaces the encounter. Yeshua does not want to give you a system about God. He wants to bring you to the point where the Kingdom becomes visible.

## When Challenged Religiously, Yeshua Responds Existentially

Yeshua almost never engages in doctrinal disputes in the classical religious sense. He does not enter into theological polemics, does not construct treatises, does not defend a system, and does not try to reform Judaism through argument. He enters conflicts, but not over doctrine; he enters tensions, but not over systematic theology. And when it seems the discussion is about religion, it is in fact about the heart, about hypocrisy, and about real life.

Time and again, the context in which he is placed would have been ideal for deep theological debate. Instead, his responses constantly shift the conversation out of the sphere of religion and into the sphere of the human being's inner state.

## How Yeshua Preaches and How Today's Preachers Preach

Yeshua does not preach a doctrine but rather a way of being. His words are not explanations about God. They are invitations to awakening. He does not say "believe this," but "follow me." He does not build a system of ideas; he opens a path of inner transformation. His preaching is performative: what he says happens within the one who listens. "Repent, for the Kingdom of Heaven is near" (Matthew 4:17). Repentance, for Yeshua, does not mean guilt.

It means a change of mind, a shift in the center of consciousness. Yeshua speaks in parables not in order to be poetic. He does it to avoid rigidifying the truth. A parable cannot be turned into dogma without being killed. "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Matthew 11:15). His truth is not meant to be repeated. It is meant to be recognized. He does not explain the Kingdom; he points to it. "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). Yeshua's preaching is always about now.

He does not promise a future salvation in exchange for correct belief; he promises present life through lived truth. "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the Kingdom of Heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father" (Matthew 7:21). The emphasis is on being, not on affirming. On living, not on belonging. Paul changes the register radically. He does not preach the Kingdom. He preaches salvation. He does not preach a state of consciousness, but the central event: the cross.

"We preach Christ crucified" (1 Corinthians 1:23). The message becomes explanatory, theological, soteriological. In place of "follow me" comes "believe that." In place of immediate inner transformation comes justification by faith. In Paul, truth is no longer recognized inwardly but accepted intellectually.

"A person is justified by faith apart from the works of the law" (Romans 3:28). Here the subtle rupture appears: the life one lives is no longer the primary criterion, but rather adherence to a correct formula. Faith becomes a substitute for transformation, not its natural expression. This shift makes the message transmissible on a large scale. Paul builds the language, the structure, the theology that allow a global religion to arise. But at the same time, the message can become separated from life.

You can believe without changing. You can confess without living. You can be "in Christ" theologically without being alive existentially. Today's preachers carry this shift even further. In many contexts, preaching is no longer either an invitation to awakening or deep theological reflection, but emotional management, moralizing, or spiritual marketing. The sermon becomes either a list of rules, a promise of prosperity, or a motivational speech dressed in religious garb.

Truth is no longer something that changes you, but something that soothes or validates you. In contemporary preaching, the emphasis often falls on sin, fear, belonging, and authority. "Believe correctly so you'll be safe." "Belong so you'll be saved." "Respect the doctrine so you'll be accepted." Within this framework, Yeshua is often presented not as a model of life but as a legal solution to a theological problem. The cross becomes a contract, not a revelation. Yeshua, however, does not preach fear.

He preaches freedom. "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32). Paul preaches liberation from the Law through faith, but he leaves room for a new law: the law of doctrine. Today's preachers, in many cases, preach psychological security, not freedom. Order, not life. Conformity, not awakening. The essential difference is not one of meaning. Yeshua preaches from being to being. Paul preaches from interpretation to belief. Today's preachers often preach from institution to behavior. Yeshua awakens. Paul explains. The preachers administer. "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). In Yeshua, truth is alive. In Paul, truth is formulated. Among today's preachers, truth is often defended. But a truth that must be defended no longer sets free. A truth that is lived does.

Yeshua did not ask to be preached, but to be embodied. "You are the light of the world" (Matthew 5:14). Not "talk about the light," but be light. Here the circle closes. Authentic preaching is not the discourse from the pulpit, but the life that becomes visible. The rest are echoes—some useful, others distorting.

## Why Yeshua Accepts Gestures of Reverence Without Cultivating Idolization

In the Gospels there are episodes in which people throw themselves at Yeshua's feet, call him "Lord," or prostrate themselves before him (Matthew 14:33; John 9:38). Read through a modern lens, these gestures look like acts of cultic adoration. But read in the context of the ancient world and of biblical language, they are first of all gestures of gratitude, recognition, and thanksgiving for life received or restored—not attempts to turn Yeshua into an idol.

In the Semitic culture of the first century, prostration (proskynesis) carried a wide range of meanings: deep respect, recognition of an authority, thanksgiving for help received, or acknowledgment of God's presence in an event. It was not reserved exclusively for divine worship in the strict theological sense. People prostrated themselves before kings, prophets, or benefactors as a form of total thanksgiving and as an acknowledgment of their dependence in that moment.

Yeshua accepts these gestures because they express the truth of lived experience, not because he demands adoration. The episode of the calming of the storm is telling. The disciples "worshiped him," saying, "Truly you are the Son of God!" (Matthew 14:33). The reaction comes after their lives have been saved. The gesture is an explosion of gratitude and recognition, not the inauguration of a ritual. Yeshua does not request it, does not standardize it, and does not turn it into a criterion of belonging.

He receives it because the truth that was seen—a life saved—calls for a bodily language to match. Likewise, in the case of the man born blind, the "worship" (John 9:38) comes at the end of a journey of awakening to sight. The man says, "Lord, I believe," and prostrates himself as a sign of thanksgiving for the life regained and the meaning rediscovered. Here, worship is gratitude made flesh, not a cultic act.

Yeshua accepts it because it is the expression of a heart that sees, not because he wishes to be the object of devotion. The same logic appears in the episodes of pleading: the Canaanite woman (Matthew 15:25), the father of the sick child (Mark 9:20–24), the cleansed leper (Mark 1:40). People throw themselves at Yeshua's feet not to adore him theologically, but to give thanks, to ask for life, and to acknowledge the help received.

The gesture says: "I no longer control; I receive." Yeshua does not correct the form, because the form is true to their inner state. A decisive clue is that Yeshua does not turn gratitude into obligation. He never says "do this always," and he does not make healing conditional on prostration. On the contrary, in the episode of the ten lepers, only one returns to give thanks (Luke 17:15–19). Yeshua does not condemn the others.

He underscores the value of gratitude as an opening of the heart, not as a cultic norm. Thanksgiving is appreciated, not imposed. The contrast with the scene of the temptations is illuminating. There, Yeshua refuses the worship demanded as currency in exchange for power: "You shall worship the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve" (Matthew 4:10). Yeshua rejects worship that produces dependence, fear, or control, because that is exactly what destroys relationship.

By contrast, he accepts the free gestures of gratitude because they create no hierarchies and demand no submission. They are responses to life, not instruments of domination. Moreover, Yeshua constantly redirects the glory from himself toward the Father. "I do not seek my own glory" (John 8:50). "My teaching is not my own" (John 7:16). When he is called "good," he sends the goodness back to God (Mark 10:18).

These statements would be contradictory if Yeshua had wanted his own person to be idolized. They become coherent once we understand that the gratitude he receives does not stop at him. The gratitude passes through him toward God. In the Gospels, the "worship" Yeshua receives is, in essence, thanksgiving made flesh and an acknowledgment of the truth that has been seen. Yeshua accepts it because to refuse it would mean denying the truth of a person's experience.

But he does not ask for it and does not organize it, precisely in order to prevent idolization. The conclusion becomes clear: worship, in that time, was often the language of total gratitude, not the cult of an idol. Yeshua accepts gratitude for life received, but refuses any form of worship that would turn gratitude into dependence. He does not gather worshipers. He receives the thanks of those who have seen.

## Why Yeshua Does Not Build a Religious Identity

Yeshua enters a deeply religious world, yet he constantly refuses to build a religious identity. Because identity is easier than change. He offers no badges of belonging, imposes no formulas, and creates no clear lines between "our people" and "the others." This absence is not an oversight; it is intention. Yeshua sees that religion, when it becomes an identity, replaces transformation with belonging.

Religious identity offers security: you know who you are, where you belong, who is right. But this security comes at a cost: rigidity, exclusion, fear of error. This is why Yeshua harshly criticizes not a lack of belief but religious certainty. "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees" (Matthew 23) is not an attack on Scripture. It is an attack on the use of Scripture as identity armor. Yeshua constantly shifts the center of gravity from "who you are" to "how you are." He does not ask what you believe. He asks how you love. He does not ask where you are going, but what you see. "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the Kingdom" (Matthew 7:21). Verbal identity guarantees nothing. The only criterion is a living relationship with the truth. By refusing to build a religious identity, Yeshua keeps the message open. The Kingdom cannot be confiscated by any one group. This is why he praises the faith of a Roman (Matthew 8:10), speaks with Samaritans (John 4), and constantly breaks through religious boundaries.

Truth, for Yeshua, does not belong to an identity. It is recognized, not possessed.

## Why Does Yeshua Form Apostles?

Yeshua does not form apostles in order to create a religious caste. The apostle is not a theologian but a living witness. "You will be my witnesses" (Acts 1:8). To be a witness does not mean to explain. It means to show. The apostles are sent not to defend a doctrine. They are sent to live what they have seen. "What we have heard, what we have seen with our own eyes… this we proclaim to you" (1 John 1:1–3). The formation of the apostles is necessary for continuity, but not for rigid institutionalization.

Yeshua does not give them a manual. He gives them the Spirit. "The Spirit will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13).

## Why Yeshua Chose Twelve Disciples

The choice of the twelve disciples is often read superficially, as an administrative gesture: Yeshua is forming a "group," a nucleus of followers, a first structure of the Church. But such a reading projects back onto Yeshua an institutional intention that the Gospels do not support. Yeshua does not act as the founder of an organization, nor as a strategist of religious expansion.

The choice of the twelve is symbolic, pedagogical, and existential before it is functional. It says more about how Yeshua sees reality than about how he intends to build a religion. Yeshua does not choose twelve because twelve were enough. Nor because he wanted an "optimized" number. The number twelve is loaded with meaning in the Jewish imagination: the twelve tribes of Israel (Genesis 35:22–26). Through this choice, Yeshua does not announce a new institution that replaces Israel, but a reconfiguration of the meaning of Israel. He gathers around himself not a moral or intellectual elite, but a miniature of ordinary humanity: fishermen, tax collectors, impulsive men, fearful men, indecisive men. Their selection is not meritocratic. It is intentionally imperfect.

Through the twelve, Yeshua says without saying: the Kingdom of God is not built on exceptions; it is made of real people. If he had chosen only "good" people, the message would have been falsified. If he had chosen only the learned, the message would have become elitist. But Yeshua chooses people who have nothing spectacular about them except a willingness to go after him, even without fully understanding where the road leads (Mark 1:16–20).

This willingness, not competence, is the criterion. The choice of the twelve is not about control. It is about relationship. Yeshua does not instruct them through manuals, but through presence. "He appointed twelve so that they might be with him" (Mark 3:14). This phrasing is essential. The primary purpose is not "to do something." It is "to be with him." Transformation is not transmitted as information. It is a contagion of consciousness. The disciples do not become different because they receive a correct doctrine.

They become different people because they live alongside a man who does not live out of fear, status, or power. At the same time, Yeshua chooses twelve in order to show that the Kingdom is not an individual project. Though his message is profoundly inward, it is not solipsistic. The Kingdom is seen between people, not in isolation. The twelve are not called to save themselves separately, but to learn together what it means to be a community that does not run on hierarchies of domination.

This is why Yeshua corrects them constantly whenever they try to introduce the logic of power: "Whoever wants to be first must be the servant of all" (Mark 9:35). Choosing a group creates the context in which these tensions can surface and be transformed. It is significant that among the twelve is also Judas. If Yeshua had been after efficiency or image, this choice would have been a failure.

But it is precisely the inclusion of Judas that shows Yeshua does not build a "clean" group. He does not eliminate the risk. He does not protect himself from betrayal through selection. This suggests that the Kingdom of God is not based on excluding those who might fail. It is based on the embrace of vulnerability. A group without risk would be a simulation of love, not real love. The twelve are also a prophetic act. Through them, Yeshua announces that God does not give up on the world.

He reconfigures it from within. Twelve ordinary men become the sign that transformation does not come through imposition from above, but through slow fermentation. Just as the Kingdom is compared to the leaven that works through the whole batch of dough (Matthew 13:33), the twelve are sufficient not to dominate, but to begin a process that cannot be controlled. It is important to notice that Yeshua does not grant the twelve a permanent special status.

When they argue among themselves about who is greatest, he does not clarify hierarchies; on the contrary, he undermines them (Luke 22:24–27). When Peter errs, he is rebuked publicly (Mark 8:33). When the disciples do not understand, Yeshua does not replace them with others more "capable." This shows that their selection is not about performance but about process. The Kingdom is not a selection of the best, but a road of transformation for those who are available.

The choice of the twelve is also a silent answer to the messianic temptation of power. A political Messiah would have gathered an army. A religious Messiah would have gathered a moral elite. Yeshua gathers a small, fragile, often confused group in order to show that real change does not come through force or through purity. Real change comes through relationship. Twelve men who learn to stop defending themselves from one another are already a greater revolution than any conquest.

In the end, Yeshua chooses twelve not in order to build a religion, but in order to embody a way of being that can be transmitted only through a life lived together. They are not "founders of the Church" in the administrative sense, but witnesses to a reality that cannot be reduced to structure. After Yeshua's death, the community will become an institution.

But before the institution, there was a circle of people who learned, slowly and imperfectly, what it means to live without fear in the presence of God.

## Why Sacrifice Nevertheless Appears in Christianity

After Yeshua's death, the communities that remained behind him faced a reality that was hard to integrate. The violent execution of a man perceived as righteous, a healer, a bearer of life created a deep psychological and spiritual rupture. It was not only about the loss of a teacher, but about the collapse of an expectation. If God was with him, how could it end this way?

In the face of this shock, the communities felt the need to explain, to make sense, and to integrate the event into a familiar structure. In this context, the language of sacrifice became a bridge to understanding. It offered meaning where there was trauma, coherence where there was confusion, and continuity with the Jewish tradition in which sacrifice was already a familiar theological language.

Moreover, it offered a stable juridical framework, capable of translating Yeshua's death into a logic of compensation and balance. It was not necessarily a new revelation, but a translation of an emotionally unbearable event into an already existing system of meaning. It is essential, however, to notice something that appears consistently in the Gospel accounts. Yeshua never explains his death in terms of a payment demanded by God.

He does not speak of a divine wrath that must be satisfied, nor of a cosmic debt that must be settled. He speaks of giving, of freedom, and of acceptance. "No one takes my life from me, but I lay it down of my own accord. I have authority to lay it down, and authority to take it up again" (John 10:18). The language is one of freedom, not of juridical constraint. The cross, in this light, is not God's accounting plan.

It is the consequence of the meeting between unconditional love and a religious and political system that cannot tolerate it. Yeshua does not die because God demands blood. Yeshua dies because a love without fear, without negotiation, and without violence becomes unbearable to structures built on control, guilt, and power. "The light has come into the world, but people loved the darkness rather than the light" (John 3:19).

The cross does not say something about God's need for sacrifice, but about the world's inability to receive the truth without destroying it. During his life, Yeshua forgives without appealing to sacrifice. Forgiveness appears before the cross and independently of it. "Son, your sins are forgiven" (Mark 2:5). There is no condition, no payment, no invocation of a future sacrifice. Forgiveness is not postponed until a juridical event; it is offered in direct encounter.

This shows clearly that, for Yeshua, the relationship between humanity and God is not broken, only unperceived. In this vision, sin is not a cosmic debt that must be settled, but a state of wandering, of not-seeing, of fear. "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34). Ignorance, not juridical guilt, is the central problem. This is why transformation does not come from payment, but from encounter. Not from the satisfaction of a divine demand.

Rather, from the awakening of the human being to truth. Forgiveness, in Yeshua's vision, is not a mechanism that changes God's attitude toward humanity. God is never presented as an offended judge who must be persuaded to forgive. Forgiveness is a recognition. It does not change God's decision, but it changes the human being's position in relation to truth. "The Kingdom of God is near" (Mark 1:15). The nearness is not the result of an accepted sacrifice.

It is the result of an awareness. So the language of sacrifice that appears later in Christianity does not describe the way God works. It is, rather, the way the communities tried to understand a traumatic loss. It is a theology of meaning after the event, not a revelation of divine intent. In Yeshua, forgiveness precedes the cross, life precedes the explanation, and relationship precedes doctrine. The cross is not the condition of God's love.

The cross is the proof that this love goes all the way to the end, even when it is rejected.

## The Demons Yeshua Casts Out

The accounts of "devils" and "demons" are among the hardest to read with the modern mind, but also among the most revealing of the difference in perspective between Yeshua, Paul, and the Christianity that came after them. The language seems the same, yet the reality it points to is profoundly different.

Yeshua uses the vocabulary of his era to describe inner states of captivity and disintegration, while, gradually, demons would become in later theology external, autonomous entities, part of a logic of "spiritual warfare." In the Gospels, Yeshua casts "unclean spirits" out of people who present very concrete symptoms: loss of identity—"My name is Legion" (Mark 5:9)—compulsive and self-destructive behavior—"night and day he would cry out and cut himself with stones" (Mark 5:5)—extreme social isolation, the inability to speak—"they brought to him a mute man who was demon-possessed" (Matthew 9:32)—violent fits that throw the body into convulsions (Mark 9:17–27).

Yeshua is not interested in the metaphysical origin of demons, but in their effect on the person. He does not explain what demons are; he recognizes them by what they produce: fragmentation, the loss of freedom, dehumanization. The demonic, in Yeshua, is not a "character" but a state in which a person is no longer himself. It is significant that, in these encounters, Yeshua always brings the person back into consciousness and identity. He asks, "What is your name?" (Mark 5:9).

He does not converse with the demon as an entity, but with the human being who has become lost in that state. After the deliverance, the criterion of healing is not victory over an invisible force, but the fact that the man is "clothed and in his right mind" (Mark 5:15). The exorcism is not a spectacle but a reintegration. On several occasions, the demons "recognize" Yeshua: "I know who you are—the Holy One of God" (Mark 1:24). Read literally, the scene seems mythological.

Read in Yeshua's logic, it takes on a deep symbolic meaning: states of disintegration are the first to react to truth. Truth is destabilizing for any structure built on fear and falsehood. Yeshua's response is simple and free of drama: "Be silent, and come out of him!" (Mark 1:25). There is no ritual, no elaborate invocation. Clear presence and spoken truth dissolve the confusion.

An essential fact is that Yeshua never builds a demonology. He does not classify spirits, does not speak of demonic hierarchies, does not describe strategies of evil. Demons do not become a theological subject but a therapeutic context. The attention stays on the person, not on the force that captured him. When he says, "If I cast out demons by the finger of God, then the Kingdom of God has come upon you" (Luke 11:20), the emphasis does not fall on the demons; it falls on the Kingdom—that is, on restored life, on the person brought back to himself.

In Paul, the language visibly changes. He speaks of "rulers," "authorities," "the powers of darkness" (Ephesians 6:12). The demonic takes on a cosmic and structural dimension. It is no longer just an inner state of the individual, but a system of forces operating upon the world. From this comes an important shift: the problem is no longer the healing of the person. For Paul, the problem is spiritual warfare. The human being becomes a participant in an invisible conflict.

Paul urges resistance, the "armor of God," continual vigilance (Ephesians 6:13–17). The demonic is no longer dissolved through presence. In Paul, the demonic must be combated through faith and belonging. Later Christianity would carry this direction even further. Demons would be understood as autonomous personal entities, directly responsible for illness, sin, and even a person's thoughts. Evil is moved almost entirely outward.

The demonic becomes the preferred explanation for everything that is psychologically disturbing, hard to integrate inwardly, or socially uncomfortable. Exorcism becomes ritual, technique, hierarchy. Fear becomes institutionalized. The person is no longer merely wounded or fragmented; he is possessed. Inner responsibility is often displaced outward.

This vision produces concrete effects: the stigmatization of mental suffering, the avoidance of introspection, the externalization of blame, and the consolidation of the religious authority of those who "have power over demons." This is exactly the direction Yeshua never follows. The difference in perspective becomes clear. In Yeshua, demons are states of disintegration; the solution is presence and truth; healing means becoming whole; the person remains at the center.

In Paul, demons become cosmic powers; the solution is spiritual warfare and resistance; the person becomes part of a larger conflict. In later Christianity, demons are autonomous entities; the solution becomes ritual and authority; fear is consolidated, and the person ends up a battlefield. Yeshua did not come to create a mythology of demons. He came to heal disintegrated people. For him, the demonic is not something to be studied. It is something to be dissolved through truth.

Wherever a person recovers his mind, his voice, his freedom, and his dignity, there the demon has gone out—whatever name you might have given it.

## Who Satan Is for Yeshua

The figure of Satan becomes much clearer when it is read from within Yeshua's message, rather than through the lens of late demonology. For Yeshua, Satan is not a cosmic anti-God, nor a character equal in power to God. Satan is the inner adversary of truth—a logic of separation that today can be described very precisely through the notion of the ego.

Only later, in Paul and in institutional Christianity, would Satan be projected outward, becoming a cosmic, personalized, feared entity. The word "satan" means, in its original sense, "adversary" or "accuser." In Yeshua, Satan does not appear as a mythological being described in cosmic detail, but as the voice that opposes lived truth.

This voice does not come from outside the person, but from an inner structure built on fear, separation, and self-protection—what, in contemporary language, we call the ego. Yeshua does not develop a demonology. All he does is expose a dynamic of consciousness. In the temptations in the wilderness (Matthew 4:1–11), Satan does not invite Yeshua to crude immorality.

He invites him to the classic moves of the ego: the assertion of the self through power ("tell these stones to become bread"), the avoidance of vulnerability by guaranteeing safety ("throw yourself down"), and dominance over others ("I will give you all the kingdoms of the world"). These are the fundamental reflexes of the separate self: survival, validation, and control. Satan, for Yeshua, is not "something" external, but the inner logic that says: defend yourself, prove yourself, control.

Yeshua does not negotiate with this voice, does not dramatize it, and does not turn it into a spectacular conflict. He sees it and refuses it. This is why the temptation is not a battle but rather an act of clarity.

## "The Ruler of This World Has No Hold on Me." Zero Ego

The statement in John 14:30 is the key to this understanding: "The ruler of this world is coming. He has no hold on me." "The ruler of this world" does not designate here a cosmic demon, but the logic of a world built on the ego: the fear of death, defensive identity, competition, justified violence, and the need for superiority. When Yeshua says that this power "has no hold on me," he is affirming that there is no point of attachment.

There is no fear, no desire for self-assertion, no need for control, and no identity separate from the Father. In modern language, we might say that Yeshua has no functional ego. Not because he has no personality, but because his identity is not built on separation. This is why the adversary cannot operate. Where there is no fear, there is no grip. Yeshua says clearly, "He is a liar and the father of lies" (John 8:44).

The fundamental lie is not doctrinal but existential. It is the idea that a person is separate from God, that he must defend himself in order to exist, and that he is alone in a hostile world. This is the narrative of the ego. The ego says: you are not safe, you are not enough, you must defend yourself, others are a threat. For Yeshua, Satan is this false story about who we are. This is why his solution is not the exorcism of an entity.

The solution is the truth that dissolves the lie. "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32). When the lie falls, the adversary falls. There is no need for battle; clarity is enough. The same unity reappears when Yeshua says "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30) and "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9). These words do not describe superiority, but the absence of division. In Yeshua there is no inner conflict, no rupture, no cracks through which fear or falsehood might enter. Evil has nowhere to get in because there are no unintegrated spaces. Yeshua's anthropology starts here: a person can become whole. "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matthew 5:8) does not describe moral perfection but a heart without inner cracks.

"The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21) shows that this wholeness is not external but internal. "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32) does not mean the freedom to abstain, but the freedom to no longer carry within you anything that can be pressed. Purity of heart is the absence of vulnerable points through which fear, shame, and guilt can be activated. When we come to Paul, the language changes radically.

Paul does not describe the human being as an integrated being but as a divided one: "I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I do" (Romans 7:19). "I see in my members another law, waging war against the law of my mind" (Romans 7:23). "Walk by the Spirit, and you will not gratify the desires of the flesh" (Galatians 5:16). If in Yeshua evil "has no hold" on the person, in Paul evil is present in the person and must be kept under control. Yeshua describes healing; Paul describes the struggle.

Yeshua shows the person who can become whole, in whom evil no longer finds leverage. Paul shows the person who lives aware of his division and learns to manage it. In Yeshua, presence and clarity predominate. In Paul, discipline and vigilance predominate. In Yeshua we see healing; in Paul we see the crucifixion of the flesh. Yeshua nowhere says that he struggles with himself; Paul states this explicitly.

This is why Yeshua can say "he has no hold on me," whereas Paul could not utter this sentence without feeling it as spiritual pride. Paul says, "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost" (1 Timothy 1:15). He remains in the posture of a person in process, in tension, in continual vigilance. Yeshua speaks from the posture of a person who has reached inner unity. We see here two different ways of not reacting to evil.

Yeshua does not react because there is nothing in him to be activated: "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34). Paul does not react because he restrains himself: "I discipline my body and keep it under control" (1 Corinthians 9:27). One no longer has any "buttons." The other learns not to press them. "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21) and "Fight the good fight" (1 Timothy 6:12), placed side by side, show two different stages of the human being.

Yeshua shows what a person looks like when there are no more cracks. Paul shows how a person lives while the cracks still exist. "The ruler of this world has no hold on me" thus becomes one of the most powerful definitions of the integrated human being in the New Testament. It is not a religious sentence but one about the inner structure of the healed person. In Yeshua we see the possibility of wholeness. In Paul we see the reality of the struggle.

Both are true, but they speak of different stages of the same human journey.

## "Demonic"—Spiritual Projection or Misunderstood Suffering?

A great deal of what is called "demonic" today in Christian discourse has nothing to do with external spiritual forces, but with internal psychological processes that we do not know how to recognize and contain. Anxiety, trauma, dissociation, repressed anger, shame, insecure attachments, and defense mechanisms become, in the absence of an inner language, realities translated religiously. Not because they are demonic in themselves, but because they are uncomfortable and hard to understand. A person who does not know his own inner life needs simple explanations for complex phenomena, and what cannot be processed psychologically is externalized spiritually. The "demonic" becomes a symbolic container for what we do not know how to manage within ourselves. In the Gospels, Yeshua does not treat human suffering as spiritual guilt.

He does not tell the anxious person that he is possessed because he is afraid, he does not declare the angry person demonic because he explodes, and he does not suspect the confused person because he asks questions. On the contrary, he draws near, listens, and heals, and his statement "It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick" (Matthew 9:12) radically shifts the frame: the problem is neither moral nor demonic. The problem is curable. Illness is not a stigma. Illness is a context for compassion.

In modern Christianity, by contrast, there often appears a tendency to over-spiritualize everything that does not align with the dominant theology. Intense emotions become "spirits," doubt becomes "temptation," a difference of opinion becomes "deception," a question becomes "rebellion," and freedom of thought is suspected as a "spiritual attack." This tendency does not arise from malice.

It arises from fear—the fear of ambiguity, the fear of losing control, the fear of destabilizing a system of meaning that offers security. "There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear, because fear has to do with punishment" (1 John 4:18). Where fear leads, discernment disappears. Psychologically speaking, the "demonic" label often functions as a defense mechanism. If I call something demonic, I am no longer obliged to understand it. I no longer have to listen. I no longer have to enter into dialogue. The case is closed by the label. This is exactly the dynamic Yeshua confronts during his life. When the Pharisees, the religious authority of the time, cannot integrate the way he heals, loves, and speaks with freedom, they resort to the harshest possible label: "He casts out demons by the prince of demons" (Matthew 12:24). This is not a theological statement but a strategy of invalidation.

If the fruits cannot be denied, the person is discredited. Yeshua's response is disarmingly clear: "How can Satan cast out Satan?" (Mark 3:23). That is: look at the effects. Healing does not come from the demonic, deliverance does not come from darkness, life is not born from death. He moves the discussion from the label to reality, from interpretation to fruits.

Moreover, Yeshua warns of a danger far deeper than possession: the confusion of light with darkness, of healing with evil. The prophetic tradition he continues had already warned: "Woe to those who call evil good and good evil" (Isaiah 5:20). In the context of the Gospels, this confusion is called "blasphemy against the Spirit": "Blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven" (Matthew 12:31).

Not because God refuses forgiveness, but because the closed heart can no longer recognize the truth. When any light is perceived as a threat, the space for healing disappears. If Yeshua were to hear a Christian today demonizing a different opinion, a sincere question, or a symbolic interpretation, he would not begin a theological debate.

He would ask the same uncomfortable questions he asked then: "Why do you see the speck in your brother's eye, but do not notice the log in your own eye?" (Matthew 7:3). And he would continue with the same unmasking of the impeccable exterior and the unexamined interior: "You clean the outside of the cup, but inside you are full of greed and self-indulgence" (Matthew 23:25). The problem is not the other person's opinion, but your inner reaction to it.

For Yeshua, discernment is not made according to doctrinal conformity but according to the fruits of life. "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:16) remains the criterion. Where there is more life, more inner freedom, more compassion and lived truth, it is not demonic, even if it does not carry your religious language.

And conversely, where there is fear, rigidity, dehumanization, and the need for control, the demonic—in the sense of separation—is already present, even if the language is impeccably religious. In the end, Yeshua would not condemn psychology, the question, or the difference. He would condemn the use of God as a weapon. He would stand with the one demonized by the community, not with those who demonize. For, as he says clearly: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13).

True liberation does not come from spiritual labels. It comes from consciousness, presence, and love.

## Is There Absolute Evil?

For Yeshua, evil is neither a list of deeds nor a label placed on people. He does not operate with external moral classifications, but with a deep gaze upon the heart. Evil, in his language, is an inner state that produces blindness, separation, and a lack of love. This is why he says: "The good person brings good things out of the good treasure of his heart, and the evil person brings evil things out of the evil treasure of his heart" (Luke 6:45), and explains clearly: "Out of the heart come evil thoughts" (Matthew 15:19).

Evil is not a person's identity, but his disconnection from the source of life. It is not a condemnation of the person, but the description of a state in which the heart no longer sees. Yeshua's harshest words are addressed not to those society considers "sinners," but to those convinced that they see clearly and who judge in the name of that certainty. "If you were blind, you would have no sin; but now that you say, 'We see,' your sin remains" (John 9:41).

Elsewhere, he calls them without hesitation "blind guides" (Matthew 15:14). The supreme evil, in this light, is not the mistake, but the moral arrogance that justifies a lack of love. Blindness that believes itself to be light becomes, for Yeshua, one of the deepest forms of evil, because it can no longer be corrected. Yeshua names evil also where religion becomes an instrument of oppression, shaming, and control.

He says of the religious leaders of his time: "You shut the Kingdom of Heaven in people's faces" (Matthew 23:13). And against this attitude he sets a simple, decisive principle: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13). Evil is not the law itself, but the use of the law against life. When the rule replaces compassion, when the form replaces the heart, evil installs itself under the appearance of righteousness. Equally telling is what Yeshua does not call evil.

He does not call evil those who are different, those who are socially stigmatized, those who are searching. He says clearly: "I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners" (Luke 5:32). Before the woman caught in adultery, he shifts the attention from guilt to conscience: "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone" (John 8:7). For Yeshua, evil is not the fall. For him it is the refusal to see and to love. Closing oneself off from the truth is the central problem. Not the mistake.

In the Apostle Paul, the description of evil takes on a different nuance. He speaks of evil as an active force in conflict with the human being's desire to do good. "I find this law at work: when I want to do good, evil lies close at hand" (Romans 7:21). Paul describes this tension with honesty: "I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want" (Romans 7:19).

The solution, in his vision, is radical: "Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh" (Galatians 5:24). The essential difference is that Yeshua sees evil as the absence of lived love, while Paul sees it as a power that demands discipline, struggle, and constant grace. In contemporary Christianity, evil is often externalized and simplified. It becomes different behavior, "wrong" opinion, "worldly" belonging.

The label replaces discernment, and fear justifies itself religiously. Exclusion comes to seem a virtue. It is precisely here that Yeshua warns: "Why do you see the speck in your brother's eye and not notice the log in your own?" (Matthew 7:3). Moral projection—the tendency to see evil only in others—becomes the sign that evil is already at work within. Taken together, three ways of defining evil emerge. For Yeshua, evil is the closed heart that believes itself to be light.

For Paul, evil is the flesh in permanent conflict with grace. For today's Christianity, evil often ends up being the other person. But Yeshua again moves the question inward: from what source does your life flow? His criterion remains the same: "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:16). Where the fruits are fear, rigidity, and a lack of compassion, evil is already present, even if the language is religious.

Where the fruits are life, freedom, and love, evil loses its power, even if the forms are not the ones expected.

## What It Means "Not to Test God"

The expression "you shall not test God" is one of the most frequently quoted and, at the same time, one of the most misunderstood of biblical formulas. It appears on Yeshua's lips in an extremely precise context and will later be taken up by Paul and by institutional Christianity in very different registers. The words remain the same, but their meaning shifts profoundly. For Yeshua, this expression appears in the episode of the temptation in the wilderness.

When he is challenged to throw himself down from the pinnacle of the temple in order to force divine intervention, he answers simply: "You shall not put the Lord your God to the test" (Matthew 4:7; Deuteronomy 6:16). Yeshua does not refuse faith here; he refuses the manipulation of God. To test God means to demand spectacular proofs in order to quiet your fear or validate your ego, to turn the relationship with the Father into a test, an experiment, a transaction.

Yeshua refuses to use God in order to prove his identity, legitimize his mission, or eliminate his human vulnerability. This attitude is coherent with his entire message. He constantly refuses the signs demanded out of distrust: "An evil and adulterous generation seeks a sign" (Matthew 12:39). For Yeshua, the demand for signs is not a sincere desire for truth, but a desire for control.

To test God means to demand absolute security before living, to demand miracles in order to avoid taking on life, to demand protection without responsibility. This is why he does not throw himself down. He walks. He walks into insecurity, into risk, into freedom. At the same time, this expression is, in Yeshua, a refusal of religious magic. Magic says: if I do X, God is obliged to do Y. Yeshua rejects this logic.

Prayer is not manipulation, faith is not a technique, and God is not a safety mechanism. "Your Father knows what you need before you ask him" (Matthew 6:8). To test God would be to believe that he must be persuaded or activated in order to be Father. In Paul, the expression begins to take on a different register. He recalls the episode from the wilderness and says: "We should not test Christ, as some of them did, and were destroyed" (1 Corinthians 10:9).

Here the emphasis shifts. For Paul, to test God means to grumble, to rebel, to live in disobedience to the divine order. The temptation is no longer the demand for guarantees, but the breaking of the established framework. God begins to be seen more as Lawgiver than as Father, and faith increasingly takes the form of obedience. This shift is subtle, but decisive. In Yeshua, the testing is tied to distrust; in Paul, it becomes tied to disobedience. The emphasis moves from living relationship to moral order. "These things happened to them as examples and were written down for our instruction" (1 Corinthians 10:11). Faith begins to be associated with communal discipline and with the fear of consequences. In later Christianity, the expression comes to be used in a way even further removed from its original meaning.

"Do not test God" becomes a mechanism for limiting initiative, the question, and risk. It comes to mean: do not step out of line, do not ask uncomfortable questions, do not dare too much, do not expect radical change. Paradoxically, the warning against distrust becomes a justification for passivity. Fear is repackaged as spiritual prudence, and the absence of movement is presented as obedience. This use would be foreign to Yeshua.

He says exactly the opposite: "Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you" (Matthew 7:7). For Yeshua, the problem is not that a person asks too much. It is that he asks out of fear, out of a desire for guarantees. When the asking comes from trust, it is not testing but the expression of a living relationship. "Do not be anxious about tomorrow" (Matthew 6:34) does not encourage passivity but frees a person from the fear that blocks life. The difference in perspective becomes clear.

In Yeshua, to test God means to demand proofs instead of trust, and to make love conditional on demonstrable protection. In Paul, it increasingly means to break an established moral framework. In later Christianity, it comes to mean stepping out of line. Where Yeshua calls to maturity without guarantees, the later systems demand conformity without question. For Yeshua, "you shall not test God" does not mean do not dare.

It means do not make the relationship conditional on safety. It does not mean do not take risks, but do not demand certainty before living. The real testing of God is not human courage. It is the refusal to live without guarantees.

## Listening vs. Obedience

The word "obedience" seems simple, but in the biblical tradition it can mean almost opposite things, depending on who uses it and from what inner center he speaks. In religious language, "obedience" often comes to be confused with submission: do what you are told, do not ask, comply. In reality, in Yeshua obedience is not blind submission.

Obedience is a form of awakening and alignment. In Paul it becomes a soteriological and communal category, tied to the order of salvation and the stability of the Church; and in institutional Christianity, the meaning can rigidify into conformity with norms, hierarchies, and dogmas. The same word, three different universes—and this is precisely why the same call can produce either freedom or fear.

In Yeshua, obedience is not preached as a virtue in itself and is never separated from truth, love, and life. He does not seek "well-behaved" people in the sense of the submissive. Yeshua seeks people who are alive, permeable to reality. This is why his language is relational and inward: "Remain in me" (John 15:4) is not a command of discipline but rather an invitation to continuity of heart, to a presence that does not break.

"Learn from me" (Matthew 11:29) does not ask for external imitation. It asks for a change of perspective: to learn a way of being. And when he says, "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Mark 4:9), he moves listening from the ear to the heart: it is not about receiving information, but about letting the truth penetrate to the point where it changes your life. When Yeshua speaks of the Father's will, he does not describe it as a set of arbitrary orders but as a reality that nourishes. "My food is to do the will of the one who sent me" (John 4:34) shows that obedience, in him, is not a sacrifice against life but the very source of life. To do the Father's will does not mean to execute commands in order to avoid punishment, but to live from a truth that sustains your being, as food sustains the body. This is why, when Yeshua says, "If you love me, you will keep my commandments" (John 14:15), the order is decisive: love precedes obedience.

Obedience is not the condition of love but its fruit. You do not conform in order to be loved; you are loved, and out of that security your life begins to align. Inevitably, this understanding comes into conflict with obedience understood as blind submission to the system. Yeshua explicitly dismantles the idea that obedience is the supreme value when he says: "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27).

Here he shows that any "obedience" that tramples life underfoot ceases to be obedience. If the rule becomes more important than the person, then the rule has become an idol, and obedience has become a form of moral blindness. This is why Yeshua heals on the Sabbath, touches the "unclean," forgives without ritual, and refuses to submit to interpretations that destroy the person in the name of God.

The spirit of this gesture would later be formulated by the apostles in a sentence that describes exactly Yeshua's line: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). In Yeshua's logic, true obedience can mean disobedience to the system when the system has lost its heart. When Yeshua speaks of his own obedience, his language remains that of freely assumed freedom, not of constraint.

"No one takes my life from me, but I lay it down" (John 10:18) means that his obedience is not a capture of the will but a voluntary giving. Even in Gethsemane, when he says "not what I will, but what you will" (Mark 14:36), we do not witness the annulment of the self but its integration: the human will is not crushed but brought into unity with the truth. Here the difference is seen: in Yeshua, obedience is not submission against conscience, but unity of conscience.

This is why he ties truth to freedom: "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32). The obedience that comes from truth does not enslave; it liberates. In Paul, however, the framework changes. Paul builds communities, defends a message, stabilizes a spiritual identity in a plural and often hostile world. In this context, obedience takes on a more structural and more soteriological function.

He can say that "through the obedience of the one, the many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19), and here the obedience of Christ becomes a representative, reparative act, with juridical weight in the economy of salvation. Obedience is no longer only inner alignment but also an element within a theological architecture: the disobedience of Adam, the obedience of Christ, the justification of the many.

In addition, Paul introduces the expression "the obedience of faith" (Romans 1:5), which ties obedience to adherence to a preached message and to communal cohesion. From here arise also the exhortations that fix obedience within the register of order: "submit to one another" (Ephesians 5:21) and "let every soul be subject to the governing authorities" (Romans 13:1). The emphasis shifts: discernment does not disappear, but, increasingly visible, the need for stability, discipline, and the protection of the community enters the scene.

Later Christianity would take up this shift and amplify it to the point of rigidification. Obedience often comes to be presented as the supreme virtue: submission to religious authority, respect for dogma, moral conformity, the acceptance of "this is how it's done." In practice, "submit to God's will" can come to mean "accept what you are told," even when it produces fear, shame, or self-annulment.

At this point, obedience decouples itself from truth and attaches itself to power: it is no longer an opening of the heart but a soothing of the system. The question becomes suspect, and discernment becomes dangerous, because it can break conformity. But it is precisely here that Yeshua's words return like a blade that cuts through the confusion: "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the Kingdom of Heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father" (Matthew 7:21).

To do the Father's will does not mean to repeat slogans and observe rituals, but to live out of love and truth—that is, out of that inner center Yeshua calls life. The difference between the three perspectives is seen clearly if we follow the effect of obedience. In Yeshua, obedience is an inner alignment with living truth, and its fruit is freedom: "If you remain in my word… you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:31–32).

In Paul, obedience becomes also a binding agent of salvation and of the community, tied to the message, to discipline, and to coherence. In institutional Christianity, obedience can become conformity and an instrument of control, and then it no longer produces life but fear; it no longer opens the person toward truth but closes him within a structure. Yeshua did not come to create obedient people, but whole people. Obedience, for him, is not the bowing of the head but the opening of the heart.

Wherever obedience produces life, truth, and love, it is from God. Wherever it produces fear, self-annulment, and inner silence, it is no longer obedience—it is mere submission.

## "If God Loves People, Why Does He Send So Many to Hell?"

Yeshua would never begin with hell. He would begin with God—and more precisely with the face of the Father. In the Gospels, Yeshua does not preach a God who hands out punishments, but a Father who seeks, waits, and heals. "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9) is the hermeneutical key to all his answers.

When Yeshua speaks of being lost, he does not describe it as an arbitrary decision of God, but as a state of wandering: the lost sheep, the prodigal son, the lost coin (Luke 15). In none of these parables does the Father send anyone away. The person leaves. The Father seeks. That is the perspective. If he were asked directly, "Why does God send people to hell?" Yeshua would probably answer by completely shifting the frame: God does not send. A person becomes lost when he refuses life.

"The light has come into the world, but people loved the darkness rather than the light" (John 3:19). Hell, in his language, is not a prison created by God, but the inner experience of separation from truth. Yeshua uses the term Gehenna not to describe a place of eternal torture, but to warn of the consequences of a life lived out of fear, hatred, and self-enclosure.

"The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21) implies also the reverse: separation is likewise inward. Hell is not where God sends you, but what the heart becomes when it refuses love. This is why Yeshua never says, "God will condemn you," but, "Do you want to be made well?" (John 5:6). He does not speak of punishment, but of a healing refused.

Judgment, for him, is not an external act but the truth that exposes the state of the heart: "The word that I have spoken will judge him" (John 12:48). For Yeshua, "hell" is not a purpose of God's, but the ultimate expression of a relational failure, the place where love is refused, not where it is withdrawn. The Father does not create beings in order to lose them, and his intention is not destruction, but life: "God does not desire the death of the sinner, but that he turn and live" (cf. Ezekiel 18:23, in continuity with the spirit of the Gospel). When Yeshua speaks of "weeping and gnashing of teeth," he does not describe the satisfaction of a punitive God, but the inner tension of a person who resists the truth and refuses healing. Hell is not a place to which God sends, but a state in which a person remains when he closes himself off from the light.

In Yeshua's logic, love is total but not coercive: God loves everyone, forces no one, and freedom—precisely because it is real—can refuse life without God ceasing to love. In Paul, the framework changes. Paul did not know Yeshua historically; he encountered him in a post-cross mystical experience. His language becomes more juridical, more eschatological, more oriented toward justice and justification.

Paul clearly affirms the universal love of God: "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). But he also introduces a framework of final judgment: "The wages of sin is death" (Romans 6:23). For Paul, the problem is not that God wants to punish, but that sin produces death—an ontological rupture.

Paul would say that God does not send people to hell out of a lack of love, but because justice and truth cannot be annulled. Grace is offered, but it can be refused. "God wants all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4), yet not all respond. Paul preserves an unresolved tension between God's universal desire and the reality of human refusal.

Unlike Yeshua, Paul speaks more clearly of eschatological consequences, of "wrath" as the reaction of divine justice to the destruction of life. But even here, the emphasis is not sadistic but moral-cosmic: the universe has an order, and breaking it has real effects. Contemporary Christianity, in many of its forms, has dramatically simplified the question.

Instead of keeping alive the tension between love, freedom, and truth, it has turned hell into a pedagogical instrument of fear. The standard answer has become: "God loves, but he is just; those who do not believe end up in hell." In this form, the emphasis falls not on healing but on belonging; not on transformation but on doctrinal correctness. Hell becomes a mechanism for controlling behavior and religious identity.

This leads to a paradoxical image: a God who loves but creates billions of people knowing that the majority will be eternally lost. This version is closer to a theology of fear than to the message of Yeshua. "There is no fear in love; perfect love casts out fear" (1 John 4:18). When hell becomes the center of the sermon, love ceases to be credible.

The difference in perspective becomes clear when we look at how the center of gravity of the relationship between God and humanity shifts. In Yeshua, God is named and experienced as Father, not as a tribunal, and "hell" appears not as a place administered from outside but as a state of inner separation, born of the refusal of truth and love; being lost is not a sentence but the missing of life, and salvation is first of all healing, a return to wholeness and to communion.

In Paul, the image grows tense: God remains loving but is also affirmed as just, and hell takes on the contour of a real consequence of sin; salvation is grace, but a grace that can be received or refused, and judgment exists as a serious reality, held nonetheless in a fragile balance with love.

In today's Christianity, this living tension often rigidifies into a scheme: God is perceived predominantly as a judge, hell becomes the final punishment, salvation is reduced to correct belonging and conformity, and fear becomes the main engine of the spiritual life. So, as the perspective shifts from relationship to rule, from healing to verdict, what is lost is precisely the heart of the original message: the call to a life lived out of love, not out of fear.

## Why Is There Suffering, If God Is Good?

Yeshua explicitly rejects the idea that suffering is a direct punishment for sin. When he is asked about the man born blind, his reaction is clear: "Neither this man sinned, nor his parents" (John 9:3). Suffering is not read as guilt, nor as moral failure. In a world where people seek causal explanations for pain, Yeshua refuses moralizing. He unties suffering from culpability and places it in a space of mystery, not of judgment.

When a public tragedy is brought up—the tower of Siloam that fell on people—Yeshua again refuses the punitive interpretation: "Do you think they were worse sinners than others? No" (Luke 13:4–5). Suffering is not proof that God chose to strike someone. For Yeshua, tragedy is not a divine message but a human context. He does not explain why it happened; he calls to awakening: not to live more correctly out of fear, but more fully out of truth.

Yeshua's central answer is this: God is not the author of suffering, but the present one within it. He says: "Blessed are those who mourn" (Matthew 5:4), not because mourning is good, but because God is there. The blessedness does not come from the pain, but from the fact that the pain is not abandoned. God does not stand at a distance explaining; he draws near, comforting. Yeshua does not offer a theoretical answer but an embodied one. When he sees suffering, he does not preach; he weeps.

"Yeshua wept" (John 11:35). His tears do not explain Lazarus's death, but they reveal God: a God who is not indifferent, not cold, not above pain. In that moment, Yeshua does not defend God's goodness through arguments but shows it through compassion. For Yeshua, suffering is tied to the condition of a fragmented world, not to the will of the Father.

He speaks of "the world" as a space of fear, falsehood, and violence (John 16:33), but immediately adds: "Take courage, I have overcome the world." The victory does not consist in the immediate elimination of suffering, but in its inability to destroy love. Evil produces pain, but it does not have the last word. Yeshua moves the center from "why" to "how": not how to avoid suffering at any cost, but how not to lose our heart within it.

"Do not be afraid" (Matthew 10:31) is one of his most repeated calls, precisely because fear amplifies suffering more than the pain itself. Fear tears a person away from God; suffering, paradoxically, can become a place of encounter. The culmination of Yeshua's answer is not a discourse but the cross. There, God does not explain suffering—he takes it on.

"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46) is not a theological lesson but the total entry into the human experience of abandonment. In Yeshua, God does not stand above the pain but passes through it without closing himself off. This is why the cross does not justify suffering but disarms it: it shows that not even the greatest pain can annul love. In Yeshua's logic, God is good not because he has eliminated suffering, but because he does not leave us alone in it.

Evil exists, pain exists, death exists—but they are no longer spaces without God. "I am with you always" (Matthew 28:20) is the final answer, not an explanation but a presence. So Yeshua does not answer the question by saying why suffering exists, but by saying who God is in the midst of it.

And the answer is this: God is the Father who descends, not the judge who explains; the healer who remains, not the architect of pain; the love that does not leave, even when the world hurts.

## Yeshua and War: On the Invisible Root of Violence

If we were to ask Yeshua what he thinks of those who start wars, he probably would not begin with geopolitics, nor with military strategy, nor with collective guilt. He would begin with the heart. Because in his vision, war does not begin on the battlefield but within the human being. In Matthew 15:19, he says: "For out of the heart come evil thoughts, murders, adulteries…"—and this short sentence is, in fact, an X-ray of human history.

War is merely the macro-level manifestation of an unintegrated heart. For Yeshua, the problem is not the weapon. The problem is fear. The problem is attachment to power. The problem is identity built on separation. In John 18:36, when Pilate asks him about his kingdom, Yeshua answers: "My kingdom is not of this world. If it were of this world, my servants would fight…" This is not a denial of political reality, but a delimitation of paradigm.

The kingdom that defends itself by the sword belongs to fear. The kingdom that has no need of the sword belongs to trust. War begins when a leader or a people feels that its identity is threatened. When inner security is lacking, external control is sought. When collective esteem is fragile, territorial expansion is sought. When there is historical shame or humiliation, revenge is sought. The need behind it is not violence. It is security. It is validation. It is the fear of disappearing.

Yeshua would see in those who start wars not merely aggressors, but people profoundly separated from their own source. In Luke 23:34, on the cross, he says: "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing." This is not a moral excuse. It is a spiritual diagnosis. Ignorance—not informational, but existential—is the fuel of violence. In Mark 10:42–45, Yeshua says: "You know that those considered rulers of the nations lord it over them… But it shall not be so among you.

Rather, whoever wants to be great among you must be your servant." This is the inversion of the paradigm of power. In the logic of the world, power imposes itself. In the logic of the Kingdom, power gives itself away. Those who start wars are seeking, in essence, control. But control is a strategy of fear. It is the attempt to manage the unpredictable through domination. The need behind it is profoundly human: the desire for stability, for continuity, for recognition.

But when this need is not integrated inwardly, it projects itself outward and becomes expansionism. For Yeshua, true authority does not come from constraint but from alignment with the will of the Father. "Whoever takes the sword will perish by the sword" (Matthew 26:52). This is not a karmic threat but a law of consciousness: the energy you cultivate becomes the environment in which you will live.

In Matthew 5:9, Yeshua says: "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God." To be a peacemaker does not mean passivity. It means to restore unity. War is possible only when "the other" becomes dehumanized. When the image of God in a person is no longer seen. When difference becomes a threat. Yeshua breaks this logic when he says: "Love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44). Not because the enemy is right.

But because love disarms the mechanism of separation. To love the enemy means to refuse to confirm his identity as an enemy. He probably would not begin with condemnation but with a question—just as he does in John 8, when he brings the mirror into the consciousness of the accusers: "Let him who is without sin among you…" He would turn the gaze toward the heart.

He would perhaps tell them what he says in Mark 8:36: "What does it profit a person to gain the whole world and lose his soul?" Because a war won externally can mean inner loss. He would not deny geopolitical complexity, but he would insist that real transformation begins at the level of consciousness. Without the conversion of the heart, the cycle of violence repeats itself. In essence, people start wars because they want to protect something: territory, identity, resources, belief.

Behind these objects there is a deeper need—the need for belonging and continuity. But Yeshua offers a belonging that does not depend on borders: "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). When belonging is internal, there is no longer any need for external expansion in order to be validated. War begins in the heart that does not feel itself to be enough. In the heart that seeks security in control and worth in domination. Yeshua does not offer a military strategy. He offers an inner transmutation.

A shift from fear to trust, from competition to communion, from separation to unity. Not everyone will receive this message. But without this alchemy of the heart, history will continue to repeat the same lesson, in different forms. For Yeshua, the true victory is not over a people. It is over the fear in one's own heart.

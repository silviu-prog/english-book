# 13. Levels of Consciousness

## Yeshua's Imperatives and the Levels of Consciousness

One of the most interesting observations we can make when reading the Gospels is that Yeshua's imperatives are not simply moral commandments. They are usually read as ethical rules — things we should or should not do. But if we look at them closely within the context of Yeshua's whole message, another, far deeper perspective emerges: these imperatives seem to be addressed to different levels of consciousness.

In modern psychology and spirituality there are models that describe the evolution of human consciousness in stages. One of them is the scale of consciousness proposed by David R. Hawkins, where different emotional states — fear, desire, courage, love, or peace — correspond to different levels of the perception of reality. Seen through this lens, Yeshua's words take on a surprising coherence. They are not merely rules of behavior; they describe a gradual ascent of consciousness.

The first level at which Yeshua addresses people is the level of fear. In the ancient world, just as in the world today, fear dominated people's lives: the fear of want, the fear of the future, the fear of death, the fear of divine judgment. That is why one of the most frequently repeated imperatives in the Gospels is simple and radical: "Do not be afraid." In Matthew 10:31 he says: "Do not be afraid; you are worth more than many sparrows." In the same spirit, in the Sermon on the Mount he says: "Do not worry about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink… nor about your body, what you will wear" (Matthew 6:25).

And a few verses later he adds: "Do not worry about tomorrow" (Matthew 6:34). In these words it is clear that the first thing Yeshua does is to calm the human nervous system. Consciousness cannot evolve while it is dominated by fear. Once fear begins to settle, Yeshua shifts the attention toward inner responsibility.

This is why one of his first public statements is: "Repent, for the Kingdom of Heaven is near" (Matthew 4:17). In modern language, this is not merely a call to moral guilt but an invitation to a change of perspective. To repent literally means to change your mind, to see things differently. In the same direction he tells the woman caught in adultery: "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" (John 8:11).

Here it is no longer a matter of fear but of the awakening of personal consciousness. The human being is called to become self-aware. Another step in this transformation is the dissolution of judgment. The moral ego feeds on comparison and condemnation. That is why Yeshua says clearly: "Do not judge, so that you will not be judged" (Matthew 7:1). Immediately after this he adds a memorable image: "Why do you see the speck in your brother's eye and not the plank in your own?" (Matthew 7:3).

Here Yeshua attacks directly the psychological mechanism of projection. The person who judges others does not see his own shadows. Through this imperative, consciousness begins to turn from the exterior toward the interior. As judgment diminishes, room appears for mature relationships. In the Sermon on the Mount, Yeshua says: "If you bring your gift to the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar and go first to be reconciled with your brother" (Matthew 5:23–24).

This statement is extraordinary, because it places the relationship above religious ritual. In the same logic comes what is known as the golden rule: "Whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them" (Matthew 7:12). At this point consciousness begins to operate in empathy. But Yeshua does not stop here. After establishing the relational foundation, he introduces the principle of love.

In John 13:34 he says: "I give you a new commandment: that you love one another; as I have loved you, so you also are to love one another." Love is no longer merely an emotion or a preference. It becomes the structure of consciousness. The human being begins to see in the other not a rival or a stranger but an extension of his own humanity.

Then comes one of the most radical statements in the entire spiritual tradition: "Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). This imperative seems impossible to the ordinary mind. From the perspective of the ego, the enemy must be fought, not loved. But it is precisely here that the difference between morality and consciousness becomes visible.

Morality says: "Do no harm." Awakened consciousness says: "Respond with love." Finally, Yeshua arrives at imperatives that seem entirely paradoxical to ordinary logic. "Do not resist the evil one. And to the one who strikes you on the right cheek, turn the other also" (Matthew 5:39). Or the even more enigmatic statement: "Be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect" (Matthew 5:48). These words can no longer be interpreted as simple rules of behavior.

They describe a consciousness that no longer operates out of defensiveness or separation. The human being no longer reacts from the ego, because his identity is no longer centered on the ego. Taken together, all these imperatives form a very clear scale of inner transformation. First, fear is calmed. Then the person is called to responsibility. After that he is invited to relinquish judgment.

Then comes relational maturity, then love, then universal compassion, and, in the end, the transcendence of the ego. In this light, the Gospels can be read not as a collection of religious rules but as a map of the evolution of human consciousness. Yeshua does not seem interested in building a rigid theological system. He is interested in the transformation of the human being. That is why his imperatives are not merely commandments.

They are descriptions of what a person looks like when his consciousness awakens. That is why many of Yeshua's words seem impossible to the ordinary reader. Not because they are exaggerated, but because they are addressed to a consciousness that has not yet arrived there. In this sense, Yeshua's words are not merely a morality. They are an invitation to a profound inner mutation.

## What the Hierarchy of Sins Says About the Level of Consciousness

Contemporary Christianity has inherited mostly the lists, but not the discernment that gave rise to them. Thus, "the gravest sins" come to be those that violate visible norms: "nonconforming" sexuality, lifestyle, wrong opinions, belonging to the wrong groups. The sins of the heart — pride, cruelty, the absence of empathy — are often tolerated or even justified "in the name of truth." In this paradigm, sin is no longer what breaks the relationship but what breaks the rule.

Morality becomes a filter, and empathy disappears. The person is no longer met but analyzed. Judgment is swift, correction is cold, and love is conditioned on prior change. Paradoxically, the very sins that Yeshua considered the gravest — hypocrisy, judgment, the absence of mercy — are rarely named as such today. They are masked under "righteous indignation," "the defense of truth," or "faithfulness."

The way a tradition defines "grave sin" says more about its level of consciousness than about its declared morality. For Yeshua, the sin that truly matters is the closing of the heart — the refusal to see, to love, and to remain open to truth when it appears. "The light has come into the world, but people loved the darkness more" (John 3:19) does not describe a list of deeds but an inner stance.

Sin, for Yeshua, is the blindness that prevents the encounter. For the Apostle Paul, the emphasis shifts. Grave sin becomes deviation from the truth, understood as the central message that must be preserved and transmitted. Hence the language of warning and demarcation: "Even if we or an angel from heaven should preach another gospel…" (Galatians 1:8). The truth is protected in order to save the community. It is no longer merely a matter of the heart but of fidelity to a content.

In today's institutional Christianity, the shift goes even further. Grave sin often becomes the departure from the norm. What counts becomes visible conformity, observance of the framework, the avoidance of sanction. Sin moves from the interior to the exterior, from relationship to rule. "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me" (Matthew 15:8) remains the diagnosis that crosses the centuries. The essential difference of perspective is clear.

Yeshua seeks the healing of the human being. Paul seeks the protection of the truth. Christianity today often ends up seeking the control of behavior. As we descend from relationship to rule, sin becomes ever easier to identify and ever harder to heal. Where sin is defined without empathy, transformation stops.

Where truth is no longer lived but imposed, it ceases to liberate: "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32) does not function under a regime of coercion. And where judgment takes the place of presence, the gravest sin becomes invisible — because it is the very loss of love. "If I have no love, I am nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:2) remains the conclusion that reorders any hierarchy.

## "Take Up Your Cross"

For Yeshua, the expression "take up your cross" is not an invitation to passive suffering, nor the glorification of pain, nor a religious fatalism. The language is concrete, existential. The cross is not a sacred object but the experience through which the old way of living — out of fear, ego, and self-salvation — dies. "If anyone wants to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me" (Mark 8:34). The order is essential: first the denial of self, then the cross.

The cross is not something that happens to us from the outside but what remains when we relinquish identification with the false self — the image, the control, the manufactured securities. "Whoever loses his life for my sake and for the gospel will save it" (Mark 8:35). The life that is "lost" is not the biological one but the life built on defense, status, and fear. Yeshua does not say that everyone has the same cross. On the contrary, each one "takes up his cross." The cross is personal because the ego is personal.

For some, the cross may mean the loss of a relationship, a career, a social identity; for others, the confrontation with illness, limitation, failure. But not every suffering is a cross. The cross is suffering assumed in truth — not suffering that is avoided, dramatized, or used as an identity. A loss becomes a cross when you no longer flee from it through resentment, victimhood, or cynicism, but remain present and true in the midst of it.

"Blessed are the poor in spirit" (Matthew 5:3) — this poverty is the emptying of self, the stripping away of certainties, the acceptance of the fact that you are neither the center nor the master of life. Yeshua does not ask people to seek out their suffering. He himself does not seek the cross. In Gethsemane he avoids it if possible: "Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me" (Luke 22:42). This shows clearly that the cross is not the ideal.

The cross is what remains when the truth can no longer be avoided. And immediately he adds: "Yet not my will, but yours be done" (Luke 22:42). This is the heart of it: no longer to direct your life out of fear, even when it hurts; not to betray your truth for the sake of comfort. The cross is bound to following him, not to religious performance. To take up your cross means to live as he lived in a world that runs on fear, power, and image.

"If they persecuted me, they will persecute you also" (John 15:20). This is not divine punishment but the natural friction between truth and a world that prefers appearance. The cross is the consequence of living in truth. What, then, does resurrection mean for us? It does not come after you have "suffered enough" but after you no longer cling to the ego. Yeshua does not say "take up your cross in order to die," but in order to live: "I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly" (John 10:10).

Resurrection is not necessarily a change of circumstances but a change of the center from which you live. The "risen" person may remain within the same outer limits, but he is no longer defined by them. "Whoever believes in me, even if he has died, will live" (John 11:25). This life begins now: freedom from fear, resentment, and the need for self-salvation. For Yeshua, the cross without resurrection would be absurd, and resurrection without the cross — an illusion. The cross is the death of the lie about oneself.

Resurrection is the birth of true life. "Unless a grain of wheat dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). Not everyone will have the same cross, but all are called to the same passage: from a life lived out of fear to a life lived out of truth. Yeshua does not say "suffer," but "follow me." And following him passes through the cross, but it does not stop there. The cross is the passage. Resurrection is the life that begins.

## How Yeshua Would Want Us to See His Resurrection

For Yeshua, the resurrection does not appear as a theological demonstration to be accepted intellectually, but as a revelation of the life that cannot be destroyed. He does not tell his disciples "believe that I have risen so that you may be saved"; rather, he shows himself, speaks with them, eats together with them, and calls them by name. The resurrection, in his way, is an encounter, not an argument. That is why his first words after the resurrection are simple: "Peace be with you" (John 20:19).

No explanations of what happened follow, but the healing of fear. The disciples sat locked away "for fear of the Jews," and Yeshua does not rebuke them for their lack of faith; he enters into their fear and dissolves it through presence. He asks: "Why are you troubled, and why do doubts arise in your hearts?" (Luke 24:38). He does not demand the elimination of doubt through effort but looks at it and passes through it together with them.

That is why he shows himself vulnerable, with his wounds visible, saying: "Look at my hands and my feet" (Luke 24:39). The wounds are not hidden. The resurrection does not erase suffering but integrates it. The message is not triumphant power but love that has not turned into resentment in the face of death. Thomas is the clearest example that the encounter is not conditioned on prior faith. Yeshua tells him: "Put your finger here… and do not be unbelieving, but believing" (John 20:27).

Faith is not demanded blindly but is born of sight. When he adds: "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed" (John 20:29), he is not praising credulity but trust in life even without direct sensory proof. Faith, for Yeshua, is openness toward reality, not adherence to a historical statement. The resurrection is immediately bound to mission and inner transformation: "As the Father has sent me, so I send you" (John 20:21).

It is not a medal for him but a responsibility for the disciples. Yeshua breathes on them and says: "Receive the Holy Spirit" (John 20:22). The resurrection is not only what happened to him but what can begin to happen in the human being: a new life, no longer driven by fear and guilt. On the road to Emmaus, Yeshua does not immediately say that he has risen but explains the Scriptures until they say: "Were not our hearts burning within us while he spoke to us on the road?" (Luke 24:32).

The resurrection is recognized by the life that is kindled in the human being, not imposed as a fact. That is why, even before the cross, Yeshua had said: "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25). Not "I will be," but "I am." The resurrection begins as a state of consciousness before it is an event after death. In contrast, the Apostle Paul builds an explicit theology of the resurrection.

For him, the resurrection is the cornerstone of faith: "If Christ has not been raised, your faith is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:17). The emphasis shifts from the encounter to the objective event. The resurrection becomes the proof that the sacrifice was accepted and that sin and death have been cosmically conquered. "Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20).

Paul sees the resurrection as the beginning of a new order of existence, valid for all who are "in Christ," affirming: "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22). He develops the idea of the risen body and the final victory, saying: "Death has been swallowed up in victory" (1 Corinthians 15:54). The resurrection is the foundation of future hope and of present justification.

Yet Paul also preserves an existential dimension when he says: "If you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above" (Colossians 3:1). Here he draws close to Yeshua's perspective, but the framework remains theological: the resurrection is a reality that you enter through faith and belonging. The difference of emphasis is clear.

Yeshua would want us to see the resurrection as the revelation of the life that cannot be killed, as an invitation to a life without fear, as the beginning of a transformation here and now. Paul would want us to see it as the central event of salvation, as proof that sin and death have been conquered and that future hope is certain. For Yeshua, the question is: why are you still afraid? For Paul, the question becomes: do you believe that Christ has risen? Both perspectives meet, but they are not identical.

Yeshua invites us to sight and peace. Paul invites us to faith and hope. In the spirit of the texts, Yeshua would say that the resurrection is recognized by the life that begins to manifest in the human being, while Paul would say that this new life is possible because the resurrection took place.

## Inner Freedom vs. Outer Security

One of the central tensions of Yeshua's message is the conflict between inner freedom and outer security. People seek security: money, status, rules, protection. Yeshua does not deny this need, but he considers it insufficient and, in the end, deceptive. "Do not worry about your life" (Matthew 6:25) is not a call to irresponsibility but an invitation to change the source of security. Outer security is fragile.

It depends on circumstances, on others, on control. Any loss threatens it. That is why a life built on security is dominated by fear. Yeshua observes that fear does not disappear through accumulation but grows. The more you have to lose, the more you fear. Inner freedom, by contrast, does not depend on circumstances. It appears when the human being is no longer defined by what he can lose.

"Seek first the Kingdom" (Matthew 6:33) is not a religious slogan but a reordering of existential priorities. The Kingdom becomes the inner place from which life is lived, not the reward for a correct life. Yeshua does not promise security. He promises peace. And peace, in his language, is not the absence of conflict but the presence of an inner state that does not waver. "My peace I give to you" (John 14:27) is different from the peace of the world, which is based on control.

Inner freedom does not eliminate risks, but it eliminates the fear that makes them paralyzing.

## What Remains When There Is Nothing Left to Defend

The final layer of Yeshua's message appears when all the mechanisms of defense collapse. When there is no longer an identity to protect, a status to maintain, a rightness to prove, a question arises: what remains? Yeshua does not answer this question theoretically. He lives it. When there is nothing left to defend, the need to control disappears. Comparison disappears. The anxiety of being "someone" disappears.

In this space appears the love that does not negotiate, the truth that does not attack, the presence that does not justify itself. Yeshua calls this space the Kingdom. On the cross, Yeshua does not defend himself, does not explain himself, does not justify himself. "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34) is not moral heroism but the expression of the fact that there is no longer a self to be wounded. Not because the pain is not real, but because the identity is no longer built on opposition.

What remains when there is nothing left to defend is not emptiness but true life. A life that is no longer reaction but response. That is no longer defensive but creative. This is the life that Yeshua calls "abundant" (John 10:10). Not because it has more, but because it no longer needs. At this point, discipleship comes to an end. Not because the road has ended, but because there is no longer a "someone" to walk the road.

What remains is only the life lived from a center that cannot be threatened. The truth requires no justification. When it appears, everything that was built as protection falls away through uselessness, not through violence. The human being has long lived out of tension, confusing vigilance with life and defense with identity. But what is real cannot disappear. When fear withdraws, what remains is not emptiness but simplicity — fullness without effort.

Here, the "I" that is right disappears, and the "others" who are wrong dissolve. Separation is not fought; it becomes unnecessary. Truth does not come to correct but to calm. The lie is not exposed; it is left without a role. Peace does not negotiate with circumstances. In this space, love is not a moral choice; it is the natural movement of unfragmented life. Words become rare, not from prohibition, but because reality is already expressed.

Silence is not an absence of meaning but a supersaturation of meaning. Yeshua no longer appears as a figure or a model, but as transparency. He does not say "follow me." He says nothing. And precisely for this reason, everything is set in order. The cross is no longer tragedy or sacrifice; it is the point at which there is no longer a self to suffer in order to save itself. What remains is the life that flows, even through pain, without contracting. Forgiveness is not an act; it is the absence of the one who would need not to forgive.

Nothing is held against anyone — not through forgetting, but because nothing is stored anymore. When there is nothing left to defend, there is no longer discipleship, road, or progress. Only what has always been here, before fear. The Kingdom does not come and go; it is recognized. And the recognition makes no noise.

## The Scale of Detachment in the Gospels

Yeshua's message is not only about morality, faith, or religious rules. At the heart of his preaching there constantly appears an invitation to detachment. Not to a cold or ascetic detachment, but to a progressive liberation from those things to which the human consciousness clings in order to build its identity, security, and worth.

In contemporary language we might say that Yeshua describes a process of untying attachments, and as each attachment falls away, the human being becomes freer and more capable of love. This movement is gradual. The person does not let go of everything at once. First the attachment to things falls away, then the attachment to security, then to results, to reputation, to possessive relationships, to the egoic identity, and, finally, to the very fear of death.

Seen this way, Yeshua's sometimes radical words are not impossible demands but stages in the maturing of consciousness. The first level of this liberation is detachment from objects and possessions. The human being often lives with the impression that his worth lies in what he owns. But Yeshua breaks this illusion by saying: "Watch out and guard yourselves against all greed for wealth; for one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15).

In the same spirit, he warns: "Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves break in and steal" (Matthew 6:19). The message is not that material things are evil in themselves, but that human identity cannot be built on them. When a person defines himself by what he has, he inevitably becomes a prisoner of the fear of losing. Once the attachment to objects is weakened, the next level of freedom is detachment from the anxiety of material security.

Here it is no longer merely a matter of things but of the constant fear of the future. That is why Yeshua says: "Do not worry about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink, nor about your body, what you will wear" (Matthew 6:25). To illustrate this freedom, he invites people to look at nature: "Look at the birds of the air: they neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and yet your heavenly Father feeds them" (Matthew 6:26).

At this point, detachment does not mean indifference but the passage from fear to trust. The person begins to live less out of anxiety and more out of openness toward life. An even subtler step appears when the person frees himself from attachment to results. Most people tie their peace of mind to the success or failure of their actions.

But Yeshua invites us to another perspective: "Do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about itself" (Matthew 6:34). In this statement a profound inner freedom is hidden. The person continues to act, to work, and to create, but he no longer binds his identity to the result. Action becomes the expression of life, not an instrument for obtaining validation.

After these outer levels appears one that is far more powerful: detachment from reputation and the opinion of others. Few things hold human consciousness more captive than the need for social approval. People are willing to sacrifice their authenticity in order to be accepted. That is precisely why Yeshua warns: "Woe to you when all people speak well of you" (Luke 6:26).

He openly criticizes the religious leaders of his time, saying that they "love the places of honor at feasts and greetings in the marketplaces" (Matthew 23:6–7). At this point, the person begins to detach himself from the social image and to live from an inner center more stable than public opinion. An even deeper level appears when the possessive attachment within relationships falls away. Here appear some of Yeshua's most radical statements.

He says: "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me" (Matthew 10:37). In another, even more shocking form, he says: "If anyone comes to me and does not hate his father and his mother… he cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:26). In the spiritual language of the era, this "hatred" does not describe a negative emotion but a breaking away from identity-based attachment. The relationships remain, but the possessiveness disappears. Love becomes free.

Beyond these layers, the Gospels lead toward an even more radical point: detachment from the egoic self. Here the human being begins to see that his social identity, his roles, and his personal story are not the essence of his being. That is why Yeshua says: "If anyone wants to come after me, let him deny himself" (Matthew 16:24). And he continues with the paradox that stands at the center of his spirituality: "Whoever loses his life for my sake will find it" (Matthew 16:25).

To lose one's life, in this sense, does not mean physical death but liberation from the egoic identity that the person constantly defends. The final level of this scale is detachment from the fear of death. When this attachment falls away, the human being becomes truly free. Yeshua expresses this freedom by saying of his own life: "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18).

And of his disciples he affirms: "Whoever loves his life will lose it, and whoever hates his life in this world will keep it for life eternal" (John 12:25). At this point, the instinct of self-protection is no longer the center of existence. The human being lives out of truth and love, not out of fear. Seen this way, the Gospels describe a true scale of inner freedom.

First the person detaches from things, then from material security, from results, from reputation, from possessive relationships, from the egoic identity, and, finally, from the fear of death. The paradox is that as these attachments fall away, the person does not become colder or more indifferent. On the contrary, he becomes more present, more free, and more capable of love. Thus, Yeshua's message is not one of sad renunciation but of a profound liberation of consciousness.

At the heart of the Gospels lies not the loss of life but the discovery of a life that no longer depends on anything external in order to be whole.

## "Your Faith Has Saved You" — or the Clarity That Liberates

When Yeshua utters the words "your faith has saved you," he does not validate a theological formula and does not confirm an intellectual adherence. In the living context of his encounters with people, these words never appear after a doctrinal confession but after an act of inner opening. They do not ratify a correct idea but recognize an inner state in which fear has been suspended and life has been able to flow again.

In the language of the era, the term translated as "faith" did not designate the acceptance of propositions about God. It indicated lived trust, availability, a heart that no longer defends itself. That is why the deeper meaning of the statement is not "you believed rightly" but "you saw clearly." Salvation is not granted as a prize for correctness; it manifests where clarity dissolves resistance. Where there is no longer defense, life enters.

Each time these words are spoken, they come at the end of an inner movement. The woman who touches the hem of his garment does not present a theology; she dares to step out of shame and fear, and this stepping out liberates her (Mark 5:34). The blind man does not recite a creed; he rises up out of helplessness and asks for sight, and this request places him in truth (Mark 10:52).

The centurion does not argue about the divine nature; he sees clearly the reality of authority and of trust, and this seeing opens the way to his healing (Matthew 8:10). In all these encounters, it is not doctrine that produces life but clarity. That is why Yeshua never says "your faith about me has saved you." He says "your faith." The emphasis falls on the state of the one before him, not on the object of faith.

Salvation is not about someone in whom you believe but about the way you cease to defend yourself against truth. The truth is not imposed; it is recognized. And the recognition appears when fear withdraws. This clarity is not a mental effort but a simple seeing. It is the moment when the person ceases to negotiate with the real and lets himself be touched by it. That is why Yeshua can say: "Do not be afraid; only believe" (Luke 8:50). Fear and clarity cannot coexist.

When fear disappears, faith — in its living sense — appears as a stillness that allows life to manifest. In this sense, "salvation" is not a heavenly verdict but a restoration of the flow. To be saved means to be brought back to wholeness, to be drawn out of fragmentation. "Son, your sins are forgiven" is not a juridical acquittal but a release from the inner tension that was blocking life (Mark 2:5).

Where the burden is lifted, the body straightens, the mind clears, the relationship heals. That is why Yeshua always binds salvation to lived truth, not to affirmation. "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32) does not refer to correct information but to a seeing that liberates. When a person sees without a mask, without calculation, without fear, the truth is no longer something to be demonstrated. It becomes evident. And the evidence brings freedom. This understanding radically changes the message.

Faith is no longer a condition imposed from the outside but a state that appears naturally when resistance disappears. Salvation is no longer deferred into a future but happens now, whenever clarity is allowed to be. "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21) does not describe a location but a state of consciousness in which life recognizes itself.

In this framework, the words "your faith has saved you" become a silent recognition of a simple truth: life heals where there is no longer fear. Clarity is not a merit but an availability. And this availability, when it appears, is enough.

## The Resurrection of Consciousness

In Christianity, the word "resurrection" is almost always associated exclusively with the historical event of Christ's resurrection. But if we read the Gospels and Yeshua's words carefully, we discover that resurrection is not only an external event that took place at a moment in the past or is awaited in an eschatological future. Resurrection is, above all, a transformation of consciousness. A passage from a state of inner death to a state of life.

Yeshua says directly: "I am the resurrection and the life; whoever believes in me, even if he has died, will live" (John 11:25). But immediately he adds something even more profound: "and everyone who lives and believes in me will never die" (John 11:26). This "will never die" cannot be reduced merely to biological death, since all people continue to die physically. It points to another dimension: inner death — the death of consciousness, of love, of presence.

In this sense, resurrection is the awakening of consciousness from a state of spiritual unconsciousness. The Apostle Paul captures this idea when he says: "Awake, you who sleep, rise from the dead, and Christ will give you light" (Ephesians 5:14). We notice here that "death" is not described as a physical state but as a state of inner sleep. To be dead means to live without consciousness, without love, without presence. It means to be captive in fear, in ego, in resentment.

In this sense, many people are biologically alive but spiritually dead. That is why resurrection is not only a promise for after death. It is an invitation for now. Yeshua says: "Truly, truly, I say to you, the hour is coming, and is now here, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God; and those who hear it will live" (John 5:25). The phrase "and is now here" is essential. Resurrection is not deferred to the end of history.

It begins at the moment when a person's consciousness awakens. The problem appears when resurrection is completely externalized. When it is seen only as an event that happened to Christ but has no application in our inner transformation. In this case, the message becomes passive: Christ has risen, and we remain the same. But in the logic of the Gospel, Christ's resurrection is a model of our transformation.

Paul says explicitly: "We were buried with him through baptism into death, so that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4). The purpose is not merely the contemplation of Christ's resurrection but participation in it. That is why Scripture speaks of a death that precedes resurrection. "So you also, consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus" (Romans 6:11).

This "death to sin" is not an annihilation of one's being but a death of the old consciousness. It is the death of fear, of pride, of resentment, of separation. In spiritual language, sin is not merely a moral violation but a state of separation from life. To die to sin means to die to that separated consciousness. That is precisely why Paul says something paradoxical: "Christ, having been raised from the dead, dies no more; death no longer has dominion over him" (Romans 6:9).

But then he applies this principle to believers as well. When a person's consciousness passes through this inner death and is reborn into a state of love and presence, death no longer holds the same power over him. The fear of death begins to dissolve. That is why the letter to the Hebrews says that Christ came "to deliver all those who, through fear of death, were subject to bondage all their lives" (Hebrews 2:15). True resurrection begins when fear dies.

For fear is one of the most powerful forms of inner death. Fear contracts consciousness, closes it, separates it. Love, by contrast, opens it. That is why the Apostle John says: "There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear" (1 John 4:18). In this sense, resurrection is the passage from fear to love. If we look at things this way, resurrection becomes a reality repeated in a person's life. It is not only a single moment but a series of inner passages.

Each time an illusion dies and a deeper understanding is born, a small resurrection takes place. Each time resentment dies and compassion appears, a resurrection takes place. Each time the ego dies and humility appears, a resurrection takes place. Yeshua himself speaks of this dynamic when he says: "Unless the grain of wheat that has fallen to the ground dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24).

This is the paradox of spiritual transformation: new life appears only after something old dies. Without death there is no resurrection. That is why resurrection can appear in any domain of life. A person can have a resurrection in relationships, when he passes from control to love. He can have a resurrection in thinking, when he passes from rigidity to understanding. He can have a resurrection in the heart, when he passes from resentment to forgiveness. Every authentic transformation is, in a certain sense, a resurrection.

In spiritual language, this is transcendence. Not in the sense of escaping from the world, but of surpassing the previous level of consciousness. The Apostle Paul describes this reality in a poetic way: "If you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above… for you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians 3:1–3). To die and to rise are no longer here biological events but states of consciousness.

In this sense, the true question is not only whether Christ has risen. The question is whether his resurrection produces a resurrection in us as well. Whether fear dies and love appears. Whether separation dies and compassion appears. Whether unconsciousness dies and presence appears. For true resurrection is not only an event from the past. It is a continual awakening of consciousness. A passage, again and again, from death to life. As the Gospel says: "We know that we have passed from death to life, because we love the brothers" (1 John 3:14). And where love appears, death already begins to lose its power.

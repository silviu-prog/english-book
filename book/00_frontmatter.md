# Who I Am

I was born on August 13, 1980, in Iași, into a religious, neo-Protestant environment, belonging to the Christian Evangelical denomination. From the very beginning, my childhood was saturated with the language of faith: memorized verses, sermons heard with close attention, church gatherings in which Scripture was treated as the absolute reference point for life. For me, the Bible was never merely a religious text. It was the universe in which I learned to think.

At the age of fourteen, I joined another neo-Protestant group in Brașov, one not officially affiliated yet holding a doctrine very close to that of the other evangelical denominations. For an adolescent, this environment had something magnetic about it: moral clarity, passion, the sense of taking part in something greater than yourself. In those years, faith was not, for me, a cultural identity but an existential calling. My passion for Scripture quickly became the center of my life.

I spent thousands of hours studying the Bible—not out of obligation. Rather, out of fascination. I remember whole evenings spent reading, comparing passages, trying to understand meanings, connections, apparent contradictions. For me, the Bible was an intellectual and spiritual puzzle of almost infinite depth. At twenty-two, I graduated from the Bible school attached to the community I belonged to. At that moment I was convinced that my life would be devoted to religious ministry. It was the natural path for someone who had already lived through years of intense study and spiritual involvement. At twenty-three, I also completed my degree at the Faculty of Psychology of Spiru Haret University in Brașov. At the time, I saw no contradiction between the two directions—theology and psychology. On the contrary, I saw them as two different ways of understanding the human being. Only later did I realize how deeply this training had shaped me.

My inclination toward psychology led me, almost inevitably, to read the biblical text not only theologically but also humanly. To look for inner dynamics, mechanisms, patterns of behavior, not only doctrines. And, if I am honest, it sometimes became hard for me to see things in purely religious terms once I had begun to see them psychologically.

Still, at twenty-seven, I made a decision that would radically change the direction of my life: I separated from that denomination. Not out of a lack of faith, but out of the conviction that the institutional form in which it was expressed had become too restrictive for the questions I had begun to ask myself. Initially, my intention was to start another church, an independent one. But I chose to wait another year before taking that step. Paradoxically, that period of waiting was the very moment when I began to drift further and further from the idea of religious organization itself.

I never stopped studying the Bible. But, gradually, my perspective changed. I began to read the text not only as a doctrinal authority but as an existential document—a collection of human experiences, spiritual intuitions, and historical tensions. Psychology helped me see in these texts not only "truths to be believed," but also states of consciousness, defense mechanisms, forms of fear, attachments, needs for validation or control. Things that, once you see them, you can no longer unsee.

This process was not a quick one. It took me roughly ten years from the moment I left the community that had formed me before I could honestly say that I identify more with an existentialist-humanist perspective than with the traditional Christian identity. This change did not mean abandoning my interest in Yeshua. On the contrary.

As I moved away from the religious institution, the figure of Yeshua became even more fascinating to me. I began to look at him not through the filter of dogma, but through the lens of fundamental questions about the human condition: freedom, responsibility, authenticity, consciousness. Ironically, it was precisely this perspective that provoked strong reactions from practicing Christians.

Many of them tell me that the way I interpret Yeshua's teachings is not Christian. Others, on the contrary, say that for the first time these teachings strike them as alive. This contrast of reactions was one of the main reasons I wrote this book. I currently host two podcasts on YouTube. One on existential philosophy, called "Existential Questions," and the other on the psychology of relationships, called "What Women Want."

Once again, I combine theology with psychology, exactly as I will do throughout this book. I am not writing from the position of an institutional theologian, nor from that of an adversary of Christianity. I write from the position of someone who lived the faith from the inside, with intensity, who spent thousands of hours in the biblical text, and who, for that very reason, feels the need to return to the fundamental question: What did Yeshua actually mean to say?

And, if I am honest, that question gave me no peace. It changed my direction. This book is the result of that question.

# Foreword

This book is not written against Christianity. Nor is it written to replace it. I do not contest anyone's faith, I do not dismantle rituals, I do not attack communities, and I do not set out to wrench from people's hands something that gives them meaning, support, or hope. I deeply respect the human need to believe, to belong, and to anchor oneself in a tradition. What I am doing here is something simpler—and, perhaps for that very reason, more uncomfortable.

I try to draw close to Yeshua, the man from Galilee, before his message became a system, a doctrine, an institution, or an ideology. Not in order to "reclaim" him from Christianity, but in order to see him as he is. Throughout history, between Yeshua's voice and the Christianity that was built in his name, there have settled centuries of interpretations, translations, political decisions, councils, fears, needs for control, and cultural contexts. None of this is, in itself, malevolent.

But it inevitably changes who Yeshua actually was. This book does not begin from the question "what must we believe about Yeshua?", but from one far more uncomfortable: how did he live, how did he see, and how did he relate to people, to truth, to error, and to God? I do not write from the stance of someone who "knows better."

I write from the stance of someone who has read, compared, asked questions, and observed real tensions between Yeshua's living message and the religious form that resulted from it afterward. The non-Christian reader is not being "converted." But he is invited to look beyond religious labels. If, along the way, some ideas prove uncomfortable, it is not because they attack faith, but because they touch a rarely explored area: the difference between following a message and belonging to a system.

Yeshua did not demand adherence to a doctrine. He did not demand uniformity. He did not demand the defense of a religious identity. And I admit this confused me for a long time. Because that was exactly what I was doing. But Yeshua demanded lucidity, ownership, presence, and inner transformation. This book does not ask you to agree with it. It asks only to be read with honesty.

# 14. The Redeemed Person

## What the Redeemed Person Looks Like from Yeshua's Perspective

In ordinary religious language, salvation is often described as a change of status: a person was lost, and then is declared saved. But when we read the Gospels closely, we discover that Yeshua speaks of salvation in a far deeper, far more existential way. For him, salvation is not merely a legal verdict in heaven, but a real transformation of a person's way of being. It is not only a promise for the future, but a change of consciousness in the present.

This is why he says of those who follow him: "I have come that the sheep may have life, and have it abundantly" (John 10:10). Salvation is life lived with an intensity and clarity that surpass fear, separation, and defensiveness. From Yeshua's perspective, the redeemed person is one who sees. He is not defined first of all by a doctrinal belief, but by a change of perception. This is why Yeshua says: "Blessed are your eyes, for they see" (Matthew 13:16).

This seeing is not intellectual but existential. The redeemed person sees reality without the filters of fear and religious identity. He no longer lives from the need to defend himself or to prove that he is right. He begins to perceive the world from an inner space of freedom. This is why Yeshua says: "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32).

The freedom he speaks of is not social or political freedom, but an inner freedom from the lies that have structured human consciousness. This freedom shows itself in the way a person relates to others. The redeemed person no longer lives by moral hierarchies. He no longer sorts the world into superior and inferior people, into clean and unclean, into the worthy and the unworthy. He sees the same life in every person.

This is why Yeshua says: "Love your enemies, bless those who curse you" (Matthew 5:44). Such a statement would be impossible for a person still living out of fear or defensive identity. To love one's enemy is not a moral feat, but the expression of a consciousness that no longer perceives the world through separation. In the same spirit, the redeemed person no longer lives by judgment. Judgment is the mechanism by which the ego confirms its own superiority.

But Yeshua says: "Do not judge, so that you will not be judged" (Matthew 7:1). He does not forbid discernment; he dismantles the inner reflex by which a person builds his identity by pointing out the faults of others. The redeemed person sees human fragility without positioning himself above it. He recognizes in others the same struggle he himself has known. Another characteristic of the redeemed person is the absence of fear. Fear is the basic structure of the consciousness of separation.

It makes a person defend himself, accumulate, control. But Yeshua says again and again: "Do not be afraid" (Matthew 10:31). For the person who lives in this state of trust, life is no longer a constant struggle for psychological survival. He lives in a deep trust in the divine reality that sustains existence. This is why Yeshua says: "Look at the birds of the air… your heavenly Father feeds them" (Matthew 6:26).

This image is not an invitation to passivity, but to liberation from the fundamental anxiety that dominates ordinary consciousness. The redeemed person is also a person of presence. He no longer lives fragmented between past and future. Fear and regret lose their power over him. This is why Yeshua says: "Do not be anxious about tomorrow" (Matthew 6:34). This statement does not deny responsibility; it shows that real life can be lived only in the present.

The person who has come out of the anxiety of the future becomes able to be truly present for others. This inner state produces a special kind of power. It is not the power of domination, but the power of presence. Yeshua says of those who live in this reality: "Whoever believes in me will also do the works that I do" (John 14:12). Power does not come from control, but from the deep alignment of a life with its source.

This is why the redeemed person becomes a presence that heals, calms, and transforms the space he enters. In this light, the salvation Yeshua describes bears a striking resemblance to what the contemplative traditions call enlightenment. It is not an accumulation of knowledge, but an awakening of consciousness. It is the moment when a person sees reality without the filter of fear and separation. He does not become morally perfect, but he becomes transparent to the life that flows through him.

This is why Yeshua says: "If your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light" (Matthew 6:22). Light is not a poetic metaphor, but the description of a consciousness that is no longer fragmented. In the end, the redeemed person, from Yeshua's perspective, is one who lives from the same source from which he himself lived. He is not defined by religion, by identity, or by spiritual status. He is defined by the life that flows freely through him.

This is why Yeshua says: "Abide in me, and I in you… whoever abides in me bears much fruit" (John 15:4–5). The fruit is the true sign of salvation. Not the declaration, not the belonging, not the ideology. Thus salvation is not only being saved from something, but awakening to something. It is the passage from a consciousness dominated by fear to a consciousness that lives from love, truth, and freedom. It is what Yeshua calls "abundant life."

And the person who lives this life becomes, without trying, a light for others: "You are the light of the world… let your light shine before others" (Matthew 5:14–16). In him, salvation is no longer a theological promise, but a living reality.

## Healing

Healing is not a secondary or decorative element, but one of the living cores of Yeshua's message and work. He does not merely speak about God; he makes God visible through the concrete restoration of life. When Yeshua appears, the Kingdom is not described through concepts, but through people who recover their bodies, their minds, and their dignity. "Yeshua went throughout all Galilee… healing every disease and every affliction" (Matthew 4:23).

The healings are not an appendix to the preaching; they are part of the preaching. The Kingdom is seen in the body, in the psyche, and in relationships. When the disciples of John the Baptist ask whether he is the one they have been waiting for, Yeshua does not answer with a theological definition, but with an observable reality: "The blind see, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear" (Matthew 11:5). This is his language about God: life restored. Illness is not presented as divine punishment, nor as a spiritual test. Yeshua explicitly rejects this logic when he says of the man born blind: "Neither this man sinned, nor his parents" (John 9:3). The world is wounded, and the presence of God is seen in the healing of this wound, not in its justification. Yeshua's healings are not only physical. He touches the body—"immediately the leprosy left him" (Mark 1:42)—but also the psyche, as seen in the man who, after being delivered, is "clothed and in his right mind" (Mark 5:15).

He restores social relationships, reintegrating the excluded, and gives identity back to the shamed: the bent-over woman becomes a "daughter of Abraham" (Luke 13:16). Even guilt and shame are healed when he says to the accused woman: "Neither do I condemn you" (John 8:11). For Yeshua, healing means becoming whole. It is significant that the same Greek word, sōzō, is used both for "to save" and for "to heal."

When Yeshua says, "Your faith has healed you" (Mark 5:34), he is not speaking about doctrinal correctness, but about a relational openness that allows restoration. To be saved means to be brought to wholeness. When he sends out his disciples, the mandate is clear and direct: "Heal the sick… and tell them: The Kingdom of God has come near to you" (Luke 10:9). The healing precedes the explanation. They are not asked to formulate a doctrine, but to restore life.

This shows that, for Yeshua, healing is not a charismatic exception, but the normal state of the Kingdom. In Paul, healing exists, but it is no longer the center of gravity. He acknowledges "gifts of healing" (1 Corinthians 12:9), yet he places them within a list of charisms, alongside others. The emphasis shifts toward justification, salvation through faith, the cross and the resurrection, the building up of the community.

It is telling that Paul does not promise universal healing, nor does he consider it the principal sign of God's presence. He speaks of his own "thorn in the flesh," which is not removed, and the answer he receives is: "My grace is sufficient for you" (2 Corinthians 12:9). Suffering begins to be reinterpreted as a pedagogical space, not necessarily as a reality that must be healed. As Christianity becomes institutionalized, this shift deepens.

Healing moves away from the center, and the emphasis falls more and more on sin and guilt, on post-mortem salvation, on doctrine and morality. Suffering is often spiritualized: a "cross to bear," "the will of God," a "test of faith." Healing is postponed to the afterlife. The body becomes secondary, and the present world becomes a place to be endured rather than restored.

Even where healing reappears in some modern currents, it is often turned into spectacle, technique, or proof of faith. Sometimes the absence of healing becomes guilt: "you were not healed because you did not believe enough." Such a logic would be foreign to Yeshua, who heals without setting conditions, without moralizing, and without turning suffering into an examination.

In Yeshua, healing is the principal sign of the Kingdom already present, and salvation is the making whole of the person here and now. In Paul, healing remains possible, but becomes one of the gifts, while the center shifts toward the cross and grace. In later Christianity, healing becomes marginal or conditional, suffering is normalized and sometimes sacralized, and the afterlife becomes the dominant concern. For Yeshua, healing is not a spiritual bonus.

It is the proof that God is present. Where life is restored, where a person recovers his body, his mind, and his dignity, there the Kingdom already is. Yeshua did not come to explain suffering. He came to heal it.

## The Change of Perspective: The Quiet Method by Which Yeshua Transforms a Person

One of the most subtle and profound characteristics of Yeshua's teaching is that he almost never tries to correct people's behavior directly. We do not see him offering long lists of rules, moral codes, or techniques of personal discipline. Although he speaks about actions—about loving, forgiving, not judging—the way he frames these exhortations shows that he does not treat behavior as the fundamental problem.

For Yeshua, the problem is not what a person does, but how a person sees reality. This is why he first changes the perspective, knowing that when the perspective changes, behavior changes almost inevitably. One of the clearest examples appears in the Sermon on the Mount. Instead of simply saying "do not do this" or "do not do that," Yeshua shifts attention from the behavior to the inner state out of which the behavior arises.

He says: "You have heard that it was said to those of old, 'You shall not murder.' But I say to you that anyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment" (Matthew 5:21-22). Similarly, he continues: "You have heard that it was said, 'You shall not commit adultery.' But I say to you that anyone who looks at a woman with lust has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matthew 5:27-28).

In these statements, the point is not to make moral rules stricter, but to show that the action is only the surface of an inner process. Yeshua moves the center of attention from the outside to the inside. He suggests that the real problem is not the act itself, but the perspective from which a person looks at life and at others. The same logic appears when he speaks about judging others.

Instead of simply saying "do not judge," he creates an image that completely changes the way a person sees the situation: "Why do you see the speck in your brother's eye, but do not notice the log in your own eye?" (Matthew 7:3). Through this metaphor, Yeshua does not try to correct the behavior of criticizing directly. He changes the perspective on one's own limitation. When a person sees his own "log," the need to judge disappears of itself. The behavior is transformed without coercion.

This method is also visible in the way he relates to sinners. In the episode of the woman caught in adultery, the people around her are focused on applying a rule: "In the Law, Moses commanded us to stone such women" (John 8:5). Yeshua does not begin a legal debate about the law. He changes the perspective of those present with a single sentence: "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her" (John 8:7).

In that moment, the perspective shifts from the woman's wrong to the awareness of one's own wrongs. The result is immediate: the people leave one by one. Their behavior changes, but not because they were given a new rule—rather because they saw reality from a different angle. Similarly, when Yeshua speaks about love for one's enemies, he does not offer a difficult moral technique that people must apply through effort.

He changes the perspective on God and on the world: "Love your enemies… so that you may be sons of your Father who is in heaven; for he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good" (Matthew 5:44-45). Through this image, Yeshua introduces a radically different perspective: reality is not organized around merit, but around a divine generosity that includes everyone.

When a person sees the world this way, love for one's enemies no longer appears as an impossible obligation, but as a natural consequence of a new understanding of reality. The same strategy appears in the parables. Each time, Yeshua does not offer rules; he changes the way people perceive life.

In the parable of the prodigal son, the emphasis does not fall on the son's wrong behavior, but on the perspective of the father who receives him back: "While he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion; he ran, fell on his neck, and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). Through this image, Yeshua radically changes the perspective on God. If God is a father who runs toward his lost son, then fear, shame, and defensiveness become useless.

A person's behavior is transformed not through coercion, but through the discovery of a reality greater than his guilt. This approach also explains why Yeshua speaks so often about "seeing." He says: "The eye is the lamp of the body. If your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light" (Matthew 6:22). Here the "eye" is not just the physical organ of sight, but the way a person perceives reality. If the perspective is clear, life becomes clear.

If the perspective is dark, all of existence becomes confused. Yeshua does not try to regulate every action a person takes, but to heal the "eye" through which a person looks at the world. In this light it becomes clear why Yeshua says: "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32). He does not say that rules will set you free, nor that moral discipline will set you free.

Freedom appears when the perspective changes, when a person sees reality as it is. From that new perspective, behavior reorganizes itself spontaneously. Therefore, Yeshua's method is not one of moral control, but of transformation of consciousness. He does not try to repair the fruit of a sick tree; he heals the root. This is why he says: "The good tree bears good fruit, but the bad tree bears bad fruit" (Matthew 7:17). If the tree becomes good, the fruit will become good without effort.

This is one of the most profound insights of the Gospels: behavior is only the visible expression of an invisible perspective. And Yeshua always works at the level of that perspective. He changes the way a person sees God, himself, and others. And when this seeing changes, the whole of life begins to transform almost inevitably.

## What Is "Abundance" in Yeshua?

Abundance, in Yeshua's language, is not accumulation, but fullness. It does not mean "more"; it means "whole." It is not about having, but about being. "Abundant life" is not life without suffering, but life without inner separation. Yeshua lives this abundance even in lack. He has no material security, no stable social status, no political power.

And yet he says: "The Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head" (Matthew 8:20). Even so, he is alive, present, free, unafraid. His abundance does not come from what he possesses, but from the fact that he is not fragmented. "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven" (Matthew 5:3). This "poverty" is not lack, but openness. It is the absence of false identities, of rigid attachments, of the fear of loss. Out of this emptying, abundance arises.

## What Would People's Lives Have Looked Like, Lived According to Yeshua's Definition of Abundance?

Life lived in abundance, according to Yeshua, would have been a life not driven by fear. "Do not be afraid" (Matthew 10:31). It would have been a life in which a person is defined not by what he gathers, but by how present he is. "Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21). It would have been a life in which relationships are not transactions, but real encounters.

"Love one another as I have loved you" (John 13:34). A life in which a person does not constantly defend himself, does not obsessively justify himself, does not live by comparison. "Do not judge, so that you will not be judged" (Matthew 7:1). Abundance would have meant the capacity to feel deeply without fleeing, to lose without losing yourself, to give without exhausting yourself.

"Whoever loses his life for my sake will gain it" (Matthew 16:25). This is not a call to masochistic sacrifice, but to the abandonment of the false identity that lives out of fear.

## The Shepherd and True Life

"I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). To lay down one's life does not mean only physical death, but total exposure, the absence of any mask, living in truth. Yeshua does not save the sheep from life; he leads them into life. He does not protect them from reality; he teaches them to live it without separation. "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). Abundant life is not a future reward, but a present state.

It is life lived from the center, from truth, from the heart. Not a controlled life, but a contained one. So when Yeshua says that he came so that people might have abundant life, he is not promising comfort, success, or security. He is promising true life. Life lived, not mimicked. Life felt, not postponed. People are called "sheep" not because they should remain so, but because they can be led from wandering to awakening. Abundance is not the destination, but the consequence of a life lived in truth.

## What Would the World Look Like If We Were All Like Yeshua?

I have asked myself this question many times, and no one has yet managed to refute my theory that in a world of people like Yeshua there would no longer be any hospitals, doctors, judges, or police. The hospitals would not disappear because we closed them, but because the suffering that makes them necessary would be transformed. The police would not disappear because we no longer need order, but because order would become natural.

Yeshua did not come to reform the exterior, but to heal the root: "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). Where a person is healed within, the outer structures are no longer mechanisms of control, but become simple expressions of a harmony that already exists. In such a world, hospitals would become spaces of care, not of crisis.

For illness, to a large extent, is the expression of an inner imbalance, and a person who lives in truth, in presence, and in love no longer generates the same ruptures between body, mind, and soul. "Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick" (Matthew 9:12)—but in a world where consciousness is whole, illness is no longer the norm, but the rare exception. Medicine would no longer be a fight against the symptom, but a support of harmony.

The police, the courts, and the prosecutors would no longer function as instruments of coercion, because evil would no longer spring from within the person. Yeshua says: "Out of the heart come evil thoughts, murders, adulteries…" (Matthew 15:19). If the heart is pure, behavior naturally becomes right. "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matthew 5:8). In a world of people who see, there is no longer any need for laws to punish blindness.

Justice would no longer be a system of sanction, but a living consciousness, shared by all. But this would not mean a sterile world, devoid of expression. On the contrary. Music would not disappear—it would become deeper. Art would not disappear—it would become more authentic. Creativity would not be fueled by trauma, but by fullness. Today, much of what we call art is the cry of an uncomprehended pain. In a world like Yeshua, art would be revelation rather than discharge.

"Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks" (Matthew 12:34). If the heart is full of life, creation becomes the expression of life, not of lack. Painting would no longer be merely interpretation, but participation. Music would no longer be merely emotion, but resonance. Art would no longer seek validation, but would flow naturally, the way light flows through a stained-glass window. Not in order to impress, but because it cannot help but express itself. What happens to competition? It would not disappear entirely, but it would be radically transformed.

It would no longer be competition born of lack, of the desire to be "more than the other," but play. A celebration of potential. "Whoever wants to be the greatest among you must be the servant of all" (Mark 10:44). This changes everything. You no longer compete in order to dominate, but in order to express yourself fully and to inspire. Sport would remain, but without the tension of the ego. It would become dance, not war.

In such a world, success would no longer mean rising above others, but aligning yourself with what you are. Failure would no longer be shame, but feedback. No one would need to prove anything anymore, because worth would no longer be negotiated. And perhaps most important: relationships would no longer be emotional transactions. No one would love in order to receive. "Love your enemies… so that you may be sons of your Father" (Matthew 5:44-45).

In a world like Yeshua, love would no longer be a strategy, but a nature. There would no longer be any "discard," because no one would live in blindness anymore. Seeing would become the norm. In the end, the question "Would there still be hospitals, police, competition?" is in fact a question about the level of consciousness from which we live. Yeshua did not describe a different world through rules, but through people. A world in which people are healed within does not need to be controlled from without.

For when a person sees—truly sees—he can no longer wound. And when he no longer wounds, the world no longer needs to be defended against him.

## What Would a Social System Truly Inspired by Yeshua Look Like?

If we tried to imagine a politics truly inspired by Yeshua's logic, the first thing that would have to be understood is that he did not come to propose a political system. Every time he was pushed in this direction, he shifted the center of gravity from the outside to the inside: "My Kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36). Not because it has no impact on the world, but because it has its origin not in structures, but in consciousness.

Any social system that tries to apply Yeshua's principles without the transformation of the person inevitably becomes a caricature—rules without life, morality without presence. In such a politics, power would no longer be defined as domination, but as responsibility. Yeshua says explicitly: "You know that the rulers of the nations lord it over them… but it shall not be so among you. Rather, whoever would be great among you must be your servant" (Matthew 20:25-26).

This completely overturns the current paradigm. The leader is no longer the one who accumulates control, but the one who reduces suffering. He is no longer the one who imposes, but the one who supports. Power becomes the capacity to contain, not to constrain. I believe that a social system inspired by Yeshua would not function on the basis of fear and sanction, but on the basis of consciousness and inner responsibility. Law would not disappear, but its role would be transformed.

Yeshua does not deny the law—"I have not come to abolish the Law, but to fulfill it" (Matthew 5:17)—but he moves its purpose from the outside to the inside. Law is no longer an instrument for controlling behavior, but a mirror that shows where a person is not yet free. In a mature society, law would become less and less necessary, because people would no longer act out of unconscious impulses. Justice, in this logic, would no longer be centered on punishment, but on restoration.

Yeshua refuses to condemn the woman caught in adultery and says: "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone" (John 8:7), and then: "Neither do I condemn you; go, and sin no more" (John 8:11). Here we see a justice that does not ignore responsibility, but that also does not reduce a person to his mistake. The system would no longer sustain cycles of guilt and punishment, but would create the conditions for real transformation.

The economy would undergo, perhaps, the most profound transformation. Instead of being built on accumulation and the fear of lack, it would be built on trust and sufficiency. "You cannot serve God and Mammon" (Matthew 6:24)—this statement is not only spiritual, but deeply social. A society driven by the fear of tomorrow will inevitably produce inequality and destructive competition.

By contrast, Yeshua points to another way of living: "Look at the birds of the air… your heavenly Father feeds them" (Matthew 6:26). Not as an invitation to passivity, but as the foundation of an economy without chronic anxiety. The distribution of resources would no longer be a field of ideological battle, but a natural expression of consciousness. In the early communities inspired by this logic, "no one claimed that his possessions were his own, but they held everything in common" (Acts 4:32).

Not through coercion, but through inner transformation. Where there is no longer any rigid attachment to possession, generosity becomes spontaneous. The relationship between groups—whether ethnic, political, or cultural—would also be radically different. Yeshua says: "Love your enemies… do good to those who hate you" (Matthew 5:44). This is not a diplomatic strategy, but a paradigm shift. Instead of polarization, there arises the capacity to see beyond differences.

Conflict would no longer be fueled by rigid identities, but would become an opportunity for deeper understanding. Such a system would not eliminate the diversity of opinions or the tensions inherent in a living society, but it would change the way they are handled. Not through escalation, but through presence. "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God" (Matthew 5:9). Politics would no longer be the art of manipulating perception, but the art of reconciliation.

And yet the most important change would not be visible in institutions, but in people. For Yeshua never believed that you can create a just world with people who are inwardly unresolved. "How can you say to your brother, 'Let me take the speck out of your eye,' when you do not see the log in your own eye?" (Matthew 7:4). A politics inspired by Yeshua would begin not with external reforms, but with inner responsibility. In the end, such a system could not be imposed.

For that would contradict its very essence. It could only emerge, organically, from a sufficient number of people who live differently. "You will know them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:16). Not by ideologies, not by promises, but by the real effect on life. A politics inspired by Yeshua is not a program. It is the consequence of a humanity that has finally learned to see.

## Yeshua and Awakening Through Relationship

Yeshua's message is not built around a technique for calming the mind, nor around a method of meditation or inner withdrawal. Constantly, he shifts attention away from the isolated interior of the individual toward the space between people, toward relationship. This is why, when he speaks of the Kingdom of God, he does not describe it as a solitary state of mental quiet, but as a reality that appears when people are transformed in the way they relate to one another.

He says: "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21), but immediately he shows that this Kingdom manifests itself in the way people forgive, are reconciled, and love one another. This orientation becomes very clear in the Sermon on the Mount. Instead of saying that a person must withdraw into himself in order to find peace, Yeshua places the emphasis on reconciliation. He affirms that authentic spirituality cannot exist alongside a rupture between people.

This is why he says: "If you bring your gift to the altar and there you remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar, go first and be reconciled with your brother, and then come and offer your gift" (Matthew 5:23–24). It is an extraordinary passage, because it moves the center of the spiritual life from ritual to relationship. God is not honored by religious gestures if the heart remains separated from one's neighbor.

Through this radical inversion, Yeshua completely changes the religious paradigm. In most spiritual traditions, transformation is presented as an inner process: a person withdraws, meditates, purifies himself, and arrives at peace. Yeshua, however, suggests that true transformation appears when a person learns to love and to forgive. He goes so far as to say: "Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you" (Matthew 5:44).

This is not a meditation technique, but a revolution of the heart. It is not an invitation to quiet introspection, but to a profound change in the way a person responds to conflict and to pain. This is why, in Yeshua's message, spiritual awakening is not isolated. It does not take place in a cave, in a state of solitary contemplation, but in the midst of human relationships. A person discovers the truth about himself not merely by sitting alone, but above all when he is confronted with the other.

In relationship, pride, fear, the need for control, and the desire for personal vindication rise to the surface. It is precisely there, in that tense space between two consciousnesses, that the possibility of transformation appears. This is why Yeshua says: "By this everyone will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another" (John 13:35). Spiritual identity is given not by doctrine, nor by ritual, but by the capacity to love.

This perspective also explains why many of Yeshua's parables are built around relationships: father and son, brother and brother, master and servant, Samaritan and stranger. In all these stories, what is at stake is not intellectual knowledge or mystical experience, but the transformation of the heart in relation to the other. In the parable of the good Samaritan, for example, the question is not who has the correct faith, but who was a neighbor to the wounded man.

Yeshua overturns the religious logic and shows that truth is verified in compassion. Seen from this perspective, Yeshua's spirituality is profoundly relational. It does not begin with the stillness of the mind, but with the heart's willingness to forgive and to love. Instead of seeking a state of personal peace, a person is called to enter into a dynamic of reconciliation.

It is precisely for this reason that, for Yeshua, the Kingdom of God is not an inner refuge, but a reality that manifests itself in community, in the way people treat one another. At this point one of the greatest tensions in the history of Christianity appears. If we read Paul's writings, we notice that the emphasis shifts from relational transformation toward faith, doctrine, and theological justification.

While Yeshua speaks about forgiveness, reconciliation, and the love of enemies, Paul speaks about faith, grace, and salvation through faith in Christ. This is not necessarily an absolute contradiction, but it is a significant shift of emphasis: from the transformed relationship to the correct faith.

It is precisely here, between these two different emphases, that one of the greatest ruptures in the history of Christianity is born, and one of the most important questions for any reader of the Gospels: what did Yeshua really want to transform first—the human mind, or the human heart in relation to others?

I spent a few years of my life studying filmmaking in Hollywood, between 2015 and 2019. Thousands of hours in which I did not merely watch films, but dissected them, understood their structure, felt their rhythm, and, above all, wrote. Screenplay after screenplay. Stories that tried to work. Some succeeded. Others did not. But beyond technique, beyond dialogue, framing, and lighting, I discovered a simple and profound truth: all good films have something in common.

It is not just about conflict. It is not just about the transformation of the protagonist. It is not even about the final success. It is about what happens after. After the last confrontation. After the apparent victory or defeat. After the resolution of the external conflict. There is one more scene. A quiet scene, sometimes subtle, sometimes explicit, but absolutely essential: the scene in which a relationship is restored. Someone returns home. Someone forgives.

Someone is seen for the first time. Someone finally allows himself to love. This is, in fact, the true resolution of a film. Because a human being is not built for performance, but for relationship. And without this last scene, however spectacular the journey may have been, the story remains incomplete. Surprisingly—or perhaps not surprisingly at all—Yeshua does exactly the same thing. His entire work is not about religion. It is not about rules.

It is not about building an institution. It is about restoring relationships. Every time he enters a story, Yeshua does not leave without repairing something invisible but fundamental: the connection. He does not heal only bodies. He heals ruptures. When he meets the tax collector despised by everyone, he does not offer him only a moral direction, but gives him back his dignity in relationship: "Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:9).

He does not say only that the man has changed, but that his relationship with life, with others, and with God has been restored. When he speaks about forgiveness, he does not do so as an ethical obligation, but as a relational liberation: "Forgive, and you will be forgiven" (Luke 6:37). For where there is no forgiveness, there is no relationship. There is only distance in disguise.

When he touches the leper—the person completely excluded from society—he does not perform only an act of physical healing, but one of reintegration: he brings him back into the relational fabric of the community. "I am willing; be cleansed!" (Matthew 8:3). In that "I am willing" there is not only power, but acceptance. And perhaps the most profound image of this restoration is in the parable of the prodigal son.

It is not the moment when the son acknowledges his mistake that is the climax, but the moment when the father runs toward him and embraces him before he can finish his confession: "For this son of mine was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found" (Luke 15:24). This is the final scene. Not the moral return. Not the correction of behavior. But the restoration of the relationship. Exactly as in a good film.

And perhaps this is one of the most misunderstood truths about salvation: it is not about becoming "correct," but about being reconnected. The relationship with yourself—where you no longer reject yourself. The relationship with others—where you no longer operate out of fear or control. The relationship with the "enemy"—where separation begins to dissolve. The relationship with God—where there is no longer any distance, but presence.

For "the Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21), and this Kingdom is not a place, but a state of unfragmented connection. The film industry taught me the structure of a story. Yeshua taught me its meaning. However complex the conflict, however dramatic the journey, however high or low the protagonist may rise or fall—the story is not complete until the relationship is restored.

And perhaps this is the real question, not only for films but for life: With whom are you still in conflict, within or without? And what would it mean if the final scene were not your victory… but your reconnection? Because, in the consciousness of God, the story never ends with who is right. It ends with who returns into relationship.

## Would the World Have Been More "Like Jesus" Without Christianity?

Looking at history, Christianity has played a profoundly ambivalent role. On the one hand, it kept the memory of Yeshua alive, transmitted his name, his words, his parables, carried his story from generation to generation. On the other hand, the same Christianity has, over time, justified wars, violence, domination, exclusion, and power exercised in his name. Yeshua said clearly: "My Kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36).

And yet institutional Christianity has often tried to turn Yeshua into a banner, into a symbol of mobilization, into a group identity rather than a way of being. At many moments in history, religion has represented Yeshua without resembling him. It is precisely this rupture that the warning speaks of: "They say, but do not do" (Matthew 23:3).

It is possible that, without Christianity as an institution, Yeshua's message would have remained smaller in scope, more limited geographically and historically, but purer in expression. It is equally possible that, without a form, without structure, and without organized transmission, the message would have been lost entirely. History offers no simple answer, and this tension remains open. Yeshua did not come to create a religion, but to awaken the person.

He did not call people to adhere to a system, but to enter into a new life. What we call the "Great Commission" is not, in essence, about religious expansion, but about the multiplication of a life lived in truth. "Greater things" does not mean the glorification of an individual or the consolidation of an institution, but continuity, maturation, and the extension of the same state of consciousness in different people. The apostles, in this key, are not guardians of dogma, but bearers of life.

They transmit what they live, not merely what they affirm. Christianity, in its historical form, has been a vehicle: sometimes faithful to the original message, sometimes distorting it. Sometimes transparent, sometimes opaque. The message is not the problem; the problem is the way it has been frozen, defended, instrumentalized. So Yeshua did not want a world full of "Christians." He wanted a world full of living people. "I have come that the sheep may have life, and have it abundantly" (John 10:10).

Resemblance to Yeshua does not depend on the existence of Christianity as an institution, but on how many people live from the same heart from which he lived. It is not the label that brings the world closer to Yeshua, but the life that flows from the inside out—free, alive, and true.

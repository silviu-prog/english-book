# 6. Consciousness, Truth, and Reality

## What Is Truth

To understand the deep rupture between Yeshua and dogmatic Christianity, we must look carefully at the way one and the same word — "truth" — takes on two almost opposite meanings. The language appears identical, but the experience it points to is radically different. For Yeshua, truth is a lived reality, a state into which one enters; in classical Christian theology, truth becomes a correct formulation about reality, something to be affirmed and defended. This mutation is no minor thing.

It changes entirely the relationship of the human being with God, with himself, and with others, because it shifts the center of gravity from transformation to conformity. For Yeshua, truth is not something one possesses, but something that happens. It appears when a person stops defending himself, when fear dissolves, and when reality is seen without distortion. That is why he offers no exhaustive definitions and demands no intellectual assent, but invites us to abide and to live: "If you abide in my word… you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:31–32). Truth is not separate from life, from love, or from freedom. It is not an object to be learned, but a state into which one enters. Yeshua does not say "you will learn the truth," but "you will know," and this knowing is experiential, liberating, transforming.

Yeshua's truth does not create borders between "those who are right" and "those who are wrong." On the contrary, it dissolves rigid identities and lifts a person out of the need for moral superiority. Truth does not validate the ego; it strips it bare. That is why Yeshua links truth to light and to the coherence of a life: "Whoever does what is true comes to the light" (John 3:21). Truth is not what you affirm, but what you live. It is not a correct proposition, but an aligned life.

In dogmatic Christian theology, however, truth is moved from the plane of experience to the plane of the correct proposition. It becomes something that must be precisely formulated, guarded, defended, and transmitted without deviation. Truth is no longer "that which sets you free," but "that which you must accept." The emphasis shifts from transformation to adherence. What matters first is no longer whether a person is inwardly free, but whether he is "in agreement" with doctrine.

Thus truth becomes a criterion of belonging and an instrument of demarcation: whoever believes correctly is "in the truth," whoever does not is "in error." For Yeshua, truth is a continuous process of unveiling. He acknowledges that people cannot bear the whole truth at once: "I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now" (John 16:12). Truth is revealed progressively, in keeping with a person's inner capacity to live it without fear.

It is dynamic, alive, bound to the maturing of consciousness. In dogma, truth is regarded as already complete: it has been formulated, fixed, and handed down. The believer's role is no longer to discover it, but to accept it. Thus truth no longer calls for inner maturity, but for outward conformity. Yeshua says of himself: "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6), not as a definition to be defended, but as a description of a state of unity between being, word, and deed. Truth is life lived without rupture. In the dogmatic reading, this statement becomes the foundation of doctrinal exclusivity: truth is located within an identity that must be correctly recognized. Thus what in Yeshua is an invitation to transformation becomes, over time, a criterion of separation. The rupture between these two understandings of truth explains many of the historical tensions of Christianity. When truth is lived, it frees, unites, and heals.

When truth is possessed, it demarcates, separates, and demands defense. Yeshua brings a truth that releases a person from fear. Dogma often ends up defending a truth that keeps a person in fear of being wrong. And between these two ways of understanding the same word lies the difference between a transformed life and a correctly formulated belief.

## "The Truth Will Set You Free" — The Meaning of a Radical Statement

When Yeshua says "The truth will set you free," he is making neither an intellectual promise nor a religious one in the classical sense. He is not speaking about accumulating correct information, nor about adhering to a true doctrine. Yeshua speaks of an existential liberation, of a change in the state of the human being. For him, truth does not inform; truth releases. Those who hear him react at once, insisting that they have never been slaves. Their response betrays the fundamental misunderstanding: they are speaking of political, social, or identity-based freedom. Yeshua is speaking of an inner bondage. For him, a person can be outwardly free and yet deeply captive within; he can be religious and yet unfree; he can keep the law and live in fear. The bondage Yeshua speaks of is not imposed from outside, but maintained from within.

It manifests through fear, attachments, identification with the ego, the need for control and certainty, the clinging to an image of oneself. A person becomes a prisoner not because he is constrained, but because he is perpetually defending himself against the truth. Truth threatens these defenses, and for that reason it is rejected. In this context, truth is not a correct idea about the world, but reality seen without distortion. Truth sets us free because it dissolves the lie about who you think you are.

It breaks the identification with roles, with the moral mask, with the self-image built out of fear. It adds nothing new to a person; on the contrary, it removes what is not real. This unveiling is not comfortable. Truth hurts before it sets free, because it exposes the ego's mechanisms of defense. That is why Yeshua provokes such powerful reactions: he disturbs the self-assured, irritates those attached to their position, and scandalizes religious structures.

Truth does not confirm identities; it disintegrates them. And without this disintegration, freedom is not possible. The liberation Yeshua speaks of is, in essence, liberation from fear. The fear of punishment, of loss, of rejection, of death. Truth offers not security, but presence. In the absence of fear, a person no longer needs to defend himself, to dominate, or to justify himself. This is real freedom: not the power to control, but the absence of the need for control.

Yeshua does not say that truth is something you "know" in a moment of intellectual revelation. He speaks of "abiding" in truth. Truth is not a conclusion, but a dwelling: remaining in honesty, in openness, in the willingness to see reality without the filter of fear. Liberation does not occur instantaneously, but in the measure that a person ceases to flee from truth. In Yeshua's vision, truth cannot be separated from love.

A truth spoken without love becomes abuse, and a love without truth becomes illusion. That is why Yeshua's truth is neither a weapon nor an instrument of moral correction. It is a presence that heals precisely because it is spoken within relationship. Thus, when Yeshua says "The truth will set you free," he is saying that truth will free you from the fear within you, from the false identity, from the need for control, and from the illusion of separation. It will not make you more correct or more religious, but more free.

And for Yeshua, freedom does not mean doing whatever one wishes, but no longer being hindered by anything from being alive. This is the statement that lies at the root of the conflict between Yeshua and the world: truth does not maintain the order of fear, but dissolves it. That is why it is so desired in words and so avoided in life.

## Silence as a Form of Truth

At the key moments of his trial, Yeshua is silent. Before religious accusations and political power, he refuses to defend himself (Mark 14:61; Luke 23:9). This silence is often interpreted as humility, but it is more than that. It is the refusal to enter into an explanatory game that would legitimize the violence. Any explanation would have been a negotiation. It would have shifted the discussion onto the terrain of power and would have validated the framework that produces death. Silence, by contrast, unmasks the system.

It shows the nakedness of violence without justifying it through argument. After Yeshua's death, the communities are left with a profound trauma. A man who spoke of love, freedom, and life is executed as a criminal. In order to carry on, they need meaning, order, and coherence. It is in this space that the theology of the cross appears, above all in the writings of Paul. Death becomes sacrifice, payment becomes salvation, blood becomes language. It is important, however, to notice the essential difference. This is an interpretation of Yeshua's death, not an explanation offered by Yeshua himself. It is an effort to integrate the trauma, not a revelation about the needs of God. If his death had been explained as necessary to God, violence would have become sacred. But Yeshua constantly refuses violence. He stops Peter when he draws his sword (Matthew 26:52), refuses political power (John 18:36), and forgives even from the cross (Luke 23:34).

Nothing in his conduct validates the idea that violence could be a divine instrument. His death is presented neither as an ideal nor as a goal. It is presented as the tragic result of the encounter between truth and fear.

## Truth in Yeshua. Why Liberation Precedes Interpretation

For Yeshua, truth is not a correct formula, nor an exact interpretation of the Scriptures, nor even a perfect understanding of his own words. Truth is not something a person possesses through intelligence or doctrine, but something a person enters through openness. "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32). The order is essential.

For Yeshua, truth is not information that produces freedom, but a living reality that is recognized when a person ceases to defend himself. Yeshua never says that the correct interpretation of the Scriptures saves. On the contrary, he frequently comes into conflict with those who know them best. "You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; yet it is they that bear witness about me. But you refuse to come to me that you may have life" (John 5:39–40).

Here the fundamental rupture is visible. Scripture can be correctly interpreted and yet life can be absent. For Yeshua, truth is not in the text, but in the encounter that the text should make possible. Truth, for Yeshua, is not something that convinces through logical force, but something that is revealed when a person is inwardly free enough to receive it. "If you abide in my word, you are truly my disciples" (John 8:31).

To abide does not mean to explain, but to dwell. It does not mean to analyze, but to live. That is why truth cannot be imposed. Any imposed truth ceases to be truth and becomes coercion. For Yeshua, liberation is not the result of a correct understanding, but the condition that makes understanding possible. "If the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (John 8:36). Freedom does not come at the end of an intellectual process; it appears when fear disappears.

Only the free person can see clearly. Only the person who is no longer on the defensive can understand without distorting. Interpretation comes after, not before. That is why Yeshua speaks differently with each person. He does not repeat formulas and offers no universal definitions. To one he says "follow me," to another "return home," to another "go and sin no more." Truth is not standardized, because people are not standardized. "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). Truth is not something he says, but what he is when the relationship is alive. In this sense, truth in Yeshua is not opposed to intellectual error, but to fear. "The light has come into the world, and people loved the darkness rather than the light because their deeds were evil" (John 3:19). Darkness is not the absence of information, but the closing of the heart. When truth threatens identity, status, or inner security, a person will reject it even if he recognizes it as truth.

That is why truth cannot be separated from freedom. Yeshua does not ask people to believe correctly before they are healed. He heals them, and faith often appears afterward. "Your faith has saved you; go in peace" (Luke 7:50). Peace precedes doctrine. Life precedes theology. The experience of healing precedes the explanation. Truth is recognized by the body before it is formulated by the mind. That is why, for Yeshua, truth is not something to be defended, but something to be lived.

It is not something to be demonstrated, but something to be allowed to be. "The kingdom of God is not coming in ways that can be observed… for behold, the kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:20–21). Truth is not a conclusion, but a state of openness. In the end, for Yeshua, it is not the correct interpretation that frees a person, but the liberation that makes him capable of interpreting without distorting.

Truth is not a weapon in the hand of the one who knows, but the breath of the one who no longer defends himself. That is why truth is not imposed. It is recognized. And only the free person can recognize it without turning it into dogma. For Yeshua, truth is not a correct proposition, but a living presence between people. He does not teach truth as information, but reveals it in encounter, in dialogue, in compassion, in the way he looks at the person before him.

When he says "I am the Way, the Truth, and the Life" (John 14:6), he offers no theological definition, but shifts the center of truth from the sphere of ideas into the sphere of relationship. Truth is not something you can separate from the person, from the context, from the inner state of the one who receives it. It is spoken to someone, in a living moment, within a concrete relationship.

That is why Yeshua never answers questions mechanically, but differently, according to the person before him: with the Samaritan woman he speaks of living water (John 4), with Nicodemus of being born again (John 3), with the Pharisees of their spiritual blindness (Matthew 23). Truth is always personalized, not standardized. In dogmatic theology, however, truth is extracted from this relational dimension and made abstract.

It becomes a formula applied uniformly, regardless of person, context, or level of consciousness. The relationship becomes secondary, and doctrinal correctness becomes paramount. Truth is no longer something born between two people, but something pronounced from the top down, as a universal rule. What in Yeshua is a living encounter becomes, in dogma, a fixed proposition.

## Truth That Dissolves the Ego vs. Truth That Reinforces It

Yeshua's truth has a profound function: it dissolves the spiritual ego. No one "has" the truth. No one is immune to self-deception. No one can claim to see perfectly. That is why he is so critical of those convinced that they "see." "If you were blind, you would have no sin; but now that you say, 'We see,' your sin remains" (John 9:41). The greatest problem is not ignorance, but rigid certainty. Not the lack of truth, but its possession.

In the Sermon on the Mount, Yeshua shifts attention from the outer law to the inner state: "You have heard that it was said… but I say to you" (Matthew 5). He does not merely correct behaviors; he dismantles the illusion that a person can be righteous through outward conformity alone. His truth does not validate religious identity, but calls it into question. That is why the Pharisees are the target of his harshest words: "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!" (Matthew 23). Not because they did not know Scripture, but because they were convinced that they had mastered it. In its dogmatic form, truth often becomes fuel for the ego: "we have the truth," "we believe correctly," "the others are lost." Truth no longer frees from the ego, but legitimizes it. It no longer produces humility, but superiority. It no longer produces compassion, but judgment. Where Yeshua's truth makes a person aware of his own blindness, dogmatic truth makes him certain of his own clarity.

When Yeshua says "you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32), the verb is not "to possess," but "to know." Knowing is experience, not accumulation. It is something that transforms you from within, not something you add to your identity. Truth is not an object of the mind, but a state of being. That is why, in his encounters, Yeshua does not seek to convince people that they have erred doctrinally, but to bring them into a state of inner authenticity.

With the woman caught in adultery he does not discuss the law, but says: "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" (John 8:11). Truth is not used to condemn, but to free from shame. With Peter, after the denial, he does not discuss the betrayal, but asks three times: "Do you love me?" (John 21:15–17). Truth is not about guilt, but about restoration. Yeshua's truth transforms. It does not classify people into the correct and the mistaken, but calls them into a deeper state of life.

It opens, it does not close. It heals, it does not control. The moment truth is taken out of life and put into formulas, it begins to lose its liberating power. When it becomes a system, it becomes a border. When it becomes dogma, it ceases to be a road. Yeshua says: "I am the Way" (John 14:6). A way is a journey, not a set of propositions. It is something you live, not something you memorize. But when truth is systematized, it is no longer a journey, but a condition of belonging.

It is no longer experience, but a criterion of exclusion. The rupture between Yeshua and institutional Christianity does not begin at the moral or metaphysical level, but precisely here: at the moment when truth was moved from relationship into doctrine, from life into formula, from encounter into system. From here onward, the question is no longer "what is the truth?" but: does the truth you carry make you more free or more closed? Does it make you more humble or more sure of yourself? More compassionate or more judgmental? More alive or more rigid? This is the question Yeshua leaves open. He does not close the discussion through definitions, but opens it through encounters. Dogma, by contrast, tends to close what Yeshua left alive. Yeshua's truth is lived, not possessed. It frees, it does not constrain. It transforms, it does not classify. It opens, it does not close. It heals, it does not control.

## How Did Yeshua Preach the Truth?

The way Yeshua preached the truth is radically different from the way truth is later spoken by Paul and, even more so, by contemporary Christianity. The difference lies, first of all, not in the declarative content, but in the inner position from which the truth is spoken. And this position changes everything. For Yeshua, truth is neither information to be accepted, nor a doctrine to be understood, nor a rule to be followed.

Truth is a light that makes visible what was already there. That is why he does not say, "Believe this about God," but rather invites: "Look." "If your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light" (Matthew 6:22). The call is not to intellectual assent, but to inner sight. And this sight cannot be imposed. Yeshua does not try to convince, does not try to win arguments, does not construct theological systems. He exposes reality as it is, and a person either recognizes it or leaves. "Everyone who is of the truth listens to my voice" (John 18:37). Truth, in Yeshua, is not persuasive, but revelatory. It does not press, does not force, does not argue — it only illuminates. This light has a clear effect: it sets free. "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32).

The essential observation is that the truth spoken by Yeshua does not burden a person with guilt, does not diminish him, does not define him through sin, and does not make him dependent. The Pharisees speak the truth of the Law, and people leave feeling guilty. Yeshua speaks the truth of the heart, and people leave feeling seen. The woman caught in adultery leaves without guilt. Zacchaeus leaves without shame. Peter leaves without condemnation. Because the truth spoken from light does not crush, whereas the truth spoken from Law crushes.

At the same time, Yeshua does not adjust the truth in order for it to be accepted. He does not negotiate it to preserve relationships, and he does not cosmeticize it to avoid reactions. "Woe to you, Pharisees…" (Matthew 23). "Get behind me, Satan!" (Matthew 16:23). "Leave the dead to bury their own dead" (Matthew 8:22). These words are not diplomatic, but neither are they spoken out of aggression. They come from a total inner freedom. Yeshua does not speak the truth to preserve the relationship; he speaks it to preserve reality.

With Paul, a subtle but major shift occurs. Truth begins to be formulated as doctrine. Notions appear such as justification, sin, grace, salvation, correct belief, heresy, sound teaching. Truth is no longer merely a light that reveals, but becomes a structure that must be explained. That is why Paul argues much, explains much, corrects much. Yeshua never explains salvation; he shows it. Paul explains it in his epistles. The difference is also visible in the effect. In Yeshua's presence, the disciples say: "Did not our hearts burn within us?" (Luke 24:32). In the Pauline space, the question becomes: "What is the correct teaching?" The emphasis shifts from living to understanding, from sight to correct belief. This is not necessarily a contradiction, but a change of emphasis that, over time, alters the way truth is perceived and transmitted.

In contemporary Christianity, this shift becomes more pronounced. Truth comes to be preached as morality and religious identity: what you are allowed and not allowed to do, who is saved and who is not, what you must believe, what you must confess, what sins you have. Truth becomes an instrument of moral control. For Yeshua, truth dissolves sin; in modern preaching, truth highlights sin. With Yeshua, a person leaves lightened; in modern preaching, a person leaves weighed down.

Yeshua's invitation, however, remains simple: "Come and see" (John 1:39). Not: "Come and believe." The difference is colossal. Belief can be imposed, learned, repeated. Sight cannot be imposed. It happens. This difference is rooted in the inner state from which Yeshua speaks the truth. "The ruler of this world has no claim on me" (John 14:30). His truth does not wound, because it is not spoken in order to be right, to correct someone, to prove something, or to win an argument. It is spoken from a place where nothing personal is at stake. For Paul, truth defends doctrine. In Christianity, truth defends the faith. For Yeshua, truth defends nothing. It only illuminates. That is why, before him, the Pharisees feel exposed, sinners feel seen, simple people feel awakened — and no one feels condemned.

When Yeshua says "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32), he is not speaking of accumulating correct information, but of an inner state that is being restored. The context is essential: "If you abide in my word… you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:31–32). "To abide" is not intellectual agreement, but existential alignment. The freedom he speaks of is not political freedom, but the inner liberation from what fragments us.

In the same dialogue, Yeshua defines bondage thus: "Everyone who commits sin is a slave to sin" (John 8:34). If sin, for him, is more a state of separation and blindness than a mere list of deeds, then bondage is the inner conflict that makes you live divided: you know the good, but you react out of fear; you desire love, but you act out of defensiveness; you want truth, but you protect yourself through subtle lies. Truth, in Yeshua's sense, is the light that exposes this division without destroying you. Inner unity arises when you no longer have to defend yourself against yourself. That is why he also says: "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matthew 5:8). A "pure heart" is not moral perfection, but a heart that is not fragmented, not divided between image and reality.

Truth purifies precisely by bringing you into a single line: what you think, what you feel, and what you do begin to be coherent. Freedom is the result of this coherence. When Yeshua affirms "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30), he describes the supreme model of unity. In him there is no conflict between desire and will, between outer and inner. That is why he can also say, "the ruler of this world is coming. He has no claim on me" (John 14:30). Where there are no inner fissures, evil finds no leverage. Truth is not a weapon, but integration.

Inner conflict feeds on functional lies: "it doesn't matter," "it doesn't affect me," "I'm not afraid," "I'm fine." When truth is allowed to penetrate, it dismantles these defenses. That is why freedom can be uncomfortable at first. But once fear is seen, it no longer rules; once shame is illuminated, it no longer controls; once desire is acknowledged, it no longer becomes compulsion. The link is direct: truth → exposes the conflict → integrates the broken parts → restores unity → produces freedom. It is not a juridical process, but a healing one. That is why Yeshua does not say "the truth will declare you forgiven," but "will set you free." Freedom is the effect of restored unity.

So the truth that frees is the elimination of inner conflict, not through force, but through illumination. Not through control, but through awareness. Not through rigid discipline, but through integration. Freedom is not the absence of outer tension. It is the absence of inner rupture.

## How the World Becomes Blind and Man Loses Sight — Was Man Created This Way or Did He Go Blind Along the Way?

For Yeshua, blindness is not a natural condition of the human being, nor a spiritual defect with which someone is born. It is a process, a gradual loss of inner sight, which arises when a person begins to live out of fear, attachment, and identification with forms. That is why he asks directly: "Having eyes, do you not see?" (Mark 8:18). The question shows clearly that people have the capacity to see, but have lost it. Sight was forgotten, not refused at creation.

Man is not born blind at the level of consciousness; he learns, over time, no longer to see. This becomes even clearer when the disciples ask him about the man born blind: "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" (John 9:2). The question reflects the religious mentality of guilt and punishment. Yeshua breaks this logic by answering: "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him" (John 9:3). Blindness is not condemnation, but a context in which sight can be restored. Man is not defined by his blindness, but by the possibility of seeing again.

At the individual level, a person begins to go blind when he replaces direct sight with received interpretation. When he lives from what he has been told about reality, rather than from a living encounter with it. Yeshua describes this state when he says of the religious leaders: "They are blind guides; and if the blind lead the blind, both will fall into a pit" (Matthew 15:14). Blindness is not biological, but cultural and religious. A person can be raised in a system that tells him what to see, what to believe, and what to feel, until he loses the capacity to see for himself. Blindness also arises when a person defends his identity more than the truth.

When being right becomes more important than seeing. That is why Yeshua says: "The light has come into the world, and people loved the darkness rather than the light because their deeds were evil" (John 3:19). Blindness is not passive; it is chosen when sight would demand change. The light is rejected not because it is not visible, but because it is uncomfortable. At the inner level, a person loses his sight when he identifies with fear. Fear narrows the field of consciousness until reality is perceived only as a threat. "Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21) shows how attachments determine sight. If the "treasure" is security, control, or image, sight contracts. A person no longer sees what is, but only what defends or threatens him.

This state is described even more deeply in the words: "If the light in you is darkness, how great is that darkness!" (Matthew 6:23). It is not a matter of mere ignorance, but of the confusion between light and darkness. A person comes to believe that he sees precisely when he is completely blinded by his own convictions. This is the most profound form of blindness: false certainty. At the collective level, the world becomes blind when these individual mechanisms become general. When fear becomes political, religious, or cultural. When systems reward conformity, not sight. Yeshua observes this reality, saying: "This people's heart has grown dull, and with their ears they can barely hear, and they have closed their eyes" (Matthew 13:15). Blindness is not imposed from outside, but accepted from within in order to avoid the discomfort of truth.

Yet Yeshua does not leave blindness as the final verdict. He describes his mission thus: "I came into this world for judgment, that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind" (John 9:39). This "judgment" is not punishment, but unmasking. Those who know they do not see are open. Those who believe they see close themselves off. The good news in Yeshua's message is that sight can be recovered. Not through the accumulation of information, but through sincerity toward reality. Not through moral effort, but through the willingness to stop fleeing from truth. When he says he came "that those who do not see may see" (John 9:39), Yeshua shows that man is not condemned to blindness. He was born open, and blindness is only the result of not using this capacity. For Yeshua, to see is not a privilege of the chosen, but the natural state of the human being when he no longer lives out of fear.

## Finding God or Learning to See

In contemporary religious language, people often speak of those who "found God." The expression suggests a search that ends with the discovery of a lost object: a person searches, wanders, and at last finds. But if we read the Gospels carefully, we notice something surprising: Yeshua does not use this formulation. He does not speak of people who find God, but of people who see, enter, are born again, and become. The emphasis falls not on the discovery of God as the object of the search, but on the transformation of the one who searches. The problem is not that God is lost, but that man does not see. That is why Yeshua says: "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matthew 5:8). We notice that he does not say, "Blessed are those who find God," but "those who see him." Here the entire spiritual perspective changes.

God is not a place you reach, nor a doctrine you discover. He becomes visible when a person's inner eye is purified. When the heart is no longer dominated by fear, by the desire to control, by the need to be right, then perception changes and a person begins to see reality differently. This sight is not only a theological perception. It changes the way a person looks at the world and at other people.

The one who sees no longer lives in the perpetual logic of ranking. He no longer obsessively searches for who is right and who is wrong. He no longer classifies people into good and bad, worthy and unworthy, orthodox and heretical. Because the moment a person begins to see, he observes that reality is far deeper than these labels. That is why Yeshua says: "Judge not, that you be not judged" (Matthew 7:1). This is not merely moral advice, but a description of the way one sees whose heart has been purified. Rigid judgment disappears because perception deepens. In the same direction, Yeshua does not speak of finding the kingdom of God, but of entering into it. "Truly, I say to you, unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 18:3). The image of the child is essential here. The child does not constantly rank people, does not obsessively defend his position, and does not live from the need to prove that he is right. He is open, alive, and available. To enter the kingdom means to return to this state of inner transparency, in which life is no longer filtered through pride and comparison.

In the dialogue with Nicodemus, Yeshua uses another radical image: being born again. "Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God" (John 3:3). Once again, the central verb is "to see." Nicodemus comes with theological questions, but Yeshua moves the conversation into an existential dimension. The problem is not that Nicodemus has not found the correct answer, but that his way of perceiving reality must be born again. The old man searches for certainties and classifications; the new man sees life in its unity.

This transformation produces a profound change in the way a person lives. The one who sees is no longer preoccupied with always establishing who is right. He no longer perpetually searches for the other's mistake. He no longer turns truth into a weapon for winning religious disputes. Because he sees that truth is not a position you defend, but a life you live. That is why Yeshua says: "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32). The freedom he speaks of is not the freedom to prove that you are right, but the freedom of no longer being a prisoner of the need to be right.

In many places in the Gospels, Yeshua describes this reality through simple images: a light that is lit, a seed that grows, a vine in which the branches abide. "I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life" (John 8:12). Light does not merely make things visible; it changes the way they are perceived. In the light of truth, a person begins to see people beyond their labels and situations beyond hasty judgments. Thus the Gospels do not describe spirituality as an intellectual discovery in which a person says triumphantly, "I have found God." They describe a transformation of perception in which a person begins to see reality differently.

God is not an object of the search, but a presence that becomes visible when the heart is purified and the inner eye opens. The one who sees no longer lives in the rigid logic of personal righteousness, of ranking, and of perpetual judgment. He becomes free to love, to understand, and to live the truth without turning it into a weapon against others. In this light, Yeshua's words take on a new clarity. God is not found like a lost object. He is seen when a person becomes capable of seeing. And this sight produces not spiritual superiority, but a gentler heart, a quieter mind, and a life that is no longer built on the competition over who is right and who is wrong. To see God means to live in a light in which truth no longer separates, but unites.

## Who Finds Whom? The Surprising Language of the Gospels

Even more surprising is the fact that in many of the parables the direction is reversed. It is not man who finds God, but God who finds man. In the parable of the lost sheep, Yeshua says: "Does he not leave the ninety-nine in the wilderness, and go after the one that is lost, until he finds it?" (Luke 15:4). The shepherd is the image of God, and the lost sheep is man. The message is clear: the initiative belongs to God. He is the one who searches.

The same idea appears in the parable of the lost coin: "Does she not light a lamp and sweep the house and search carefully until she finds it?" (Luke 15:8). Once again, God is the one who searches. In these parables, man is not the hero of the spiritual search. He is the lost one who is found. That is why Yeshua states directly: "The Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10). This reversal profoundly changes the way we understand spirituality.

If man must find God, then everything depends on his intelligence, his effort, or his spiritual technique. But if God seeks man, then the story is different. It is no longer about religious performance, but about the opening of the heart. In this context it becomes clear why Yeshua so often uses the verb to see. "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matthew 5:8). He does not say that they will find him. He says that they will see him. The problem is not that God is hidden, but that man's inner eye is covered. That is why the transformation of the heart becomes the key to perception.

Likewise, in the dialogue with Nicodemus, Yeshua says: "Unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God" (John 3:3). Once again, the emphasis falls not on intellectual discovery, but on transformation. To be born again means to look at the world with new eyes. It does not mean that you have discovered a religious secret, but that your perception has changed. That is why the language of the Gospels is full of dynamic images: a door through which you enter, a light that is lit, a seed that grows. Yeshua says: "I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture" (John 10:9). We notice once again the same movement: entering, going out, life.

God is not an object of the search, but the reality in which life unfolds when a person begins to see. This perspective explains why the expression "I found God" appears more often in later spirituality than in the words of Yeshua. It is a natural way of describing religious experience, but it is not the preferred language of the Gospels. For Yeshua, the problem is not that God must be discovered, but that man must become capable of perceiving what is already present.

Thus the message of the Gospels takes on a surprising clarity. God is not lost somewhere in the universe. He need not be found like a hidden object. Instead, man must learn to see, to enter, to be born again. And, paradoxically, in this transformation he discovers that it was not he who found God, but God who found him.

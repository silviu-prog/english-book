# 9. The Cross, Death, and the Christian Narrative

## The Cross: Consequence, Not Plan

In classical Christianity, the cross becomes the absolute center: the plan of God, the mechanism of salvation, the pivot point of cosmic history. But in the Gospels, when you follow Yeshua step by step, you discover something else: the cross appears as the outcome of an inevitable collision, not as a plan explained, taught, and administered.

Yeshua enters a world structured on fear, purity, status, religious control, and political domination, and what he brings is a freedom that these systems cannot manage. The cross is born of the incompatibility between a man who cannot be bought and a system that cannot tolerate the existence of an unbuyable man.

Yeshua does not preach a God who demands blood in order to be able to forgive. He preaches a Father who already makes the sun rise on the good and the evil alike (Matthew 5:45), a God who seeks out the one who is lost (Luke 15:4–7), not a God who waits for payment in order to change his disposition. If forgiveness can be spoken directly, in front of a paralytic, without altar, without priest, without sacrifice (Mark 2:5), then the cross cannot be interpreted simply as the "price" without which God does not forgive. That would mean Yeshua forgave "illegally" before the price had been paid. It would turn the Gospels into an internal contradiction.

It is more coherent to see the cross as a historical event produced by a religious-political system that, confronted with freedom, responds by elimination. In this sense, the cross is not "the proof that God had to do this," but the proof of how the world reacts to truth.

When Yeshua enters Jerusalem and strikes directly at the heart of the sacred economy—the space in which religion, commerce, and status keep one another alive—the gesture is not "mystical" but one of unmasking. He overturns the tables of the money changers and refuses to let "the Father's house" be a marketplace (Mark 11:15–17). From here on, the conflict is no longer underground but frontal.

In the language of systems, Yeshua becomes a risk: not because he "taught beautifully," but because his teaching produces people who can no longer be controlled through fear. When Peter raises his sword, Yeshua stops him: "Put your sword back in its place" (Matthew 26:52). This shows that Yeshua does not construct his death as a military victory or as an instrumental martyrdom. He does not seek to win through force.

He does not use violence to impose truth, because truth imposed is no longer truth but domination. Yet the refusal of violence has a cost in a violent world: the man who does not defend himself with the same weapons becomes vulnerable. The cross appears as the price of nonviolence, not as a divine requirement. Even the "must" in the passion announcements has this nature: "The Son of Man must suffer" (Mark 8:31).

Not because God demands it, but because this road is the logical consequence of how power works. Truth, when it does not negotiate, triggers the reaction. Love, when it cannot be corrupted, becomes a threat. Yeshua does not glorify suffering. He shows that, in a world built on fear, unconditional love will be interpreted as a danger. If you read the cross as a plan, you are left with a God who needs blood in order to forgive.

If you read the cross as a consequence, you are left with a God who forgives, and the cross becomes the mirror of the world: this is what the world looks like when it meets freedom. It is not a theory. It is a revelation. When Yeshua speaks about the cross, he does so almost exclusively in relation to the disciples. "Whoever wants to come after me must deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me" (Mark 8:34). The cross is not presented as something he does in their place, but as something that discloses the truth about life.

Love without defense, truth without negotiation, and freedom without guarantees have a cost in a world governed by fear. Yeshua does not say "my cross saves you"; he shows what happens when a person lives without fear. The cross is not a theological instrument but a mirror. It does not explain; it exposes.

## Why Yeshua's Death Is Not Explained by Yeshua

For Yeshua, his death is not an object of theological explanation but an event he takes upon himself. He does not construct a doctrine of the cross, and he offers no theory about the necessity of his death. He never says that God demands blood, nor that his death is the condition of forgiveness, nor that it is a legal payment for sin. In his words, life is central, not death. "I have come that they may have life, and have it abundantly" (John 10:10).

To explain death would have shifted the center of gravity from life toward justification, from presence toward mechanism. Yeshua speaks about death only in the language of freedom and self-giving, not of divine necessity. "No one takes my life from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18). This statement does not explain why he must die, but how he enters death. Not as the victim of a heavenly plan, but as a free man who does not negotiate truth in order to save his biological existence. Death is not presented as the goal, but as the possible consequence of fidelity to love.

Another reason Yeshua does not explain his death is that the explanation would immediately have become an object of religious power. A theory about the cross could have been used to control, to make guilty, or to constrain. Yeshua constantly avoids this kind of authority. "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36).

To explain death in causal terms would have turned it into an instrument. His silence protects truth from becoming a weapon. In his teaching, Yeshua always shifts attention from explanation to relationship. He does not say: understand what is going to happen; he says: remain with me. "Remain in me, and I in you" (John 15:4). Death is not something to be understood beforehand, but something to be crossed without fear. Truth is not a logical conclusion but a state of presence.

This is why Yeshua does not prepare the minds of the disciples for a theory of the cross, but prepares their hearts for absence, loss, and disorientation. Yeshua also avoids explanation because his death is an act of disclosure, not an argument. The cross shows what happens when unconditional love meets fear organized into a system. "If they persecuted me, they will persecute you too" (John 15:20).

Death is not necessary in order to convince God; it becomes inevitable in a world that cannot tolerate truth lived to the very end. When Yeshua anticipates death, he does so in existential, not juridical, language. "Unless a grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). This is not an explanation of a divine requirement, but a description of a pattern of life. True life does not defend itself at any price.

It gives itself away. Death is not the goal, but the risk that love takes upon itself in a world governed by fear. Yeshua does not explain his death because the explanation would have come too soon. Before the resurrection, any theory would have been interpreted as failure. After the resurrection, the explanation would have been redundant. The resurrection does not explain death; it relativizes it. "He is not here; he has risen" (Luke 24:6). Meaning is not attached to death, but to the life that passes through it.

In the end, Yeshua does not explain his death because he does not want people to relate to God through fear or duty. He does not say: believe correctly about the cross and you will be saved. He says: follow me. "Whoever wants to come after me must deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me" (Mark 8:34). The cross is not a concept to be understood, but a reality to be taken on when truth becomes more important than safety.

Yeshua's silence is not a lack of meaning, but the protection of meaning. His death is not explained because it does not need to be justified. It does not explain the love of God; it exposes it. And what is exposed is not defended through theory, but recognized through encounter.

## Yeshua Speaks About Death, but Not About Its Theological Function

Yeshua speaks repeatedly about the fact that he will be rejected, will suffer, and will be killed. The announcements are clear and repeated, without ambiguity: "The Son of Man must suffer many things, be rejected… and be killed" (Mark 8:31; Mark 9:31; Mark 10:33–34). Yeshua is not naive about the consequences of the road he is walking.

He sees clearly that truth spoken without compromise provokes reaction, and that inner freedom threatens structures built on fear and control. The essential observation, however, is this: although he speaks about death, Yeshua never speaks about its theological function. He does not say that the Father demands blood; he does not affirm that divine justice must be satisfied; he does not speak of his death as the price of a cosmic debt. His language is not juridical but descriptive.

He says that "the Son of Man must suffer," not because God demands it, but because this is how systems of power react when they are confronted with truth (Mark 8:31). The word "must" appears frequently in Yeshua's speech, but this "must" has no legal or sacrificial sense. "The Son of Man must be lifted up" (John 3:14). "The Scriptures must be fulfilled" (Luke 24:44).

This "must" does not express a demand of God, but a coherence of reality. It describes what happens inevitably when truth is lived without negotiation in a world built on fear, moralism, and control. Yeshua's death is not necessary for God. It does not repair a relationship broken in heaven. It does not change a divine disposition. It becomes inevitable in a world that cannot tolerate inner freedom and undefended love.

"If they persecuted me, they will persecute you too" (John 15:20). This is not a theology of sacrifice, but an anthropology of fear.

## Why Yeshua Refuses to Offer a Complete Explanation

Yeshua refuses systemic explanations because explanations can become substitutes for living. If he had explained his death as a mechanism of salvation, people would have been tempted to believe something about him rather than to follow him. They would have transferred the responsibility for their transformation onto an external event and replaced presence with theology. This is why Yeshua does not say "understand what is going to happen to me," but "follow me" (Mark 8:34). The call is not intellectual but existential.

His silence is not a lack of answer but pedagogy. It does not allow his death to become a mental object before it has been a living confrontation with fear, attachment, and the desire for safety.

## Did Yeshua Die for Our Sins?

The statement "he died for our sins" is one of the most repeated formulas in Christianity. It seems clear, stable, definitive. And yet, when we view it through three different windows—Paul, Yeshua, and John—its meaning takes on unexpected depths. They do not contradict one another, but neither do they say exactly the same thing. Each places the emphasis in a different place: one theological, one existential, one relational. In Paul's writings, the cross becomes the center of the formulation of salvation.

He affirms it without equivocation: "Christ died for our sins, according to the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3). The language is clear, almost juridical. Sin appears as an objective reality, as a real rupture between humanity and God, and Yeshua's death is the act by which this rupture is resolved. From this logic is born the idea that a person is "justified by faith, apart from the works of the Law" (Romans 3:28). The order is clear: first you are forgiven, then you are called to live differently. First comes the objective event of the cross, then the inner process of transformation. The emphasis falls on what Yeshua did for the human being.

If, however, we return to the words and life of Yeshua, we notice something surprising: he never preaches the theory "I will die for your sins." His message is something else: "Repent, for the Kingdom of Heaven is near" (Matthew 4:17).

He speaks of metanoia, of a change in the way of seeing and of living, of a life that comes out of fear and enters into truth. His death is not announced as a payment demanded by God, but as the natural outcome of a life that refuses compromise with violence, power, and falsehood. When he says, "There is no greater love than to lay down one's life for one's friends" (John 15:13), he is not describing a divine transaction, but the nature of love carried to the very end.

In this light, "he died for our sins" takes on a different meaning: Yeshua enters fully into the human condition, bears our rejection, our hatred, our fear, and our violence without responding in kind. On the cross we do not see God demanding satisfaction, but humanity revealing its own blindness and Yeshua revealing a love that does not withdraw. His words, "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34), are not a juridical ritual, but an unmasking of sin and its dissolution in light.

Sin is not "paid for," but exposed in all its reality, and love is not defeated by it. John, in the famous verse, brings a third nuance: "For God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, so that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life" (John 3:16). Here the language is not juridical but relational. God "gives" not in order to demand satisfaction, but in order to reveal himself.

This is why the continuation is essential: "God did not send the Son into the world to judge the world, but in order that the world might be saved through him" (John 3:17). The emphasis falls on the love that is revealed, not on the punishment that is avoided. In John, "to believe" does not mean to accept a theological transaction, but to enter into a living relationship. "Whoever remains in love remains in God" (1 John 4:16). Eternal life does not begin after death; it begins now, as participation in this love.

It is not a future reward but a present state. If we set these three perspectives side by side, a subtle but decisive difference appears. In Paul, the order is: death → forgiveness → transformation. In Yeshua, the order is: truth lived → conflict with the world → death → revelation. In John, the order is: love revealed → faith as relationship → eternal life. They do not exclude one another, but neither do they say the same thing. Paul formulates the theological meaning of the cross.

Yeshua lives the existential meaning. John discloses the relational meaning. Spoken in the language of Yeshua, Paul's statement can be heard differently: he died "for our sins" not in order to pay for them in our place, but in order to show us where they lead. Not in order to change the heart of God, but in order to open ours. The cross is not a gesture by which God is reconciled to humanity, but a mirror in which the human being sees what fear produces and what love produces when it does not withdraw.

In this mirror, the words "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32) take on a concrete meaning. This is why, if we remain only with Paul's formulation, we risk having a doctrine of the cross without transformation. If we listen to Yeshua, the cross ceases to be merely something we believe in and becomes a way of living. "Whoever wants to come after me must deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me" (Mark 8:34) is not an invitation to suffering, but to coming out of fear.

It is not the call to die physically, but to stop living out of the mechanisms that led to the cross. Thus, "he died for our sins" can be read in three registers that complete one another: as sacrifice in the language of Paul, as love carried to the very end in the life of Yeshua, and as an invitation to living participation in the language of John.

And the full meaning lies not in choosing one against the others, but in seeing them together, as three ways of describing the same mystery: love that does not withdraw in the face of sin, but transforms it from within.

## Why Did Yeshua Not Come Down from the Cross?

Perhaps Yeshua does not come down from the cross because, in that moment, there is no longer anything to defend. Not an image. Not status. Not personal vindication. Not even his own biological life. The cross is the place where the ego's last negotiation with existence disappears. It is the total acceptance of loss. And the ego cannot do this.

The ego can temporarily sacrifice something for a greater gain; it can endure if it knows it will be rewarded; it can even suffer if suffering grants it a special identity. But it cannot accept pure loss. It cannot go all the way without the guarantee of recovery. The entire structure of the ego operates through self-preservation. Let me not lose control. Let me not lose validation. Let me not lose love. Let me not lose the image I have of myself. Let me not lose my position.

Let me not lose the meaning I have built for myself. Even many of the religious forms a person adopts preserve the same logic: I humble myself in order to be exalted, I love in order to be loved, I forgive in order to be forgiven, I give up in order to receive more later. The ego knows how to hide even within spirituality. It can turn even sacrifice into an investment.

But in Yeshua there seems to be something that goes beyond transaction. Beyond calculation. Beyond the need to recover something for oneself. This is why the cross is so hard to look at. Because it exposes the fundamental illusion of the human being: the conviction that his existence depends on what he can keep. Perhaps it is precisely here that sin takes on a deeper meaning than the mere breaking of a rule. Sin becomes inner separation. The rupture between a person and what he is before the mask. Before fear. Before the defensively constructed identity.

A person becomes so identified with his mechanisms of defense that he no longer knows who he is without them. He defends his image until he loses his soul. He defends his ego until he separates himself from life. The cross thus becomes the place where this separation begins to die. Not through violence, but through total surrender. Because when a person accepts the possibility of complete loss, something paradoxical appears: an inner unity that the ego can never produce.

As long as you have to win in order to exist, you will live fragmented. You will always be dependent on the outcome. Always at war with reality. Always in fear. Yeshua seems to enter the cross without this inner struggle. This is why he is so silent. The ego constantly needs to justify itself, to prove itself, to defend itself. Truth does not. Truth can be rejected without becoming violent. It can be mocked without hating. It can be crucified without turning into its opposite.

And perhaps the most profound thing is not that Yeshua dies, but that he does not try to save himself through the same logic by which the world is always trying to save itself. The crowd says: "If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross" (Matthew 27:40). But it is precisely here that the difference between the consciousness of the ego and the consciousness of the kingdom is revealed. For the ego, power means the capacity to avoid loss.

For Yeshua, true freedom seems to begin in the moment when you are no longer defined by what you can lose. And yet, the question of the resurrection remains. Because there is a way of understanding the resurrection that completely cancels the meaning of the cross. If the resurrection becomes merely the hero's final triumph, then the cross risks being reduced to a temporary stage before victory. As if the loss had not been real. As if everything had been merely a cosmic strategy for winning.

In this reading, the ego can go on functioning calmly, because it knows that in the end it is the one who will receive the trophy. But perhaps the resurrection is not the return of the glorified ego. Perhaps it is not the psychological "self" that rises. Not the identity that needed to be validated, defended, and continued. Perhaps the resurrection is the revelation that what is real in a human being cannot be destroyed by death because it was never born of the ego.

Then the cross and the resurrection are no longer opposites. The cross is the death of the false. The resurrection is what remains when the false falls away. The cross destroys the illusion of the separate self. The resurrection reveals the life that already existed beyond this illusion. In this light, Yeshua does not "win" after he loses. Because where there is still a need for victory, the ego has not died completely. Rather, through the cross, everything that could be lost disappears.

And through the resurrection, what could never be lost is revealed. Perhaps this is why truth sets free. Not because it offers the ego a more sophisticated spiritual victory, but because it dissolves the very structure that permanently needed victory.

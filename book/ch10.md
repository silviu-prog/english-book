# 10. Yeshua vs. Paul

## Did Yeshua Want a Doctrine Developed After Him? Did He Want a Paul?

Yeshua leaves behind no doctrinal blueprint, but a way of life. He does not ask for theological systematization, but for discipleship; not for metaphysical clarifications, but for transformation. Yeshua never speaks of "developing doctrine." His constant call is: "Follow Me" (Mark 1:17), "Remain in Me" (John 15:4), "Learn from Me" (Matthew 11:29). These are not invitations to theological elaboration, but to living imitation.

Truth, for Yeshua, is not a formula to be refined, but a reality to be lived: "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). Here, "the truth" is not a set of correct propositions, but an existential direction. This is why Yeshua establishes no theological schools, leaves no treatises, and sets up no canon of interpretation. He says explicitly that understanding will remain partial: "I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now" (John 16:12).

This statement is not an invitation to a future doctrine, but a recognition of the gradual maturing of consciousness. Its continuation is entrusted to the Spirit, not to theologians: "The Spirit of truth will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13). The emphasis falls on guidance, not on codification. Did Yeshua want the message to reach the nations? Yes — but not in the form of an exportable doctrine, but as a visible life.

Yeshua says that the news of the Kingdom will reach "to the ends of the earth" (Matthew 24:14), yet He Himself limits His public mission to Israel: "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matthew 15:24). The opening toward the nations appears through concrete examples, not through a systematic missionary plan: the Roman centurion praised for his faith (Matthew 8:10), the Canaanite woman who is heard (Matthew 15:28), the Samaritan held up as a model (Luke 10:33–37).

The universal, for Yeshua, is the recognition of truth wherever it appears, not the imposition of a single framework. "The Great Commission" ("go and make disciples of all nations" — Matthew 28:19) is often cited as a global doctrinal mandate. Even here, however, the language is that of discipleship, not of dogmatizing: "teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you." Yeshua's commandments are ethical and relational (love, forgiveness, truth), not theological schemes. Did Yeshua want an apostle Paul?

In the Gospels, Paul does not appear and is not explicitly anticipated. The Twelve are chosen symbolically (Mark 3:14–19) to represent the reconstitution of Israel, not to inaugurate a worldwide theological elite. Yeshua does not say that other apostles will come with independent revelations, nor does He explicitly open the way for a parallel authority. On the contrary, He warns: "Many will come in My name" (Matthew 24:5), calling for discernment.

## Why Does Paul Claim That Yeshua Appeared to Him?

Paul grounds his authority in a personal revelatory experience: "Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?" (1 Corinthians 9:1; Galatians 1:15–16). For Paul, this experience is equivalent to the apostolic calling. He stresses that he did not receive the gospel "from man" (Galatians 1:12), but through revelation. This statement is decisive: Paul founds his legitimacy not on historical continuity, but on direct experience.

Is Paul's self-proclamation as an apostle legitimate? The answer depends on the criterion used. From the standpoint of religious experience, Paul is consistent: he describes a calling that changes his life radically (Acts 9). From the standpoint of the community, his legitimacy is negotiated: Paul enters into tensions with the apostles in Jerusalem (Galatians 2), is contested ("is he not an apostle?" — 2 Corinthians 10–12), and constantly defends his authority.

This shows that his apostleship was neither self-evident nor unanimously accepted from the start. Moreover, Paul explicitly takes on the role of interpreter and systematizer. He shifts the center of gravity from the lived Kingdom to the Christ-event (the cross and the resurrection) and to justification by faith (Romans 3–5). This shift is decisive for the birth of Christian doctrine. But it is, at the same time, a development that does not appear as such in Yeshua's direct preaching.

From this arises a difference of perspective: Yeshua calls people to follow. Paul calls people to believe in a message. Yeshua lives the truth and invites imitation. Paul explains the truth and demands assent. This does not automatically make Paul's enterprise "illegitimate," but it makes it distinct. Paul does not simply continue what Yeshua said; he interprets and translates the message for a new world, at the price of introducing a more juridical, more doctrinal, more universalizable theology.

From this comes both the force and the tension of later Christianity. Yeshua did not leave a plan for doctrinal development. He left a life to be lived. Paul is not "the voice of Yeshua," but his interpreter. His authority comes from experience and from the reception of the communities, not from an explicit mandate of Yeshua in the Gospels. Today's Christianity rests, in large part, on this Pauline interpretation. To understand this is not to reject Paul, but to avoid confusing him with Yeshua.

## How Yeshua Would Have Seen Someone Like Paul

The first thing Yeshua would have seen in Paul is intensity. Paul is a man of the absolute: before, a persecutor "full of zeal" (Philippians 3:6); after the experience on the road, a herald just as radical. Yeshua does not reject passionate people. On the contrary, He often meets the "fiery" ones (Peter, the sons of thunder) and transforms them. For Yeshua, the problem is not the fire, but the direction of the fire.

That is why He probably would not have condemned Paul for his zeal, but would have asked him: from what does it spring? (cf. Matthew 7:16). Yeshua would also have recognized Paul's real experience. In the Gospels, Yeshua validates experiences that produce transformation: "by their fruits" (Matthew 7:20). Paul's conversion is profound and verifiable: from persecutor he becomes servant; from violent man, sufferer; from self-assured, vulnerable (Galatians 1:13–24; 2 Corinthians 11).

Yeshua does not deny an authentic experience merely because it fails to conform to an institutional structure. He says: "The wind blows where it wishes" (John 3:8). But Yeshua would have paid attention to the language. Here is where the tension appears. Yeshua's language is relational, paradoxical, oriented toward the heart: "follow Me," "do not be afraid," "love." Paul's language is explanatory, juridical, systematizing: justification, condemnation, death, life, faith, flesh, law.

Yeshua does not reject explanation, but He constantly warns against substituting explanation for life: "You search the Scriptures… yet you refuse to come to Me that you may have life" (John 5:39–40). Here Yeshua would have challenged Paul: do you explain life, or do you replace it with explanation? Yeshua would also have noticed the tension of authority. Paul affirms his apostleship through personal revelation (Galatians 1:12) and constantly defends his legitimacy (2 Corinthians 10–12).

Yeshua, however, is reserved toward self-assumed authority: "The greatest among you shall be your servant" (Matthew 23:11). It is not impossible that Yeshua would have called Paul not to prove that he is an apostle, but to rest from the need to prove it. A decisive point would have been the center of the message. Yeshua proclaims the Kingdom of God as a reality lived now (Mark 1:15). Paul proclaims the crucified and risen Christ as the central saving event (1 Corinthians 2:2).

Yeshua would have recognized the value of the testimony about the cross, but He would have asked: are you leading people to live the Kingdom, or to subscribe to an explanation about salvation? For Yeshua, the truth is not merely believed, but followed (John 14:6). Yeshua would probably have been ambivalent: affirming toward Paul's transformation; cautious toward his systematization; demanding toward the effects of his message on people's fear, freedom, and love.

Most certainly, Yeshua would not have rejected Paul as "false," but neither would He have confused him with Himself. In the Gospels, Yeshua distinguishes between the witness and the model. Witnesses speak about the light; the model lives the light (John 1:7–9). Paul is, in this sense, a powerful witness. Yeshua remains the model. Perhaps most important of all: Yeshua would have looked at the historical fruits. Wherever Paul's message brought freedom, courage, and living communities, Yeshua would have said: good.

Wherever it produced fear, guilt, and rigidity, He would have said: here you have misunderstood. For Yeshua, the truth does not defend itself through authority, but is recognized through life. Yeshua would not have seen in Paul a "replacement" for Himself, but a man touched by truth who chose to interpret it. Yeshua does not judge the intention, but He does not negotiate the meaning: a truth that does not set free is not yet the full truth. Yeshua does not seek perfect theologians. He seeks people who, even when they explain, do not cease to live.

## How Faith Is Transformed in Paul

In Paul, faith takes on a different function. The context is essential: Paul is not speaking to a small circle of disciples who knew Yeshua personally, but to scattered, multicultural communities that must be united without the Mosaic Law. In this context, faith becomes the principle of cohesion and justification. For Paul, faith is the human response to the Christ-event: the death and the resurrection.

"One is justified by faith apart from works of the law" (Romans 3:28). Here, faith is no longer merely existential trust, but an act of accepting a saving truth. Not just "I trust," but "I believe that Christ died and rose for me." This shift is decisive. Faith becomes the means by which a person is justified before God. It takes on a formal soteriological function: without faith, there is no salvation.

Inevitably, faith begins to be formulated, preached, defended. The distinction appears between "believing correctly" and "not believing." In Paul, faith is oriented toward the event, not only toward experience. It looks back, toward the cross and the resurrection, and forward, toward justification and eternal life. This structuring makes faith transmissible within the community, but it also distances it from the immediate living we see in Yeshua.

The fundamental difference between Yeshua and Paul is not opposition, but emphasis. In Yeshua, faith is trust that opens life, comes before explanation, is relational, and is situated in the present. In Paul, faith is a response to a saving event, is formulated theologically, and becomes a criterion of belonging. Yeshua says: "Come and see." Paul says: "Believe and you will be saved." Both formulas speak of access to life, but on different levels.

Yeshua works at the level of direct consciousness; Paul works at the level of the community, of history, and of theological reconciliation. Through Paul, faith becomes universalizable. It can be transmitted without the physical presence of Yeshua. Communities can exist, can be united, can endure in time. This is an undeniable gain. But something is lost of the fluidity and simplicity of Yeshua's faith.

Faith risks becoming a condition, not an opening; a criterion, not a process; an identity, not a relationship. When faith becomes something you have, rather than something you live, it can turn from a gate into a wall. Yeshua never asks: "Do you believe correctly?" His question is always: "Do you trust?" So, for Yeshua, faith is the trust that makes life possible. For Paul, faith is the means by which a person is declared saved. Yeshua treats faith as the beginning of the road. Paul treats it as the central element of salvation. Between the two there is no contradiction, but there is a difference of level. The problem arises when Paul's faith completely replaces Yeshua's, and living trust is reduced to doctrinal assent. Yeshua does not ask to be believed. He asks to be followed.

## Why Yeshua Does Not Speak of "Grace," While Paul Speaks of It So Much

One of the most surprising things, when we read the Gospels attentively, is this: Yeshua almost never uses the concept of "grace" as a distinct theological term. Paul, by contrast, will make "grace" (*charis*) one of the central pillars of his theology. This difference is not accidental and is not a matter of vocabulary, but of existential positioning. Yeshua lives what Paul explains. Yeshua embodies the reality that Paul conceptualizes.

For Yeshua, grace is not a subject for preaching because it is the medium in which He lives. He does not speak about grace, just as a fish does not speak about water. His entire relationship with God is one of assumed intimacy, not of negotiation. Yeshua does not begin from the premise that the human being is excluded and then re-included. He begins from an already existing relationship: "Your Father knows what you need" (Matthew 6:8). This statement would be impossible within a theology of the absence of grace.

In the Gospels, what theology will later call "grace" appears under other forms: compassion, mercy, nearness, the absence of judgment, the restoration of dignity. Yeshua says: "The Father makes His sun rise on the evil and on the good" (Matthew 5:45). This is not a definition of grace, but a description of an already active reality. Grace is not offered; it is observed. God does not begin to be good when a person changes. Goodness precedes any change.

That is why Yeshua never says: "You have received grace." He says: "Do not be afraid," "rise up," "come," "follow Me" (Mark 1:17; Matthew 9:2). His language is relational, not conceptual. He does not explain why God loves without conditions; He lives this love publicly, scandalously, visibly. Grace, for Yeshua, is not a doctrine to be accepted, but a reality to be recognized. This positioning also explains the absence of transactional language.

Yeshua does not speak of "merit" and "demerit," of "earning" or "losing" grace. He says: "Your Father is merciful" (Luke 6:36). Mercy is not a juridical exception, but the nature of the relationship. Grace is not the solution to a problem; it is the context in which the problem appears and dissolves. In Paul, the situation is radically different. Paul enters Christianity from within a theology of the Law, of guilt, and of justice.

For him, the fundamental problem is this: how can a just God accept a guilty person? Out of this tension is born the need to conceptualize grace. "By grace you have been saved, through faith" (Ephesians 2:8). Grace appears as a theological solution to a juridical problem. For Paul, grace is necessary because sin is defined as ontological separation: "all have sinned" (Romans 3:23). Grace becomes that which repairs a real rupture between God and the human being.

That is why Paul speaks so much about grace: he needs a concept to counterbalance the Law, condemnation, and the human being's inability to save himself. Grace is the antidote to a severe diagnosis. This shift is decisive. In Yeshua, grace is implicit; in Paul, grace becomes explicit. In Yeshua, grace is the air of the relationship; in Paul, grace is the intervention that changes a person's status. From this comes the juridical language: "justification," "righteousness," "the free gift" (Romans 5:1; 5:15). Grace is no longer the medium of life, but the act by which life becomes possible again. The Christianity that comes after Yeshua will inherit this Pauline paradigm on a massive scale. Grace will become the opposite of the Law, the currency of salvation, the criterion of belonging: you are "under grace" or "outside of grace."

Although grace is said to be free, it often ends up conditioned by correct belief, by adherence to doctrine, by the acceptance of an explanation about the cross. Thus grace ceases to be the medium of freedom and becomes an administrable concept. Yeshua never built a theology of grace because He did not begin from a theology of separation. He did not have to explain why God loves without conditions, because He never doubted this fact.

Paul, by contrast, had to construct a language that would allow the passage from guilt to acceptance without giving up the idea of justice. The final difference is profound. For Yeshua, grace is what is already true about God. For Paul, grace is what makes the truth bearable for a guilty conscience. Yeshua did not preach grace. He lived it. Yeshua did not build a theology of grace because He did not begin from a theology of separation.

One of the most revealing contrasts between Yeshua's message and the Christian theology formulated later is this: Yeshua never felt the need to construct a theology of grace. Not because He ignored the reality of suffering, of going astray, or of evil, but because He never began from the premise that the human being is ontologically separated from God.

Grace, as a doctrine, appears only where separation is considered real, structural, and definitive without a special intervention. In Yeshua's discourse, the relationship between God and the human being is presupposed as existing before any moral correction. He does not speak of a God who is withdrawn, offended, or inaccessible, but of a Father who is present, involved, and near: "Your Father knows what you need before you ask Him" (Matthew 6:8).

This statement would be impossible within a theology of separation. If God were fundamentally distant, grace would have to be explained. In Yeshua, it is not explained because it is not negotiated. That is why Yeshua never says that God begins to love when a person repents. On the contrary, He describes a love that precedes any change: "He makes His sun rise on the evil and on the good" (Matthew 5:45).

This is not a conditional promise, but an observation about the nature of reality. Grace is not a response to sin; it is the background against which sin appears as a going astray. The absence of a theology of grace in Yeshua is directly connected to the way He defines sin. Sin is not, for Yeshua, an ontological state of separation, but a state of not-seeing. "I came so that those who do not see may see" (John 9:39). Blindness does not call for a juridical act of rehabilitation, but for light.

That is why Yeshua does not speak of grace as "forgiveness granted," but of truth that sets free (John 8:32). Liberation does not come from a divine exception, but from the recognition of a reality already present. In Yeshua's parables, God never appears as a judge who must be persuaded to be merciful. The father of the prodigal son is not softened by repentance; he runs out before the speech of guilt is even spoken (Luke 15:20).

The shepherd does not negotiate the value of the lost sheep; he seeks it because it belongs to him (Luke 15:4–7). These images do not describe grace as an exceptional intervention, but as the normality of the relationship. Yeshua does not need to define grace because He does not operate with a God who could, in principle, not be good. Goodness is not a decision, but a nature. "Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful" (Luke 6:36).

The human being is not asked to fulfill conditions in order to receive grace, but to align himself with what is already true about God. Grace is not something that comes from outside; it is something recognized from within. This positioning changes radically with Paul. Paul begins from a different perspective: sin is a universal condition that separates the human being from God ("all have sinned" — Romans 3:23), and the Law makes this separation visible through condemnation.

Within this framework, grace becomes absolutely necessary as a theological solution. If the human being is, by definition, separated, then it must be explained how reconnection is possible. Thus grace appears as an unmerited gift, as a unilateral act of God that repairs a real rupture ("by grace you have been saved" — Ephesians 2:8). In Paul, grace is explicit because the problem is explicit. Grace becomes the answer to guilt, to powerlessness, to the human being's inability to save himself.

The language inevitably changes: justification, righteousness, payment, substitution (Romans 5:1; 3:24–26). Grace is no longer the medium of life, but the intervention that changes a person's status before a just God. The Christianity that comes after Yeshua will inherit this theology of grace precisely because it will also inherit the theology of separation. God is seen as being, by nature, at a distance; the human being is seen as being, by nature, guilty.

Grace becomes the necessary bridge between two separated realities. Although it is proclaimed as free, it often ends up conditioned by correct faith, correct doctrine, and correct belonging. Thus grace ceases to be freedom and becomes a system. Yeshua did not build a theology of grace because He did not need to explain why God is on the side of the human being. He lived this reality without conceptualizing it. In His presence, people did not receive "grace"; they woke up. They rose. They came back to themselves. Grace was not announced, but breathed.

The final difference is this: in Yeshua, grace is what never ceased to be true; in later theology, grace is what becomes necessary in order to repair what was assumed to be broken. Yeshua did not preach grace because He did not preach separation. He preached the Kingdom — a reality already present, which makes grace unnecessary as an explanation and sufficient as life.

## Paul Transforms the Human Ego into a Spiritual Power

Yeshua's statement, "I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven" (Luke 10:18), is often read as a cosmic vision. But in Yeshua's logic, the meaning is simpler and more radical. The ego collapses wherever the truth is lived. The disciples return rejoicing that "the spirits submit to us," and Yeshua immediately shifts the emphasis. Not onto power, but onto liberation from the logic of the adversary. The power of the ego falls not when it is attacked, but when it is no longer fed.

In Paul, this inner reality begins to be externalized. Satan becomes "the god of this age" (2 Corinthians 4:4), a power that "blinds the minds" and part of a cosmic struggle against "the rulers of the darkness" (Ephesians 6:12). What in Yeshua is a structure of consciousness becomes, in Paul, a transpersonal force. The emphasis shifts from clarity to conflict, from awakening to resistance.

This shift makes sense within a context of persecution and historical tension, but it changes the perspective. The human being is no longer called merely to step out of illusion, but to fight an external power. The adversary is no longer something to be seen, but something to be combated.

## In Christianity the Ego Becomes Projected as an Absolute Demon

The Christianity that comes after Paul takes externalization to the extreme. The ego is projected entirely outside the human being in the form of a personalized devil, of demons, and of omnipresent malevolent forces. The result is paradoxical. The ego remains untouched, fear is amplified, responsibility is moved outward, and the struggle becomes permanent. Instead of being dissolved through truth, the ego is fed through religious conflict: "us against them," "good against evil," "God against Satan."

This is exactly the dynamic that Yeshua never uses. In Yeshua, Satan is the ego, the logic of separation. The adversary is internal, the solution is truth and presence, and fear vanishes together with the ego. In Paul, Satan becomes a cosmic power, the adversary is external, the solution is resistance and struggle, and spiritual tension becomes central.

In later Christianity, Satan ends up as an absolute personalized enemy, the ego is projected outward, fear becomes the engine, and conflict is sacralized. For Yeshua, Satan is not something to be defeated, but something to be seen. It is the ego that vanishes the moment the truth is lived. "The ruler of this world has no hold on Me" means that the ego has no grip on a free consciousness. The adversary falls not through struggle, but through the absence of fear.

## The Lists of "Sins" in Paul

Paul begins to enumerate behaviors considered incompatible with life "in Christ" (1 Corinthians 6:9–10; Galatians 5:19–21). These lists appear in concrete communal contexts and serve a disciplinary role. But they produce a major shift: sin becomes a catalogable behavior, not merely a state of the heart. Although Paul affirms explicitly that "love is the fulfillment of the Law" (Romans 13:10), in pastoral and communal practice the emphasis begins to move.

Love remains the declared ideal, but the management of concrete communities requires boundaries, rules, and criteria of belonging. Thus the concern gradually shifts toward defining the borders, establishing norms of behavior, and clarifying what it means to be "inside" or "outside." Love no longer functions only as an inner principle that orients the conscience, but is accompanied by structures meant to protect the unity and coherence of the group.

In this process, the emphasis does not disappear from the discourse, but it begins to be mediated by rules, and belonging becomes an ever more clearly defined reality. Institutional Christianity will take up Paul's language and systematize it until it becomes a stable theological architecture. Sin becomes the central criterion of identity, with the human being defined as "sinful by nature," not merely as a being capable of going astray or of not-seeing.

It becomes the very point of preaching, the place from which the message sets out and toward which religious discourse constantly returns. Within this framework, sin becomes the justification of fear and the reason for institutional mediation: the sacraments, the hierarchies, and the mechanisms of authorization are presented as necessary in order to manage a condition considered structurally guilty.

Out of this systematization develop hierarchies of sins, an obsessive attention given to sexuality, an ever stronger emphasis on guilt and shame, and, in the end, the fear of eternal punishment. Sin is no longer a state to be overcome through awakening and relationship, but becomes an instrument of organization, control, and delimitation.

Thus, what at first was pastoral language meant to explain human fragility ends up structuring a culture of fear, in which the believer's identity is built around guilt rather than around freedom. Within this framework, sin is no longer a state of inattention, but a condemnation. The human being is no longer someone to be awakened, but someone to be corrected. The relationship with God is mediated by the resolution of the problem of sin.

This direction would be foreign to Yeshua, who says: "It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick" (Matthew 9:12). For Him, sin is a sickness to be healed, not a crime to be punished. The difference of perspective is clearly stated when we observe the way sin is understood and, implicitly, the way the human being is understood. In Yeshua, sin is described through existential images such as blindness, going astray, or inattention to the truth, not as ontological guilt.

The problem is not that the human being is evil by nature, but that he does not see, does not hear, or is not present. That is why the solution is not condemnation, but the truth that illuminates, the love that restores the relationship, and the healing that reestablishes life. Forgiveness is unconditional and precedes any correction, because the human being is considered recoverable at any moment, capable of returning when he sees. In Paul, sin takes on another density.

It is no longer merely an individual going astray, but a universal state and a power that acts upon all humanity. The human being is caught in a condition from which he cannot free himself alone, and the solution becomes salvation through Christ as a decisive intervention from outside. Forgiveness is no longer merely a relational recognition, but a soteriological act that changes a person's status. The emphasis shifts from awakening to rescue, from healing to justification.

In later Christianity, this displacement reaches a rigid form. Sin becomes the defining guilt, the central element of human identity. The solution is no longer the encounter that heals, but conformity to a framework and the acceptance of institutional mediation. Fear and shame become the engines of religious life, and the human being is permanently regarded as suspect, always in danger of erring again.

Thus, what in Yeshua was hope and recovery is gradually transformed into a system of control, in which freedom is replaced by vigilance, and truth by fear. In later theological Christianity, sin comes to be defined first of all as an offense committed against God, as an affront to a divine will, a violation of a sacred order or of a revealed law. The emphasis falls on the broken vertical relationship, on accumulated guilt, and on the necessity of a reparation that comes from outside the human being. The human being is regarded as guilty before he is regarded as wounded, and the principal problem becomes his status before God.

In Yeshua, however, sin has a radically different meaning. It is not described as an offense against God, but as a wrong committed against oneself, against one's own life, and against one's inner truth. That is why Yeshua speaks of blindness, going astray, sleep, a hardened heart, and inattention. Sin is not something that wounds God, but something that disintegrates the human being. God is not the one harmed; the human being is the one who loses himself.

In this logic, sin is not a spiritual crime, but a form of existential self-betrayal. To sin means to live beneath the level of one's truth, to separate oneself from life, to break away from one's own conscience. That is why the solution, in Yeshua, is not payment or atonement, but awakening. It is not the satisfaction of a divine justice, but the healing of an inner rupture. Forgiveness does not change God's attitude toward the human being, but frees the human being from self-condemnation and from not-seeing.

Thus, if in Christianity sin is defined as "to wrong God," in Yeshua sin is, more deeply, "to wrong oneself." Not because God would be excluded from the equation, but because God is not separate from life. To lose oneself means to lose oneself from the truth, and the truth is already the place of the encounter with God. In this vision, sin does not call for punishment, but for light, and salvation is not an acquittal, but a return to oneself. Yeshua did not come to convince us that we are sinners. He came to show us where we do not see. For Him, sin is not condemnation, but an invitation to awakening.

When sin becomes more important than life, more important than love, more important than healing, we are no longer speaking of Yeshua's message, but of a theology built after Him.

# 8. Love, Forgiveness, Sin

## Love in Yeshua

At the center of Yeshua's message stands love—but not as doctrine, not as an isolated moral virtue, and not as an abstract ideal. Love, for Yeshua, is a way of being. It is not defined; it is lived. It is not preached as a norm but revealed as a state of consciousness. This is why Yeshua does not offer a theory of love but creates situations in which love becomes visible: he touches the one who is excluded (Mark 1:40–42), he sits at table with the one who is despised (Mark 2:15–17), he refuses violence even when it would be justified (Matthew 26:52).

Love is not something you "must" do; it is something that happens when fear disappears. For Yeshua, the opposite of love is not sin but fear. Every time he says "do not be afraid" (Matthew 10:31; Luke 12:32; John 14:27), he implicitly opens the space in which love can arise. Love is not commanded as a moral obligation; it becomes possible as the effect of inner freedom.

This is why the commandment to love one's enemies (Matthew 5:44; Luke 6:27–36) is not a superhuman requirement but a consequence of disidentification: if you no longer have a self to defend, you no longer have an enemy to hate. Love, in Yeshua, is not conditioned by the behavior of the other but by your own state of presence. In his relationship with sin, Yeshua does not operate from a juridical logic. He does not love "in spite of" sin, nor "in order to correct" sin. He sees the person beyond the label.

"Neither do I condemn you" (John 8:11) is not the suspension of truth but the refusal to reduce a human being to their mistake. Love, here, is not moral tolerance but clear sight: Yeshua does not deny the reality of wounds, but he refuses to turn them into identity. In Paul, love takes another form. Not because Paul is "less spiritual," but because he operates in a different register: that of organizing the community and systematizing the message.

Paul speaks of love as the supreme virtue, as the fulfillment of the Law, as the bond of the community (Romans 13:8–10; Colossians 3:14). The famous hymn to love in 1 Corinthians 13 is profound, beautiful, and true, but it is already a definition. Love becomes something that can be described, evaluated, measured: "love is patient, love is kind…" (1 Corinthians 13:4–7). This shift is subtle, but essential.

In Yeshua, love is a state; in Paul, love becomes a criterion. In Yeshua, love springs from truth ("I am the truth"—John 14:6); in Paul, love comes to be integrated into a normative framework. Although Paul affirms that love is greater than faith and hope (1 Corinthians 13:13), he nevertheless inserts it into a doctrinal architecture: there is a love that "builds up" (1 Corinthians 8:1), a love that functions within the body of the community (1 Corinthians 12).

While Yeshua dissolves the boundaries of identity through love—"you are all brothers" (Matthew 23:8), "there is neither Jew nor Samaritan," implicit in his parables (Luke 10:33–37)—Paul is concerned with maintaining the cohesion of the community. Love becomes an instrument of order: "do not put a stumbling block in another's way" (Romans 14:13), "maintain the unity of the Spirit" (Ephesians 4:3), "all things should be done decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40).

Not because Paul is authoritarian, but because he is speaking to a community that risks falling apart. In this context, love begins to be associated with responsibility, with discipline, with conformity to the common good. Here the major difference appears: in Yeshua, love is free and unlocated; in Paul, love is contextualized within the community. Here begins the difference that, once seen, can no longer be ignored. In Yeshua, love has no purpose; in Paul, love has a function.

It "builds up" (1 Corinthians 8:1), it "unites" (Colossians 3:14), it "covers" (1 Corinthians 13:7). This functionalization is the beginning of dogmatization: love is no longer merely the expression of truth lived, but becomes the virtue that sustains the system. In time, this Pauline love will be taken up by the institution and transformed into a moral obligation. And, without our noticing, something changes. Love no longer flows from freedom; it begins to come from conformity.

And here something essential is lost: "Love one another" becomes a normative imperative (Ephesians 5:1–2), associated with obedience, good order, and sound doctrine (Titus 2:1–2). Thus love risks becoming conditional: we love "correctly," we love "responsibly," we love "with discernment." Love no longer flows from freedom but from conformity. Yeshua never says "love so that you may be saved."

He says: "By this everyone will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another" (John 13:35)—love as the sign of a reality already present. Paul, without saying so explicitly, comes to bind love to the life of the saved community and to a walk "worthy of the calling" (Ephesians 4:1–2). In Yeshua, love is the sign that the Kingdom is here (Luke 17:21). In Paul, love becomes the proof of spiritual maturity (1 Corinthians 14:20; Ephesians 4:13).

This difference shifts the emphasis: from experience to evaluation, from presence to criterion. It is not a matter of radical opposition, but of a displacement of meaning. Yeshua lives love from within truth; Paul explains love from within structure. Yeshua frees love from fear ("do not be afraid"—Luke 12:32); Paul tries to protect love from chaos ("God is not a God of disorder"—1 Corinthians 14:33).

One is love as revelation; the other is love as an organizing virtue. In this sense, Yeshua's love cannot be dogmatized without being lost. It cannot be imposed, defined, or administered. It appears only where a person no longer lives out of self-defense. Pauline love can be taught, learned, demanded. Yeshua's love can only be recognized when it happens.

The final difference is this: in Yeshua, love is what remains when fear disappears (John 4:18); in Paul, love is what must be preserved so that the community does not fall apart (Ephesians 4:15–16). Both speak about love. But only one speaks from love.

## Empathy in Yeshua

Empathy, in its deepest sense, is not agreement with another's deed, nor the suspension of discernment, but the capacity to enter into the lived reality of a person without reducing them to their mistake. In this light, the differences between Yeshua, the Apostle Paul, and contemporary Christianity become visible not merely as theological nuances, but above all as different ways of relating to a human being. In Yeshua, empathy precedes any moral framework.

He does not begin by defining sin; he begins by seeing the person. The encounter with the woman caught in adultery (John 8) is emblematic: Yeshua does not contest the existence of sin, but he refuses to let sin be her identity. His silence before her accusers suspends the inner tribunal of everyone present. Only after the woman is rehumanized does the invitation appear: "Go, and sin no more." Correction comes after empathy, not in its place.

In this sense, Yeshua operates from a consciousness in which moral judgment is not the primary instrument but the natural consequence of truth lived. His empathy is not sentimental; it is radical presence. He enters into the other's story without analyzing, labeling, or ranking it. This is why Yeshua can say "do not judge" without relativizing good and evil: judgment blocks access to the heart, and without the heart there is no transformation.

Paul shifts the emphasis from the living encounter to the structuring of the community. He writes letters in order to organize, correct, delimit. Here a subtle change appears: empathy is present ("weep with those who weep"), but it is subordinated to doctrinal truth. A person is loved insofar as they align with the truth proclaimed. For Paul, the danger is not only suffering but going astray. This is why his language frequently introduces moral evaluation and explicit correction.

Empathy becomes contextual: it is granted to the one who acknowledges the framework. Where Yeshua says "come and see," Paul often says "come and set yourself right." Not from a lack of love, but from a change of mission: from revelation to preservation.

## Christianity Today: Empathy Lost in Moralism

Contemporary Christianity has taken up above all the Pauline instruments—dogmas, rules, delimitations—but often without the heart of Yeshua that would have made them alive. Thus empathy is confused with weakness, and judgment with fidelity to the truth. A person is analyzed "in terms of morality," and this analysis automatically excludes empathy, because it turns the other into a case rather than a being. When morality becomes a filter, the relationship breaks.

To say "you have sinned" without feeling the inner burden of the one standing before you is not liberating truth but moralizing. Dogma, used as a shield, offers identity-security but blocks the encounter. This is why many Christians can speak correctly about sin and completely wrongly about the human being. As we discussed earlier, judgment presupposes a position of superiority, while empathy requires existential equality.

The moment you position yourself "above" in order to evaluate, you can no longer enter the other's story. Dogma, when absolutized, offers answers before asking questions. Empathy, by contrast, listens before knowing. Yeshua never began a conversation with "the truth says that…," but with presence. Paul began to do so out of communal necessity. The Christianity of today has often been left with only the proposition, and not the Spirit. And without the Spirit, truth becomes a weapon.

Empathy does not exclude correction; it makes it possible. In Yeshua, truth comes from a heart that has already been there, in the other's suffering. In Paul, truth comes from concern for good order. In present-day Christianity, truth often comes from fear—the fear of losing control, identity, certainty. Where there is judgment, empathy stops. Where there is rigid dogma, the heart closes.

And without empathy, Christianity ceases to be good news and becomes only a cold correction of behavior. Yeshua did not come to tell us what we are, but to see us. From there every healing begins.

## Forgiveness in Yeshua

Forgiveness, in Yeshua's message, is neither a juridical mechanism nor a moral transaction between human and God. It does not appear as a response to a tallied guilt, but as the effect of an inner realignment. When Yeshua says "your sins are forgiven," he is not making an administrative announcement about a person's moral status; he is describing a reality that is already beginning to manifest: the person no longer lives separated from God through fear, shame, and self-condemnation.

This becomes clear in the episode of the paralytic lowered through the roof. Before any physical healing, Yeshua says: "Son, your sins are forgiven" (Mark 2:5). The immediate reaction of the scribes is revealing: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7). Their question already presupposes a juridical theology: sin is an offense against God, and forgiveness is an act of vertical authority.

Yeshua does not directly deny this logic, but he undermines it from within. He shows that the human being's problem is not, first of all, guilt, but the inner rupture that keeps them paralyzed. This is why forgiveness precedes healing. Not because illness is a punishment for sin, but because shame, fear, and self-exclusion create a state of closure that blocks life. "Rise and walk" (Mark 2:11) becomes possible only after a person no longer sees themselves as separate. Forgiveness, in Yeshua, is the restoration of the flow of life, not the erasure of a moral record. When Yeshua says "your sins are forgiven" to the woman known as a "sinner" (Luke 7:48), he does not condition forgiveness on formal repentance, confession, or sacrifice. He observes that her great love is the sign of an openness already present: "her sins are forgiven, for she loved much" (Luke 7:47). The order is essential.

She does not love because she has been forgiven; she is forgiven because love has dissolved the separation. Forgiveness does not produce love; love makes forgiveness possible. Here a crucial question arises: did Yeshua have the "power" to forgive sins? In the religious logic of the time, forgiveness was tied to the temple, sacrifice, and priesthood. Yeshua breaks this tie. He does not send people to ritual in order to be forgiven; he brings them back into relationship.

The power he exercises is not juridical but revelatory: he makes visible the fact that God is not separated from the human being in the way the human being believes themselves separated from God. "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). From this perspective, forgiveness is not a one-time act but a state of remembering unity. The human being is not forgiven in order to be able to love; the human being loves because they begin to live from a place in which separation is no longer real.

This is why Yeshua does not condition love on the forgiveness of sins. He never says: "you cannot love until you are forgiven." On the contrary, he shows that love itself is the way out of sin. This leads to another uncomfortable question: can we be in love without our sins being "forgiven"? In Yeshua's language, yes—because love is precisely the space in which sin loses its power.

Sin, in the original sense of the term (*hamartia*), means "to miss the mark," to live from a mistaken perception of reality. When a person lives in love, the mark is no longer missed. Not because the person becomes "without fault," but because they no longer live from separation. Yeshua does not speak of a God who keeps a record of sins in order to decide whether a person deserves love.

He says explicitly: "Your Father is merciful" (Luke 6:36) and "God did not send the Son into the world to condemn the world, but in order that the world might be saved through him" (John 3:17). Judgment, in Yeshua's vision, is not an external verdict but the natural consequence of living in fear: "the light has come into the world, and people loved the darkness" (John 3:19). It is not God who separates; it is the human being who separates themselves.

This vision contrasts sharply with the one formulated by Paul and then taken up by the Christian doctrine that followed Yeshua, down to our own day. In Paul, sin becomes a cosmic power that ontologically separates the human being from God: "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23). Forgiveness becomes necessary in order to repair this separation, and Yeshua's death is interpreted as a juridical act of satisfying divine justice (Romans 3:25; 2 Corinthians 5:21).

In this paradigm, love is no longer enough. Even if you love, you remain separated until your sins are forgiven through an external act: correct faith, acceptance of the sacrifice, belonging to the saved community. Later Christian doctrine will consolidate this logic: God loves, but cannot receive the human being without resolving the problem of sin; forgiveness becomes the condition for full love. Yeshua never says this.

He does not affirm that sin separates the human being from God ontologically. He says that sin separates the human being from life. The difference is enormous. In Yeshua, God remains present even in our wandering: "the Father makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good" (Matthew 5:45). The separation is not relational on God's part, but perceptual on the human being's part. This is why forgiveness is necessary not in order to change God's attitude, but in order to heal the human being's perception.

A person needs to hear "your sins are forgiven" because they live in constant self-judgment. The voice that condemns is not the voice of God, but the voice of internalized fear. Yeshua does not negotiate with this voice; he deactivates it. In Christian doctrine today, the emphasis often falls on final judgment, on definitive separation, on the verdict. In Yeshua's message, the emphasis falls on restoration, on return, on remembrance.

The parable of the prodigal son is essential here: the father does not wait for atonement, does not demand explanations, does not pronounce forgiveness as a sentence. He runs, embraces, and restores the status of son before the son finishes his speech of guilt (Luke 15:20–24). In Yeshua, forgiveness is not the condition of love but its expression; sin does not separate the human being from God, but makes them believe they are separated; God does not judge in order to exclude, but illuminates in order to heal.

Forgiveness is not a bureaucratic gate to love. It is the moment in which a person ceases to exclude themselves from it.

## "Father, Forgive Them, for They Know Not What They Do" — The Anatomy of Unconsciousness in Yeshua

On the cross there is a sentence that concentrates, as in a seed, the entire inner pedagogy of Yeshua. While he is mocked, beaten, and crucified, he speaks the words recorded in Luke 23:34: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do." This phrase is not a sentimental appeal, nor a figure of speech. It is a precise spiritual diagnosis of the human condition and, at the same time, an indication of the only therapy that can heal that condition.

For Yeshua, "unconsciousness" does not mean a lack of information. The people at Golgotha know very well what they are doing on the external level: they strike, they spit, they mock, they crucify. Their unconsciousness is not cognitive but inner. They do not see the state out of which they do all this. They do not see their fear, their pride, their attachment to power, their need for control, their total identification with their roles—"soldier," "priest," "crowd." They do not see the automatic mechanisms that drive them.

And because they do not see them, they mistake them for justice, for duty, for the Law, for the will of God. This is unconsciousness in Yeshua's language: to be possessed by your own mechanisms without knowing you are possessed by them. When he says "they know not what they do," Yeshua is not saying that they fail to realize they are killing an innocent man. His statement is far deeper: they do not know from what inner place they are doing this. They believe they are defending order, religion, God, the application of the Law.

In reality, they are discharging fear, anger; they are defending their ego, their system, their identity. And this confusion is so total that there is no distance between them and the mechanisms that animate them. From here the essential question is born: why does Yeshua not say "Show them, Lord"? Why does he not ask for immediate revelation for them? Because to show them, in that moment, would have produced shame, guilt, humiliation, and a forced exposure of the ego.

And this does not awaken consciousness; it activates defense. A person caught in their mechanism, confronted with it brutally, does not say "you are right," but "no, we are right." The mechanism is still functioning, so it defends itself. This is why Yeshua's solution is not confrontation but forgiveness. For him, forgiveness is not a moral gesture but a therapeutic one. To forgive means to no longer resist the other's mechanism, to no longer press it, to no longer force it, to no longer expose it.

It means to let time extinguish the anger, the fear, and the exhaustion of the ego, until a person can arrive on their own at the point where they say: "What have I done…?" If you show them beforehand, they defend themselves. If you forgive them, you create the space in which, later, they can see themselves. Yeshua's order is clear: first forgiveness—that is, the calming of the inner field. Then awareness becomes possible. Only at the end can the truth be seen. Conversely, truth thrown over an unforgiven person produces only defense.

In this light, the unconscious person is the one who believes they are right when they do evil, who feels moral when they are violent, and who believes they are defending God in the very moment they crucify him. They see no contradiction, because they have no access to the inner mechanism that drives them. This is why Yeshua does not say "they know not that they do," but "they know not *what* they do."

It is not the absence of the deed that is the problem, but the absence of seeing the state from which the deed proceeds. The essence of Yeshua's perspective thus becomes clear: unconsciousness is total identification with the inner mechanism; sin is to act from it without seeing it; and the solution is not confrontation but forgiveness—because forgiveness offers time. Time in which consciousness can appear without pressure, without shame, without defense. On the cross, Yeshua does not ask for revelation for them. He asks for time for them.

## Forgiveness as the Withdrawal of Conflict, Not the Rewriting of Morality

In the words spoken on the cross and recorded in Luke 23:34—"Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do"—Yeshua does not propose a new morality, nor does he relativize evil. He does not say that what is happening is not grave, nor that violence, mockery, and injustice become acceptable. What he does is far more subtle and far more profound: he withdraws the conflict between himself and those who crucify him, without withdrawing the truth about the deed. This distinction is essential.

Ordinarily, when we see an evil, our spontaneous reaction is to enter into conflict with the person who commits it. Not only to name the deed as evil, but to identify the person with the evil produced. In that moment, our struggle is no longer only with the deed, but with the person. And this struggle becomes the fuel for the inner mechanisms of both. Yeshua breaks this circle. He leaves the deed in its truth, but he refuses to enter into conflict with the people who do it.

This is why he does not say "what they are doing is not evil," but "they know not what they do." In this way, evil is not denied, but the person is no longer reduced to the evil they do. This attitude harmonizes with his entire teaching, including the radical call in Matthew 5:44: "Love your enemies." To love your enemy does not mean to declare that the enmity is not real, but to refuse to turn the person into the enmity itself. It means to withdraw the personal conflict, without rewriting morality.

When you tell an unconscious person "what you are doing is evil," they often hear "you are evil." The identification hardens, and the inner mechanism defends itself. When, however, you see that the person is driven by mechanisms they do not see, a radical change appears: you no longer struggle with them. Not because the deed has become good, but because you see that the direct struggle with the person feeds the very mechanism that produces the deed. Forgiveness, in this sense, is the withdrawal from this struggle.

It is the refusal to keep feeding the conflict that keeps the mechanism alive. The inner mechanisms—fear, pride, the need for control, shame—live on opposition, tension, guilt, and confrontation. When someone no longer enters this game, the mechanism is left without fuel. This is the profound meaning of forgiveness in Yeshua. It is not a declaration that evil is no longer evil. It is a declaration that the person is no longer treated as identical with the evil they do.

The evil remains named as evil, but the personal conflict is withdrawn. In the light of this understanding, it becomes clear why Yeshua does not ask "Show them, Lord." A forced revelation would have produced shame and defense. Instead, forgiveness creates the space in which, when the inner mechanism quiets, the person can come on their own to see the truth about their deed. Thus forgiveness is not the weakening of morality, but the condition in which morality can be seen without defense.

It is the withdrawal of conflict that allows the appearance of consciousness. The evil remains evil, but the person is no longer held captive in the identity of "evil." This is the liberating force of forgiveness in Yeshua's teaching: it does not change the truth about the deed; it changes our relationship with the person who did it.

## Yeshua Rebukes Rarely

In the Gospels, Yeshua rarely rebukes vulnerable persons and often rebukes rigid structures. His rebuke does not aim to make anyone "feel small," but to bring to light the mechanism in which someone is caught. When he says to Peter "Get behind me, Satan" in Matthew 16:23, he does not humiliate Peter; he unmasks the source of his thinking in that moment. Peter is not reduced to his mistake; immediately afterward, he remains in Yeshua's proximity.

When he pronounces the series of "woes" against the Pharisees in Matthew 23, the rebuke is public and cutting, because the target is not fragile but a system consolidated in hypocrisy. There the rebuke serves to break the moral armor into which nothing any longer penetrates. By contrast, toward those caught in shame, Yeshua does not rebuke but creates space. To the woman caught in adultery he says "Neither do I condemn you" in John 8:11.

To Peter after his denial he does not deliver a sermon, but restores him through a gentle dialogue in John 21:15–17. In Yeshua, rebuke appears when a person is identified with a rigid, powerful mechanism. And even then, the aim is not punishment but unmasking. In Paul, rebuke takes on a more organizational role. The communities are fragile, moral and doctrinal slippages appear, and he uses rebuke as an instrument for preserving order.

In Galatians 2:11, Paul says that he rebuked Peter "to his face, because he stood condemned." Here the rebuke is public and direct, because what is at stake is the influence over the community. In 2 Timothy 4:2, Paul explicitly urges: "reprove, rebuke, exhort." Rebuke becomes part of the pedagogy of the spiritual leader. In Paul, rebuke is no longer so finely calibrated to the inner state of the person, but to the effect of their behavior on the community.

The emphasis shifts from healing the mechanism to correcting the behavior. In many forms of contemporary Christianity, rebuke has become predominantly moral. It aims to show a person that they are wrong, often with the intention of making them feel guilty so that they will change. Here an inversion of Yeshua's logic appears. Rebuke no longer unmasks the mechanism; it labels the person. It no longer creates space for consciousness; it activates shame and defense. This is why, in practice, modern rebuke often produces outward conformity or inward rebellion, but rarely consciousness. In Yeshua, rebuke is rare, precise, and directed toward the mechanism, not toward the person's identity. In Paul, rebuke is a necessary instrument for the health of the community. In the Christianity of today, rebuke is frequently used as a moral instrument, losing its original therapeutic finesse.

This is why, in Yeshua, rebuke can coexist with forgiveness without contradiction. Because it does not enter into conflict with the person, but brings to light what holds them captive.

## Conflict in Yeshua

The image of Yeshua is often reduced either to a gentleness without edges, or to a prophet permanently in opposition. Both directions miss the reality in the Gospels: Yeshua does not seek conflict, but he does not avoid it when truth and life are threatened. His conflict is asymmetrical. It is not with people, but with the structures that produce fear, exclusion, and falsehood.

While in Paul conflict shifts toward the defense of the message and the community, in later Christianity conflict often becomes identity and, sometimes, even strategy. Yeshua frequently enters into conflict, but not out of reaction. He does not engage in polemics to win debates, and he does not seek to humiliate his adversaries. When conflict appears, it appears as a side effect of truth lived.

"You have heard that it was said… but I say to you" (Matthew 5) is not gratuitous provocation but a reorientation of consciousness. His conflicts are oriented vertically—truth versus falsehood—not horizontally, us versus them. This is why he can be extremely direct with the religious leaders ("Woe to you…", Matthew 23) and at the same time profoundly empathetic with the marginalized. The target is not the persons, but the roles and systems that weigh down on life.

"The Sabbath was made for man" (Mark 2:27) becomes a frontal confrontation with legalism, without hatred and without fear. Yeshua avoids escalation. When he is asked for a sign out of distrust, he refuses: "An evil and adulterous generation seeks for a sign" (Matthew 12:39). When he is pushed toward a political stance, he withdraws: "Jesus, knowing that they intended to come and take him by force to make him king, withdrew again to the mountain by himself" (John 6:15).

When Peter draws the sword, Yeshua stops him: "Put your sword back in its place" (Matthew 26:52). His conflict does not seek victory, but clarity. This is why "Blessed are the meek" (Matthew 5:5) is not opposed to firmness, but is its foundation. This attitude is seen most clearly in the way Yeshua redefines conflict through active non-violence: "Love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44). This is not capitulation, but a way out of the spiral of reaction.

Truth is not defended by force; it is shown through presence. "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36) clarifies the method: without coercion, without demonization. In the maximum moment of conflict—the cross—Yeshua remains within this logic: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). The conflict does not turn into hatred; it remains lucidity. In Paul, the context shifts the emphasis. Fragile communities, external pressures, and internal confusions demand delimitation.

Conflict becomes an instrument for protecting the message. Paul polemicizes harshly against "a different gospel" (Galatians 1:6–9), defends apostolic authority (2 Corinthians 10–12), and draws moral and doctrinal boundaries. The language of battle appears as metaphor: "Fight the good fight of the faith" (1 Timothy 6:12). His conflict is rhetorical and normative: letters, warnings, communal discipline. The aim is not to humiliate the person, but to preserve cohesion and fidelity to the message.

"Purge the evil person from among you" (1 Corinthians 5:13) shows this protective function. This shift is pragmatic and necessary for survival, but it changes the perspective. Conflict is no longer only the effect of truth lived; it also becomes a means of preserving order. Truth is no longer only shown; it is also defended. Tension begins to be accepted as the price of stability. In later Christianity, this displacement intensifies.

Conflict is no longer the exception but the norm. The defense of doctrine, the combating of "heretics," the maintenance of boundaries become central aims. The language of battle becomes normalized, and fear becomes the fuel. Yeshua's essential distinction between person and system is lost. People come to be treated as problems. Gentleness becomes suspect, and non-violence seems naïve. "They tie up heavy burdens and lay them on people's shoulders" (Matthew 23:4) takes on a painful resonance.

The difference of perspective becomes clear. In Yeshua, conflict is not sought, but it becomes inevitable when life is being defended. "The light has come into the world, and people loved the darkness rather than the light" (John 3:19). The target is the falsehood that holds a person captive, not the person themselves. The method remains consistently non-violent: truth spoken, presence assumed, the refusal to answer evil with evil. "I came that they may have life" (John 10:10) shows the aim: life, not victory.

In Paul, conflict protects the community and the message. In later Christianity, conflict comes to protect the system. There the inversion occurs. When conflict defends life, it is legitimate. When conflict defends the structure, it becomes an idol. Yeshua was not non-conflictual, but non-violent. He did not avoid tension; he refused hostility. Truth, for him, has no need of wars in order to remain truth.

It remains truth through what it produces: life, freedom, and whole human beings.

## Temptation in Yeshua

In the Gospels, Yeshua is "tempted" (Matthew 4:1–11), but the temptation is not associated with shame or guilt. On the contrary, the episode in the wilderness has an initiatory character. The temptation does not defile Yeshua; it reveals the perspective from which he lives. It appears not as a moral threat, but as an existential clarification. The three temptations in the wilderness do not propose obvious immoralities, but apparently reasonable solutions.

The first proposes survival without trust—the reduction of life to immediate need: "Tell these stones to become bread" (Matthew 4:3). The second proposes security without responsibility—the forcing of God as guarantee: "Throw yourself down" (Matthew 4:6). The third proposes power without truth—control as a shortcut: "I will give you all the kingdoms" (Matthew 4:9). For Yeshua, temptation is the voice of the ego, the logic of separation that says: solve it yourself, secure yourself, take control.

His response is not struggle but clarity. He sees the temptation and refuses it through truth—"It is written…" (Matthew 4:4, 7, 10). Temptation becomes the place where the ego is unmasked. This is why Yeshua can say: "The ruler of this world has no claim on me" (John 14:30). To be tempted does not mean to be weak, but to be set face to face with a choice of perspective.

The Lord's Prayer—"lead us not into temptation" (Matthew 6:13)—does not ask for the avoidance of difficult situations, but for protection from the logic of separation. In Yeshua, temptation is the danger of exchanging trust in the Father for the solutions of the ego. This is why he warns: "Watch and pray, that you may not enter into temptation" (Matthew 26:41). To "enter" into temptation means to adhere to the narrative of fear. Yeshua does not moralize temptation; he contextualizes it. Temptation is a proposal, not a guilt.

What matters is what you live from when it appears. This is why, in Yeshua, temptation does not define the person; the perspective from which they respond does. In Paul, temptation takes on a different tone. He recognizes its reality and promises help: "God will not let you be tempted beyond your strength" (1 Corinthians 10:13). Temptation becomes a test of fidelity and discipline. The tension between "flesh" and "Spirit" appears (Romans 7–8). The emphasis shifts from clarity to resistance.

Temptation is something to be overcome, not merely to be seen. Although Paul affirms that the struggle "is not against flesh and blood" (Ephesians 6:12), the language becomes militarized: armor, resistance, perseverance (Ephesians 6:13–17). The ego as an inner structure is less thematized; temptation is externalized as pressure or force. This shift helps fragile communities, but it changes the perspective: the spiritual life becomes a regime of tension.

Temptation is no longer an occasion for seeing, but a threat to salvation. In institutional Christianity, temptation is often hypermoralized (especially in the area of sexuality), externalized (the devil "attacks you"), personalized (guilt and shame), and made permanent (anxious vigilance). The ego is no longer recognized as the source, but projected outward. The paradoxical result is that fear grows and the ego strengthens.

Temptation comes to be seen as proof of weakness, not as an invitation to awakening. The spiritual life becomes avoidance, not responsibility. This vision is foreign to Yeshua, who says: "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32). For him, temptation falls away when the falsehood is seen. In Yeshua, temptation is the proposal of the ego; its nature is existential, not moralistic; the solution is clarity and trust; temptation becomes a place of revelation.

In Paul, temptation is test and conflict; the emphasis falls on resistance and discipline; the language of battle appears; the community is protected. In later Christianity, temptation becomes an omnipresent threat; fear and shame are amplified; responsibility is externalized; the ego remains unseen. For Yeshua, to be tempted does not mean to be close to falling, but close to truth. Temptation is the moment in which the ego makes its offer. When the offer is seen, its power disappears.

It is not through struggle that temptation is passed, but through clarity. Temptation does not define you. The perspective from which you live does.

## Sin According to Yeshua

When we read the New Testament attentively, we discover that the term "sin" is used for different realities, depending on the author who is speaking. Yeshua uses the language of sin rarely, relationally, and therapeutically; Paul transforms it into a central theological category; and later Christianity fixes it within a moral-juridical system. This is not a matter of simple terminological evolution, but of a change of perspective that modifies the image of the human being, of God, and of salvation.

For Yeshua, sin is neither a list of broken prohibitions nor an inherited ontological state. He never says that the human being is born sinful or separated from God. On the contrary, he speaks of people as children of the Father (Matthew 5:45) and of a relationship that already exists, not one that has been lost.

In Yeshua, sin is not defined juridically and is not formulated as an accounting violation of a divine law; it is described through profoundly existential images that speak of the human being's condition. He calls it blindness, not deliberate guilt: "If you were blind, you would have no sin" (John 9:41), suggesting that the problem is not intentional wickedness but the incapacity to see.

He describes it as wandering, through the image of the lost sheep (Luke 15:4–7), where sin is not rebellion but a loss of direction. He speaks of spiritual sleep, of the dozing of consciousness (Matthew 25:5), of a hardened heart—that is, an inner rigidity that can no longer receive the truth (Mark 8:17)—and of a radical inattention to reality, summed up in the words from the cross: "they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34).

In all these images, sin is not presented as an offense against God, but as a state of not-seeing, of not-hearing, of not-being-present to the truth. In all these images, sin is not an offense against God, but a loss of inner orientation. The human being is not "evil," but disoriented. This is why Yeshua does not condemn but illuminates: "the truth will set you free" (John 8:32). Liberation comes through seeing, not through punishment.

When Yeshua says that "out of the heart come evil things" (Matthew 15:19), he is not affirming that the heart is corrupt by nature, but that an unclarified interior produces destructive behaviors. The solution is not external control, but the purification of the heart (Matthew 5:8). In Yeshua, what he does not do is as eloquent as what he says. He does not classify sins by degrees and does not construct exhaustive lists that order guilt into a moral hierarchy.

He does not condition forgiveness on a ritual atonement and does not require the carrying out of a sacred mechanism for the relationship to be restored. Moreover, Yeshua refuses to transform sin into an identity. He does not fix the human being in the label of "sinner," but sees beyond the deed, toward the person and their possibility of return. In this way, sin remains a state that can be overcome, not a definition that closes the human being into a destiny.

In the episode of the woman caught in adultery (John 8:3–11), Yeshua acknowledges the reality of the deed but refuses to transform it into an existential sentence. "Neither do I condemn you" precedes any invitation to change. In Yeshua, forgiveness precedes correction. In Paul, the language changes decisively. Sin is no longer only individual wandering, but a universal condition: "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23).

Sin becomes a power that dwells in the human being and dominates them (Romans 7:17–23). In Paul, an important theological mutation appears in the way sin is understood. He introduces the idea of a sin that enters the world through Adam and that affects all of humanity as a transmitted condition, not merely as a personal state (Romans 5:12). Sin is no longer only wandering or not-seeing; it becomes a force that operates in history.

Within this framework, the opposition between flesh and Spirit appears, a structural tension within the human being, in which the desire for the good coexists with the inability to accomplish it. "For the good that I want to do, I do not do" (Romans 7:19). The human being is described as powerless to free themselves on their own, caught in a condition that surpasses them, and from this follows the necessity of an external saving intervention.

Salvation is no longer only awakening or return; it becomes liberation from an ontological state in which the human being cannot save themselves through their own consciousness. The scene of the woman caught in adultery is essential for understanding this order. Yeshua does not deny the deed, but he refuses to transform it into identity. "Neither do I condemn you" comes before "go, and sin no more" (John 8:11).

Forgiveness is not the moral reward for setting things right, but the fertile soil from which the setting-right can arise. For Yeshua, condemnation does not heal; clear seeing heals. In the Sermon on the Mount, Yeshua radically shifts the meaning of sin from the deed to the heart. It is not killing that is the final problem, but the hatred that precedes it (Matthew 5:21–22). It is not external adultery that is the center, but the lust that fragments the interior (Matthew 5:27–28).

Sin is not the breaking of a rule, but a state of divided consciousness, lived out of fear, comparison, and self-defense. This is why Yeshua constantly attacks hypocrisy, judgment, and moral rigidity (Matthew 23): not because he minimizes evil, but because he sees its root more deeply. With Paul, the language changes decisively. Sin is no longer described predominantly as wandering, but as a universal condition into which the human being is born.

"Through one man sin entered the world" (Romans 5:12). "All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23). Sin becomes a transpersonal power, almost a law that operates in the human being independently of their will (Romans 7:23). This shift produces a major change: sin precedes the deed. The human being no longer errs and then becomes estranged; they are already in a state of separation before any personal choice.

From this reading will later be born the doctrine of original sin, taken up and consolidated by institutional Christianity, even though Yeshua nowhere affirms that a child is born guilty or condemned (Romans 5:18–19). Pauline language is profoundly juridical: guilt, condemnation, justification, payment. "The wages of sin is death" (Romans 6:23). Salvation becomes the resolution of a legal problem between the human being and God.

Yeshua's death is interpreted as the satisfaction of divine justice, as the necessary substitution for a guilt that must be paid (Romans 3:25; 2 Corinthians 5:21). This logic does not appear in the direct preaching of Yeshua, who does not speak of a God who must be appeased, but of a Father who must be recognized. The shift in meaning is subtle but profound. For Yeshua, transformation comes from "seeing": the truth liberates because it dissolves fear (John 8:32).

For Paul, the emphasis shifts to faith in an external saving event: "if you confess with your mouth…" (Romans 10:9). Sin is no longer overcome through an awakening of consciousness, but through doctrinal adherence. The Christianity that followed Yeshua—and largely the Christianity of today—has chosen the Pauline paradigm. Not because Yeshua was insufficient, but because religious systems function more efficiently on guilt than on freedom, on fear than on seeing.

Guilt can be administered, forgiveness can be conditioned, salvation can be distributed. Inner awakening, however, cannot be controlled. Yeshua did not come to convince us that we are lost. He came to show us that we live from a mistaken perception about ourselves and about God. For him, sin is not a sentence. It is inattention to life. And salvation is not the way out of a condemnation, but the return to what has always been true.

## "The Sinful Nature" in Yeshua

Yeshua never formulates a theory about "the sinful nature" as a human nature that is irremediably corrupt. His language is oriented constantly toward seeing and blindness, toward consciousness and unconsciousness. When he says on the cross "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do," recorded in Luke 23:34, the problem he names is not an ontologically evil nature but a lack of seeing. People are not presented as evil in essence, but as blind to the mechanisms that drive them.

The same perspective appears when he speaks of "the log in your eye" in Matthew 7:3–5. The emphasis falls not on a corrupt nature, but on an inner mechanism that distorts perception. The problem is not what the human being is in essence, but how they see. This is why Yeshua says in Matthew 6:22 that "if your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light." The language is one of inner clarity, not of a condemned identity.

When he affirms that "out of the heart come evil thoughts" (Matthew 15:18–19), he is not suggesting that the heart is evil by nature, but that it is unclear, unilluminated, caught in unconscious mechanisms. Evil appears as the effect of this lack of clarity, not as the expression of an irremediably corrupt essence. In Paul, the perspective takes on a different language. In the epistles, the idea appears of a "nature" that works actively against the human being.

In Romans 7:18, Paul says that "nothing good dwells in me, that is, in my earthly nature." In Galatians 5:17, he affirms that "the nature desires against the Spirit." Here, sin is no longer described as a lack of seeing, but as an inner force, a principle that pushes the human being in the wrong direction. The language shifts from clarity and blindness to inner conflict between two natures. The solution is no longer only illumination, but death to this nature and life in the Spirit.

The emphasis falls on struggle, not on seeing. In many forms of contemporary Christianity, this Pauline perspective has been amplified to the point of becoming identitarian. The human being is defined first of all as having a sinful nature. The emphasis falls on guilt, on inner corruption, and on the need for moral correction. In this framework, the principal problem is no longer blindness, but the nature of the human being itself. Thus Yeshua's nuance is often lost.

Instead of sin being seen as the result of unconsciousness and of unseen inner mechanisms, it becomes an identity label. Instead of the solution being clarity and consciousness, the solution becomes moral discipline and the continual struggle with oneself. The difference between these perspectives radically changes the approach. In Yeshua, healing comes through inner illumination and the recovery of sight. In Paul, healing comes through the conflict between nature and Spirit.

In present-day Christianity, healing is often sought through moral correction and outward conformity. In Yeshua's language, the human being is not evil by nature, but blinded by mechanisms. This is why he speaks of seeing, forgiveness, and clarity, not of a nature irremediably corrupt.

## Why Are People "Evil"?

Yeshua and Paul sometimes use the same vocabulary—sin, evil, guilt—but they speak of different realities. This difference will profoundly shape later Christianity and the way the human being comes to look upon themselves. For Yeshua, evil is not the essence of the human being. He never says that the human being is evil by nature or that they were born irremediably corrupt. His language is diagnostic, not condemnatory.

When he speaks of evil, Yeshua locates it in ignorance, fear, and disorientation. The statement spoken on the cross, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34), is the key to his anthropology: evil does not come from a conscious satanic will, but from the lack of seeing. People do evil because they do not see the consequences, do not see the truth, do not see the other as themselves.

This is why Yeshua constantly uses metaphors such as blindness—"For judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see" (John 9:39–41)—wandering, as in the parable of the lost sheep (Luke 15:4–7), sleep and inattention, as in the parable of the sleeping virgins (Matthew 25:5), or the hardened heart: "Do you not yet understand? Are your hearts hardened?" (Mark 8:17). Evil is not a substance, but a state of consciousness. The human being is not "evil," but disconnected.

When Yeshua says that "out of the heart come evil thoughts" (Matthew 15:19), he is not affirming that the heart is fundamentally corrupt, but that an unformed interior produces destructive behaviors. The "heart" is the center of a person's orientation. If this center is dominated by fear, comparison, judgment, and the desire for control, the result will be evil. But the solution is not to replace the heart, but to awaken it.

This is why Yeshua's response is not punishment, but truth—"you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32)—love—"love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44)—forgiveness—"seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22)—and purity of heart—"blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matthew 5:8). For Yeshua, the human being is curable. Even when he acknowledges human ambivalence, Yeshua does not demonize the human being.

"If you, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children…" (Matthew 7:11) shows that people can be immature, reactive, wounded, and yet capable of good. Evil does not annul the good; it overshadows it. This is why Yeshua eats with sinners and tax collectors and says: "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners" (Matthew 9:10–13). The human being is not reduced to their worst deed. In Paul, the perspective changes profoundly. Evil is no longer first of all blindness, but a state.

"All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23) becomes the universal statement about the human condition. Paul speaks of sin as a power that dwells in the human being: "It is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells within me" (Romans 7:17). The human being does not merely do evil; they are under sin. From here arises the idea of a radical powerlessness: "I want to do good, but I cannot" (Romans 7:18). This is a major change from Yeshua.

In Paul, the problem is no longer only the lack of seeing, but captivity. The explanation becomes theological: "Through one man sin entered the world" (Romans 5:12). Ignorance is no longer sufficient as an explanation. The human being needs external intervention. From here comes the necessity of the cross as a juridical and ontological resolution of the problem of sin. Later Christianity will accentuate this direction to the very end.

The human being comes to be described as sinful by nature, guilty before any deed, ontologically separated from God. Evil is no longer something that happens within the human being, but something that defines the human being. From this derive the theology of guilt, the fear of punishment, the obsession with individual salvation, and the permanent need for justification. Forgiveness is no longer light that heals, but a sentence annulled. The human being is no longer seen as a sick person who needs truth, but as a guilty person who needs acquittal.

The difference of perspective is clear. In Yeshua, evil springs from a lack of seeing: the human being does evil because they live in a fragmented consciousness, blinded by fear and separation. The solution is awakening. In Paul, the human being is under sin, caught in a condition from which they cannot free themselves on their own. The solution is the intervention of grace.

In later Christianity, the human being becomes almost exclusively defined by sin, and the present life is pushed into the background, in expectation of a resolution beyond it. Yeshua starts from the idea that people are blind. Paul starts from the idea that people are guilty. Later Christianity often comes to start from the idea that people are dangerous. The difference is not one of nuance, but of vision about the human being. Yeshua sees in the human being something to be awakened.

Later theology sees in the human being something to be corrected. And from this difference are born two completely different ways of living one's faith.

## The Value of the Human Being in Yeshua

In Yeshua, the value of the human being is the premise, not the reward. The human being does not acquire value after they change; they are treated as valuable before any change. This is why he draws near without conditions, eats with tax collectors and sinners, and says that he came precisely to call those who do not fit the religious standards: "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners" (Luke 5:32).

The call does not proceed from moral evaluation, but from the recognition of a dignity already present. When the disciples want to keep the children at a distance, Yeshua stops the filter and says: "Let the children come to me" (Mark 10:14). When the crowd wants to punish the woman caught in adultery, he restores her dignity before any correction: "Neither do I condemn you" (John 8:11). In the parable of the Samaritan, value does not depend on religious identity, but on compassion lived (Luke 10:33).

For Yeshua, the human being is not defined by their fall, but by the possibility of becoming whole. "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matthew 5:8) presupposes that the heart can become clear, and "the Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21) presupposes that the place of the encounter with God is already within the human being. The value of the human being is the starting point of his work, not its result. In Paul, the perspective starts from a different place.

"All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23) describes the human being first of all as lacking and fallen. This is why their value no longer appears as a premise, but as the result of Christ's intervention: "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). For Paul, the dignity of the human being is real, but it must be restored.

The human being is a "vessel of clay" (2 Corinthians 4:7), the bearer of a treasure that does not come from themselves. The emphasis falls on grace, and value is not denied, but is understood as vulnerable and dependent on salvation. This is why Paul's language is tense and often internally conflicted: "I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I do" (Romans 7:19). The human being is precious because they are loved by God, but also fragile because they are under sin.

The value is real, but wounded, and needs continual support through grace. In many forms of contemporary Christianity, a subtle shift has occurred. The value of the human being comes to be conditioned by their correct positioning. It is no longer a premise, as in Yeshua, nor a restored gift, as in Paul, but a status granted to those who believe correctly and conform. Verses such as "whoever does not believe will be condemned" (Mark 16:16) are read as a delimitation of identity.

The emphasis falls on belonging, on doctrine, on visible morality. The human being is easily reduced to labels, and shame and guilt become pedagogical instruments. Dignity sometimes seems suspended until one is set right. Now the paradox appears: while Yeshua starts from the value of the human being in order to heal them, some forms of Christianity start from the human being's problem in order to offer them value only after correction. The difference of perspective becomes clear when it is stated simply.

Yeshua sees in the human being something to be awakened. Paul sees in the human being something to be restored. Contemporary Christianity often sees in the human being something to be corrected. This is why Yeshua can sit calmly with sinners without first demanding their change. Paul calls for transformation in order to protect the new life. And the Christianity of today sometimes demands the change in order to grant acceptance. For Yeshua, the human being is valuable before they are good. For Paul, the human being becomes valuable through grace.

For contemporary Christianity, the human being is often valuable if they align themselves. And here one feels the different tone of the Gospels. Yeshua does not speak with worthless people whom he must save, but with valuable people who have forgotten who they are. "You are the light of the world" (Matthew 5:14) is not a promise for later, but a recognition for now.

## "The Log in Your Eye" — The Order of Consciousness Before Correction

In the Sermon on the Mount, Yeshua speaks one of the best known and, at the same time, least understood images: "Why do you see the speck in your brother's eye, but do not notice the log in your own eye? … First take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye" (the Gospel according to Matthew 7:3–5). This statement is not a lesson in moral politeness, nor an exhortation to passive tolerance.

It is a profound lesson about the mechanisms of unconsciousness and about the order in which consciousness appears. The "speck" represents the visible mistake of the other, their behavior, what bothers us about them and what we feel the need to correct. It is the external part, easy to observe, easy to name, easy to judge. The "log," however, is not a visible mistake, but the inner state from which we look at the other.

It is our irritation, our moral superiority, our need to correct, our pride that we "see better," the inner trigger activated by the other's behavior. The very fact that we see the speck so clearly shows that the log is active. Yeshua suggests that the intensity with which the other's mistake bothers us is, in fact, an indicator of our own inner mechanism. He does not say that the speck does not exist, nor that it should not be removed.

He says that the order is essential: first the log, then the speck. This order completely changes how we understand correction. If the log is still in our eye, the correction will come from pride, from irritation, from the need to prove ourselves, from inner tension. Even if what we say is morally correct, the inner place from which we say it is unconscious. Truth spoken from a mechanism does not bring consciousness, but conflict. This is why Yeshua adds: "then you will see clearly."

Clear seeing is not about observing the other's mistake more keenly, but about seeing them without the filter of our mechanisms. After the log is removed, there is no more irritation, no more superiority, no more tension. There is no more need to correct in order to confirm ourselves. In this state, correction becomes possible without conflict. It is no longer a confrontation between two prides, but a clear gesture of consciousness.

Only here is the help real, because it is no longer contaminated by the personal mechanism. The connection of this image with forgiveness is direct. The log is exactly what forgiveness dissolves. Forgiveness withdraws the tension, withdraws the inner conflict, withdraws the need to judge and to prove. In the absence of this tension, seeing becomes clear. For Yeshua, correcting another is the last step, not the first. The first step is your own healing from the mechanism out of which you would want to correct them.

Only after this healing can you see the speck without still carrying the log.

## Why Yeshua Does Not Rebuke the Prostitutes

In the Gospels, a fact appears that stands out by contrast: Yeshua never rebukes the prostitutes. Not because he considers the deed good, but because he sees clearly the inner state of the person before him. In the encounter with the woman known as a sinner in the Pharisee's house, recorded in the Gospel according to Luke 7:36–50, Yeshua does not scold her, does not expose her, does not moralize her.

Instead, he says to her: "Your sins are forgiven… Your faith has saved you; go in peace." For Yeshua, this woman has no need of rebuke, because her mechanism is already broken by shame, pain, and awareness. She does not come with defensiveness, but with inner brokenness. In this state, rebuke would be useless and even harmful. This is why Yeshua offers exactly the opposite: space, dignity, and forgiveness.

The same pattern appears in the encounter with the woman caught in adultery in the Gospel according to John 8:3–11. Before a crowd ready to condemn her, Yeshua shifts the attention from her deed to the state of those who accuse her: "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone." Then he says to the woman: "Neither do I condemn you. Go, and sin no more." There is no rebuke, but a restoration of consciousness without humiliation.

In Yeshua, rebuke is reserved for those who are blocked in moral pride and self-assurance, not for those already inwardly broken. The prostitutes, the tax collectors, the public sinners are treated with gentleness, while the Pharisees, certain of their own righteousness, receive the harshest words, as is seen in Matthew 23. In Paul, the tone changes visibly. In the epistles, prostitution is mentioned directly as a practice to be avoided in the community.

In 1 Corinthians 6:15–18, Paul says clearly: "Flee from sexual immorality… The one who joins himself to a prostitute becomes one body with her." Here the emphasis is no longer on the inner state of the person, but on protecting the community and preserving a clear moral standard. In Paul, rebuke has an educative and disciplinary role. It is no longer finely calibrated to a person's level of inner brokenness, but to the necessity of correcting behaviors that affect communal life.

In contemporary Christianity, this Pauline line has become dominant. Prostitution is often treated first of all as a moral problem, and the person is viewed through the lens of the deed. Frequently, the approach is one of condemnation, shaming, or moral pressure, losing the finesse with which Yeshua saw the human being behind the behavior.

The essential difference remains this: Yeshua looks first at the inner state of the person and decides whether rebuke would awaken consciousness or deepen the shame. Paul and later Christianity look first at the behavior and at its effect on the community. This is why, in the Gospels, the prostitutes are not rebuked, but restored. In the Pauline writings and in the practice of many forms of Christianity today, they are first of all morally corrected.

## Why Yeshua Forgives Without Sacrifice

One of the most unsettling differences between Yeshua's message and later Christian theology is this: Yeshua forgives without sacrifice. Not just occasionally. Not as an exception. But as a constant way of being. For a practicing Christian, this idea sounds like blasphemy. Christianity affirms clearly, "Yeshua forgave on the basis of the sacrifice that was to be himself." But that is what Paul says, not Yeshua. Yeshua never said, "I forgive you because I will die for it."

In a religious world in which forgiveness was tightly bound to the Temple, to blood, to the altar, and to intermediaries, Yeshua grants forgiveness directly, personally, and unconditionally. He does not send the human being to bring an offering before forgiving them; on the contrary, forgiveness often precedes any correction or visible change (Mark 2:5; Luke 7:48). This practice is not a theological oversight. It is a radical affirmation about the nature of God.

In classical religious logic, sin creates a debt, and forgiveness presupposes a compensation. Sacrifice is the mechanism by which the balance is restored. This logic is present in the Levitical system and will be taken up, reinterpreted, and amplified later in Pauline theology. Yeshua, however, does not operate within a transactional framework. When the paralytic is brought before him, Yeshua does not say: "bring a sacrifice," nor "pray for forgiveness," nor "go to the priests."

He says simply: "Son, your sins are forgiven" (Mark 2:5). The reaction of those present is revealing: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7). Their question is correct. Yeshua's implicit answer is even more radical: God is not separated from this act of forgiveness. In Yeshua's encounters with people considered "sinners," forgiveness does not appear as a reward for repentance, but as the unveiling of a reality already present.

The woman who washes his feet with her tears does not receive forgiveness because she wept; she weeps because something has already been recognized within her. Yeshua says: "Her sins, which are many, are forgiven; therefore she loved much" (Luke 7:47). The order is important. Love does not buy forgiveness; forgiveness frees love. In this framework, sacrifice becomes unnecessary—not because sin is trivial, but because forgiveness does not repair a broken relationship; it unveils a relationship that was never interrupted.

## God Need Not Be Appeased

One of the most profound things Yeshua does is to eliminate the idea of a God who must be convinced, persuaded, or satisfied. In the parable of the prodigal son, the father does not wait for explanations, does not demand compensation, does not require penance. He runs toward the son before he finishes his speech of guilt (Luke 15:20). This image is essential. The God of Yeshua is not a heavenly creditor. He is a presence that restores without keeping accounts.

If God is not offended in a juridical sense, then sacrifice is no longer necessary as a mechanism of reconciliation. Yeshua does not speak of sin as an ontological rupture between the human being and God. He speaks of blindness, loss, wandering, inattention (Matthew 13:15; Luke 19:10). If sin does not create a real separation, then sacrifice cannot "repair" something that was not broken.

What needs to be healed is not the relationship between God and the human being, but the human being's perception of this relationship. This is why Yeshua says: "Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick" (Mark 2:17). The physician does not pay for the illness. He treats it. Yeshua forgives not as the priest of a system, but as a spiritual authority who operates from within the divine reality. This is why his gesture is perceived as scandalous. He bypasses the institution, the altar, the blood, the intermediaries.

Not because he despises them, but because he surpasses them. When he says: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13), Yeshua does not reject the tradition but reveals its limit. Sacrifice belongs to a stage of consciousness in which the relationship with God is mediated by fear. Mercy belongs to a stage in which the relationship is direct.

## The Gravest Sins in Yeshua

The idea of a "grave sin" is not identical across all the strata of Christianity. It changes along with the perspective on the human being: in Yeshua, sin is what breaks the living relationship; in the Apostle Paul, sin is what threatens the truth and the community; in the Christianity of today, sin often becomes what violates a norm. The difference is not one of list, but of level of consciousness.

For Yeshua, the gravest sins are not the "visible" or scandalous ones, but those that close the heart. He does not insist obsessively on sexuality, money, or ritual, but on hypocrisy, the lack of mercy, voluntary blindness, and judgment. The Pharisees are rebuked not because they sin "more," but because they can no longer see the human being. "Woe to you" is not a moral condemnation, but a diagnosis: you have lost contact with life. Yeshua identifies as grave the refusal of truth lived.

"The sin against the Holy Spirit" is not a one-time mistake, but the definitive closure toward correction, toward light, toward reality. Where a person can no longer recognize the good when they encounter it, transformation becomes impossible. In this logic, adultery, theft, or lying are curable; the hardening of the heart is not. For Yeshua, grave sin is anything that separates: it separates the human being from themselves, from the other, and from God.

This is why he can forgive deeds but harshly confront attitudes. It is not behavior that is the center, but the state of the heart from which behavior springs. In Paul, the emphasis shifts. Not because he denies empathy, but because his mission is different: the building and protecting of communities. For him, the gravest sins become those that corrupt the truth or tear apart the body of the community.

Idolatry, immorality, division, heresy—all are dangerous because they pull the human being and the group in the wrong direction. Paul introduces a clearer hierarchy of sins, because he operates within a normative framework. The grave sin is what cannot coexist with the new identity in Christ. From here comes the language of temporary exclusion, discipline, and correction. Not out of cruelty, but out of the conviction that truth precedes comfort.

Yet this shift has a side effect: sin begins to be defined more through conformity than through consciousness. The human being is loved, but is constantly evaluated against a standard. Empathy remains, but it is conditioned by acceptance of the framework.

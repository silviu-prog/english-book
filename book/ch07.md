# 7. Faith, Salvation, and Repentance

## Faith in Yeshua

Yeshua uses the word "faith" frequently, but its meaning is existential, not doctrinal. For him, faith is living trust, openness, and a readiness to be transformed. When he says, "Your faith has saved you" (Luke 7:50), he is describing a condition of the heart that allows for healing and wholeness. Likewise, "if you had faith as small as a mustard seed…" (Matthew 17:20) does not point to the acceptance of a creed, but to a living relationship with the reality of God. This faith is not oriented toward his death, but toward the present Kingdom. It is not a mental assent, but a state that opens a person to life. Paul, by contrast, shifts faith toward the event of the cross and the resurrection: "If you confess with your mouth that Yeshua is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved" (Romans 10:9).

Confronted with this formulation, Yeshua might respond, in his paradoxical style, that a faith which does not change one's way of living is not faith but religious opinion. This is why he warns: "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the Kingdom, but the one who does the will of my Father" (Matthew 7:21).

## What Was the Role of Faith for Yeshua

The word "faith" is probably the most used and the most misunderstood term in Christianity. Everyone talks about faith, but rarely does anyone ask what it meant for Yeshua, and whether that meaning is identical to the one it later acquires in apostolic theology. A careful reading shows that, although they use the same word, Yeshua and the Apostle Paul operate with different emphases that profoundly change the religious experience.

For Yeshua, faith is never framed as the acceptance of a set of correct ideas about God. He does not ask people to believe things about him, but to trust the reality they live together with him. The term the Gospel uses indicates trust, openness, availability, rather than intellectual conviction. This is why Yeshua frequently says, "Your faith has healed you" (Mark 5:34), not "your faith in me has saved you" in a doctrinal sense.

Faith is the act by which a person ceases to defend himself against life, ceases to contract in fear, and allows the reality of God to be active within him. It is not a spiritual performance, but a relaxing of control. It is essential that Yeshua praises faith precisely where there is no religious correctness: in a Roman centurion (Matthew 8:10), in a woman considered impure (Mark 5:28–34), in a Samaritan (Luke 17:19).

This shows clearly that, for Yeshua, faith is not religious belonging, nor conformity to the Law. It is the capacity to trust a reality deeper than your fear. Moreover, Yeshua links faith to sight: "Let it be done to you according to your faith" (Matthew 9:29). Faith is not the opposite of sight, but its condition. A person sees the Kingdom to the extent that he is no longer blinded by the need for security.

For this reason, unbelief is not morally condemned; it is treated as a form of fear or inner rigidity. For Yeshua, faith is not the goal of the spiritual life, but the gate. Once the gate is passed, the living of the Kingdom begins. This is why he does not build a "doctrine of faith," but constantly invites people to follow a way of life. Faith is dynamic, relational, lived, not formulated.

## Faith vs. Trust

One of the greatest distortions of later Christianity is the confusion of faith with the intellectual acceptance of statements about God. For Yeshua, faith is never a correct opinion about reality, but an existential positioning toward reality. It is not something you "have" in your mind, but something that happens within you when you stop defending yourself against life. Yeshua does not ask people to believe ideas about him.

He does not say, "Believe that I am the Messiah," or "Believe the correct doctrine." He says, "Do not be afraid" (Matthew 14:27), "Come" (Matthew 14:29), "Follow me" (Mark 1:17). All of these call for trust, not conviction. Faith, for Yeshua, is the opposite of fear, not the opposite of reason. When faith is confused with correct opinion, it moves from the heart into identity. It becomes something that defines you and separates you from others. But for Yeshua, faith does not separate. It opens.

This is why he recognizes it where religion is absent and criticizes it where religion is sure of itself.

## "If You Had Faith as Small as a Mustard Seed"

Yeshua's statement about faith "as small as a mustard seed" (Matthew 17:20; Luke 17:6) is often read as a hyperbole about power: if you have enough faith, you can move mountains. But Yeshua is not speaking here about the quantity of faith, but about its nature. The mustard seed is not powerful. It is small, fragile, almost insignificant. Its power lies not in its size, but in the fact that it is alive.

The faith Yeshua speaks of is not intense, not heroic, not spectacular. It is real. It is that minimal openness in which a person no longer completely resists the reality of God. The mountains that are moved are not geographical, but inner. They are the rigid structures of fear, of defensive identity, of the internalized impossible. Yeshua does not say that you need much faith, but that you need true faith, even if it is small. A great but false faith—that is, an ideological one—moves nothing. This statement directly undermines spiritual performance. Yeshua does not ask you to become more believing, but to become more sincere. A grain of real faith is more transformative than a massive religious certainty.

## Why Yeshua Never Asks You to "Believe in Him"

A surprising fact, yet verifiable in the Gospels, is this: Yeshua does not ask people to "believe in him" in the doctrinal sense that would later become central. He says, "Believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15), "Believe in God" (John 14:1), but his call is always relational and existential, not confessional. The reason is simple and profound: Yeshua does not want ideological followers, but transformed people.

To believe in him as a theological figure can become a substitute for actually following him. You can believe correctly and live just as fearfully, just as rigidly, just as violently. This is why Yeshua always shifts the emphasis from statement to movement. "Why do you call me 'Lord, Lord,' and do not do what I say?" (Luke 6:46). True faith is seen in the way you live, not in what you affirm about Yeshua.

## "Believe That You Have Received It, and It Will Be Yours"

The verse "Therefore I tell you, whatever you ask for in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours" (Mark 11:24) has become, over time, one of the most quoted and, at the same time, one of the most distorted of Yeshua's words. It is often used as a formula for obtaining what one desires, as a spiritual technique, or as proof that faith is a method of forcing reality to conform to our will.

But in the Gospels, this verse does not appear in a context of controlling reality, but in a context of purifying the heart and aligning with the Father. Immediately before, Yeshua speaks of forgiveness: "And whenever you stand praying, forgive whatever you have against anyone" (Mark 11:25). This proximity is essential. The faith Yeshua speaks of is not an intensification of desire, but an emptying of the heart of its blockages.

Prayer is not an instrument through which you obtain, but a space in which you become free of fear, resentment, and separation. Yeshua never links faith to possession, but to sight. "Everything is possible for the one who believes" (Mark 9:23) is spoken to a desperate father, not to an ambitious man. Faith is not the power to produce results, but the capacity to remain open before reality without closing oneself in fear.

To "believe that you have received" does not mean to imagine the desired object, but to enter a state in which the heart no longer lives out of lack. The same logic appears when Yeshua says, "Your Father knows what you need before you ask him" (Matthew 6:8). If the Father already knows, then prayer is neither information nor negotiation. It is alignment. To believe that you have received means to settle into trust before the outer form appears. It means to live from relationship, not from anxiety. Yeshua never teaches that desire must be intensified, but that fear must be dissolved. "Do not be anxious about tomorrow" (Matthew 6:34) is the frame within which Mark 11:24 is understood. When a person no longer lives out of worry, he can ask without clinging. He can desire without controlling. He can wait without fear. For Yeshua, "to believe that you have received" is a state of the heart that has come out of lack and entered into trust.

It is not a technique of obtaining, but an expression of inner freedom. With Paul, the emphasis shifts. Paul does not quote this verse and does not develop the idea of prayer in this direction. He speaks of prayer as a space of dependence and of accepting the will of God: "Do not be anxious about anything, but in everything, by prayer, present your requests to God" (Philippians 4:6). The result is not obtaining the thing requested, but "the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding" (Philippians 4:7). For Paul, prayer does not necessarily change the circumstances, but the person. The personal example is decisive: "Three times I pleaded with the Lord to take it away from me… and he said to me, 'My grace is sufficient for you'" (2 Corinthians 12:8–9). Here the difference in tone becomes clear.

Paul does not operate from the certainty that what you ask for materializes, but from the trust that, regardless of the outcome, grace is sufficient. Faith, for Paul, is less about "already having" and more about remaining steadfast: "We walk by faith, not by sight" (2 Corinthians 5:7). Prayer becomes the space in which a person submits to the will of God, not the space in which reality aligns itself with human desire.

In later Christianity, the verse from Mark 11:24 undergoes two opposite transformations. In some traditions, it is almost ignored, because it seems too bold. In others, especially in modern currents, it becomes a formula: if you visualize enough, if you believe enough, you will receive. Prayer is transformed either into resignation or into a technique of manifestation. Both lose the living meaning of Yeshua's words. In the first case, faith is reduced to passive acceptance.

In the second, to active control. But Yeshua speaks neither of passivity nor of control. He speaks of trust. For Yeshua, the moment of prayer is the moment in which a person comes out of fear and enters into relationship. "When you ask, believe that you have received it" means: enter already into the state of trust in which lack no longer rules your heart. Do not ask out of anxiety, but out of peace. Not out of lack, but out of fullness.

Paul would say: pray and leave the result in God's hands. Later Christianity often says: either expect nothing, or force reality through faith. Yeshua says something else: live as if the Father is already present and attentive. Because this is the reality out of which faith is born.

## How Faith Became Identity

With the development of the Christian communities, faith begins to function as a marker of identity. It is no longer merely trust, but belonging. Formulas, confessions, and boundaries appear. Faith becomes something that defines you socially and separates you from others. Yeshua never builds a group identity based on faith. He constantly dissolves boundaries: he praises the faith of those "outside" and criticizes the security of those "inside." Identity-faith is stable, but rigid.

Faith as trust is unstable, but alive. When faith becomes identity, the fear of doubt appears. But Yeshua is not afraid of doubt. He is afraid of dead certainty. The disciples doubt constantly, and Yeshua does not exclude them. The Pharisees are certain, and Yeshua comes into conflict with them. For Yeshua, faith is not a correct doctrine, a secure identity, a spiritual performance. It is the minimal trust that allows life to resettle.

For Paul, faith becomes the key to salvation, the principle of justification, the bond of the community. Between the two there is no contradiction, but a creative tension. Yet if Paul's faith completely replaces that of Yeshua, what remains is a faith that knows much and transforms little. If Yeshua's faith remains central, even a single seed is enough. Yeshua does not seek secure believers. He seeks people willing to stop defending themselves.

## What Yeshua Would Say About Centering Salvation on His Death

Yeshua announces his death, but he never explains it as a juridical mechanism of salvation. He speaks of it as the consequence of fidelity to truth in a world that rejects the light: "The light has come into the world, but people loved the darkness more" (John 3:19). His death is bound to the mission of bearing witness to the truth: "For this I was born… to bear witness to the truth" (John 18:37).

If he were to comment on Pauline theology, Yeshua might say that his death is not the cause of forgiveness, but the mirror of human resistance to the truth. The cross does not change the heart of God toward humanity; it reveals the state of the human heart. The prayer from the cross expresses this clearly: "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34). Forgiveness is not activated by death, but is already present, even in the midst of violence.

For Paul, the resurrection is the proof of salvation and the guarantee of eternal life. Yeshua, however, speaks of eternal life as a reality accessible now: "Whoever hears my word… has passed from death to life" (John 5:24). When he says, "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25), he points not only to a future event to be believed, but to a present state to be lived.

The resurrection, in Yeshua's language, is the coming out of fear, of hatred, and of inner death, not merely the hope of a future restoration. Yeshua would not deny the cross and the resurrection, but he would refuse to turn them into a substitute for transformation. He would say, implicitly, that his death saves no one if life remains unchanged. The truth he lived saves only when it is lived onward.

This is why his call remains: "If you remain in my word… the truth will set you free" (John 8:31–32). Thus, Yeshua would shift the emphasis from believing something about him to becoming what he lived. The cross not as a payment that closes the road, but as a door that opens it—toward a transformed life, here and now.

## The Two Times Yeshua Says "Wow!"

The Gospels very rarely record moments in which Yeshua is described as marveling. Precisely for this reason, those few situations become extremely revealing. They show not only his reaction, but also what he truly considered unusual in human behavior. Surprisingly, Yeshua is never amazed by sin. He is not amazed by prostitution, by adultery, by corruption, by greed, or by lying. All of these appear, in his eyes, as the predictable consequences of human unconsciousness.

They are not events that astonish him, but natural manifestations of an inner state in which a person does not see himself. In contrast, there are two clear situations in which the text explicitly says that Yeshua marvels. Both have to do not with the morality of people, but with their level of inner sight. The first appears in the encounter with the Roman centurion, recounted in Matthew 8:10.

The centurion, a non-Jew and an officer of the occupying army, says that he is not worthy to receive Yeshua into his house, and that it is enough for him to speak a word for his servant to be healed. The text says: "When Jesus heard this, he marveled and said to those who followed him: Truly I tell you, I have not found such great faith even in Israel." Yeshua's amazement is provoked by the inner clarity of a man outside religion.

A pagan sees clearly what the religious fail to see. The second situation is the opposite of the first and is recorded in Mark 6:6: "And he marveled at their unbelief." Here, Yeshua is in Nazareth, among those who had known him since childhood. Religious people, familiar with the Scriptures, yet incapable of seeing what is right in front of them. In both cases, Yeshua's amazement has to do with consciousness, not with moral behavior.

He marvels when someone sees very clearly, and he marvels when someone does not see at all, though he ought to see. This observation reveals something essential about his perspective: evil deeds do not surprise him, because they are the effects of unconsciousness. What is rare and surprising is consciousness, the human capacity to see beyond one's own mechanisms. Thus, the moments of Yeshua's amazement become precious clues to what he valued most. Not outer moral conformity, but inner sight.

## For Yeshua, Does Faith Not Save?

In the Gospels, Yeshua often says, "Your faith has saved you," or "has healed you." But the meaning of these statements is essential to clarify. Faith is not the cause of salvation, but the condition in which salvation becomes possible. It does not produce the transformation, but allows the transformation to happen. Faith, for Yeshua, is the relaxing of defenses, the suspension of fear, the willingness to let the reality of God be active in your life.

Without this openness, nothing can change—not because God refuses, but because the person remains closed. This is why faith is often minimal, fragile, almost imperceptible. Yeshua says that a mustard seed is enough (Matthew 17:20). This statement would be absurd if salvation worked on the basis of the quantity or intensity of faith.

But it becomes perfectly coherent if salvation is the coming out of fear and closure, not the accumulation of a spiritual virtue. A single seed is enough because it is alive. Not because it is large. For Yeshua, salvation is not triggered by faith as an isolated inner act, but by the encounter that follows faith. Faith does not save; relationship saves. Faith is not the goal, but the threshold. Once the threshold is crossed, the road begins.

This is why Yeshua never stops at "you have faith"; he continues with "go," "change," "let go," "love," "forgive." Salvation is a process, not a verdict. It is revealing that Yeshua speaks very rarely of salvation in terms of an "after death." His emphasis is almost obsessively on the life of now: who sees the Kingdom, who enters it, who lives from it. Salvation is liberation from a way of being dominated by fear, judgment, and attachment.

Faith is not the entry ticket into a heavenly future, but the moment in which you stop opposing life. This explains why Yeshua can recognize salvation where doctrinal faith is absent. He praises the trust of a Roman, not his religious belonging. He forgives and saves without demanding theological confessions. He never asks, "What do you believe about me?" but "What do you want?", "What are you seeking?", "Will you follow me?".

For Yeshua, salvation is not conditioned on having correct ideas about God, but on entering into a living relationship with the truth. Therefore, for Yeshua, faith does not save in a mechanical sense. It is not the coin with which you buy salvation. It is the opening through which salvation can take place. To say that "for Yeshua, faith does not save" does not mean to minimize faith, but to free it from a function it does not have. Salvation, for Yeshua, is life lived from the Kingdom.

Faith is only the beginning of the step toward it. For Yeshua, faith is not a mechanism, not a juridical condition, not a goal. Faith is the gate. Salvation is the road. Yeshua does not say, "Believe and you will be saved." He says, implicitly, "Open yourself and you will live."

## For Yeshua, Salvation Is a Process

For Yeshua, salvation is not a juridical moment, but a process of awakening. He says, "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21), "Today salvation has come" (Luke 19:9). Salvation is something that happens in real time, in the way a person begins to live differently. For the Apostle Paul, salvation takes the form of an event: justification by faith (Romans 5:1). The emphasis shifts from continuous transformation to status.

A person is declared saved on the basis of faith. This shift has historical and pastoral meaning, but it changes the experience. Salvation risks becoming something you have received, not something you live. Yeshua does not speak of status; he speaks of life. Paul speaks of guaranteed eternal life; Yeshua speaks of life freed from fear.

## What It Means for Yeshua to Be Saved

Yeshua does not speak of salvation as a future reward or a favorable sentence in a heavenly courtroom. He uses the language of salvation to describe a change of state, a passage from one way of being to another. "Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:9) does not mean that Zacchaeus received a spiritual certificate, but that his life resettled: his relationship with money, with people, with himself changed in concrete ways.

For Yeshua, to be saved means to be drawn out of captivity. But the captivity is not primarily juridical, but inner: fear, shame, rigid attachment, the need for control, isolation. Salvation is liberation from these structures that diminish life. This is why Yeshua links salvation to sight ("the blind see"), to rising ("get up"), to turning ("turn back"), to entering ("enter the Kingdom"). All of these are verbs of life lived, not of verdict.

Within this frame, salvation is inseparable from the present. It happens "today," "now," "here." It is not deferred until after death, though it is not denied beyond death. The emphasis is clear: if salvation does not transform the life of now, it is not salvation in Yeshua's sense. For Yeshua, salvation is closely bound to the Kingdom of God. But the Kingdom is not a place you arrive at; it is a reality you enter into.

"The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). To be saved means to begin to live from this reality, to see the world differently, to react differently, to love differently. This explains why Yeshua can say that some "are near the Kingdom," yet are not yet in it. Salvation is not binary (you either are or you are not), but processual. It is a road of awakening, not an instantaneous switch of status.

This is why Yeshua never offers a "plan of salvation" in clear steps. He offers parables, encounters, invitations. Because salvation is not a scheme, but an experience of transformation.

## Why Yeshua Told the Rich Young Man to Sell His Wealth

On a superficial reading, Yeshua seems to condemn wealth in itself. On a careful reading, however, it becomes clear that money is not the problem, but the place it occupies in the inner structure of a person. The account appears in all three Synoptic Gospels (Matthew 19:16–22; Mark 10:17–22; Luke 18:18–23). A young man comes to Yeshua with a sincere question: "What must I do to inherit eternal life?" The question is already revealing.

He does not ask "How should I live?" but "What must I do?"—that is, what action, what performance, what addition he is still lacking. Eternal life is seen as an object that can be obtained. Yeshua begins by bringing him into the register of the Law: the commandments. The young man replies that he has kept them all. The text does not suggest hypocrisy. On the contrary, the Gospel of Mark adds an essential detail: "Yeshua, looking at him, loved him" (Mark 10:21).

We are not dealing with an evil, corrupt, or shallow man, but with a sincere, moral, and disciplined one. For exactly this reason, his problem is deeper. Yeshua's response is not a universal rule, but a personalized diagnosis: "One thing you lack: sell everything you have, give to the poor… then come and follow me" (Mark 10:21). Yeshua does not say "everyone must sell their possessions," but "one thing you lack." It is not wealth in itself, but attachment to it, that is the obstacle.

The young man's reaction confirms the diagnosis: "He went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions" (Mark 10:22). The sorrow does not come from the loss of money, but from the impossibility of giving up his principal source of security and identity. Here it becomes clear that wealth is not a neutral good for him, but the center of his life. In Yeshua's logic, the real problem is not what a person possesses, but what he lives from. "Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21).

Money becomes dangerous when it replaces trust in God with control, accumulation, and self-sufficiency. This is why Yeshua says, immediately after the young man's departure: "How hard it will be for those who have wealth to enter the Kingdom of God" (Mark 10:23). Not "because they are rich," but because wealth creates the illusion of self-sufficiency. This statement shocks even the disciples. In the mentality of the time, wealth was often interpreted as a sign of divine blessing.

Yeshua overturns this logic: "What is impossible for people is possible for God" (Mark 10:27). The problem is not moral, but existential: wealth makes it difficult to abandon control. Yeshua does not condemn property as such. He does not say that money is evil, but that it cannot be served without becoming a master: "You cannot serve God and Mammon" (Matthew 6:24). Mammon is not the coin, but the principle of life based on accumulation as security.

In the case of the rich young man, Mammon had already become master. It is essential to note that Yeshua does not tell him, "Sell everything and you will be saved," but "Come and follow me." Selling his wealth is not the goal, but the condition of the freedom necessary for discipleship. Poverty is not the ideal, but total availability. Wealth must be abandoned only where it hinders following. This is confirmed by the fact that Yeshua does not ask the same thing of all the wealthy.

Zacchaeus, for example, voluntarily gives up only part of his wealth, and Yeshua declares: "Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:8–9). The difference is clear: for Zacchaeus, money is no longer the center; for the rich young man, it still is. Through this episode, Yeshua does not preach an economic ideology, but makes visible a universal truth: anything that becomes a source of identity, security, and worth apart from God becomes an idol. For some it is money.

For others, it is status, religion, power, or morality. Yeshua does not ask everyone to give up the same thing, but each one to give up what holds him captive. The conclusion of this episode is not about poverty, but about freedom. Yeshua tells the rich young man to sell his wealth not to punish him, not to test him arbitrarily, but because he saw the exact place where his life was bound. Where a person cannot let go, there he is not free.

Yeshua did not come to make us poorer. He came to make us freer.

## Salvation in Today's Christianity

In much of contemporary Christianity, salvation has been moved from the plane of lived life to the plane of theological security. To be saved often means to be "right with God," to have a destiny settled after death. The emphasis falls on what you believe, not on how you live. On doctrinal correctness, not on the transformation of consciousness. This shift has profound consequences. Salvation becomes something static, closed, defensive. Once obtained, it is no longer called into question.

Life can remain dominated by fear, judgment, rigidity, while salvation remains intact as a status. Within this frame, salvation is no longer the power that changes life, but the assurance that life does not decisively matter. This version of salvation is, paradoxically, very institutionally efficient. It offers security, clear boundaries, belonging.

But it is hard to reconcile with the message of Yeshua, who says that a tree is known by its fruit, and that not all who say "Lord, Lord" live from the Kingdom (Matthew 7:21). The fundamental difference between Yeshua and today's Christianity is not about whether salvation exists, but about what kind of reality salvation is. For Yeshua, salvation is liberated life; it is present and processual; it is seen in relationships, not in declarations.

It does not need to be declared, because it is lived. In dominant Christianity, salvation is a spiritual status; it is future and guaranteed; it is validated through correct faith. Yeshua asks, "Are you alive?" Christianity often asks, "Are you saved?" In this way, security, doctrinal coherence, and transmissibility are gained. But the living tension of salvation as a road is lost. Its transforming character is lost. The risk is lost.

For Yeshua, salvation is risky because it draws you out of your old structures. For today's Christianity, salvation is safe because it allows you to remain, sometimes, exactly where you are. For Yeshua, to be saved means to be drawn out of fear, to enter a wider life, to live from the Kingdom. For today's Christianity, to be saved often means to have a settled status, to be in the correct camp, to have a guarantee for the "afterward."

Yeshua did not come to tell us how to get to heaven. He came to show us what life looks like when heaven enters a person.

## The Kingdom of God Comes to Earth; We Do Not Go to Heaven

For many Christians, the essence of faith is an "afterward": after death you go to heaven or to hell, depending on faith and morality. Heaven becomes the goal, life becomes the test, and God becomes the evaluator. But when you listen to Yeshua, you notice that the center of his message is not "where you end up," but "what you see now." He does not speak of a geography of the postmortem as the main theme.

He speaks of the Kingdom as a present reality, one that can be missed even while living religiously. Yeshua says, "The time has come and the Kingdom of God is near; repent and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15). "Near" here does not mean only temporally, but existentially: it is at hand, accessible, available. And when he is asked about signs, he cuts off the spectacular expectation entirely: "The Kingdom of God does not come in such a way as to strike the eye… for behold, the Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:20–21). This changes everything. If the Kingdom is within, then it is not a future reward, but a present awakening. In the parables, the Kingdom is described as leaven that rises, as a seed, as a mustard grain, as a hidden treasure (Matthew 13:31–33, 44).

All of these are images of an inner process and of recognition, not of "transport to a place." The Kingdom is not a heavenly resort. It is a change of perception in which the reality of God becomes visible within ordinary life. This is why Yeshua insists on "eyes to see" and "ears to hear" (Matthew 13:13–16). Not because the information is missing, but because people are blind to what is already there.

"Heaven" in the popular sense can become, psychologically, a mechanism for deferring transformation: you live with the hope that, one day, another reality will begin. The Kingdom, in Yeshua's sense, is the opposite of deferral: it is here, and the problem is that you do not see it because you are caught in fear, worry, judgment, accumulation. This is why he says, "Do not be anxious… seek first the Kingdom" (Matthew 6:31–33). The Kingdom is not a "spiritual bonus"; it is the meaning that reorganizes anxiety.

When Yeshua speaks of eternal life, he often binds it to knowledge, not to location: "And this is eternal life: that they know you, the only true God…" (John 17:3). "Knowledge" here is not information, but intimacy, recognition, another way of being in relationship with the real. So eternal life begins as a mode of consciousness, not as a postmortem displacement.

In this light, the Kingdom of God does not completely replace the idea of an "afterward," but places it in the background. Yeshua's emphasis is: "Wake up now, see now, live now." And if there is an "afterward," it cannot be an excuse for the absence of the Kingdom here.

## Did Yeshua Believe in Heaven and Hell?

Yeshua does not speak of Heaven and Hell in the sense in which they are understood today, and when he uses language that seems "eschatological," he uses it symbolically, pedagogically, and existentially, not as a description of clearly defined metaphysical postmortem destinations. Yeshua never preaches a binary system of reward and punishment after death, as would become central in later Christianity. He does not say, "If you believe correctly you go to Heaven; if not, you go to Hell."

The meaning of his message is the Kingdom of God, not the afterlife as a juridical system. For Yeshua, the Kingdom is not a place you reach after death, but a reality that draws near, opens, and can be lived here and now: "The Kingdom of God is near" (Mark 1:15), "The Kingdom of God is in your midst" (Luke 17:21). The language is present, not postmortem. Yeshua speaks of a change of state, not of a change of cosmic address.

When Yeshua uses language that seems to describe "hell," he does not use the theological concept of Hell as we know it today. The term most often used is Gehenna (Matthew 5:22; Mark 9:43). Gehenna was not a metaphysical place, but the Valley of Hinnom, a real location associated with fire, destruction, and ruin—a symbol of self-destruction and of the consequences of living from violence, hatred, and fear.

When Yeshua warns about Gehenna, he is not describing a place of eternal torture, but the direction of a life lived against the truth. For Yeshua, "punishment" is not imposed from outside by an angry God, but is the inner result of a wrong orientation. "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:16). A life lived from fear, judgment, and closure already produces hell.

This is why Yeshua speaks of "weeping and gnashing of teeth" as a state of inner exclusion, not as a divine juridical sentence (Matthew 8:12). The language is existential, not penal. Revealingly, Yeshua speaks very little about the afterlife and very much about how you live now. He does not motivate people through the promise of Heaven or the fear of Hell, but through the call to full life: "I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly" (John 10:10).

The emphasis is not on where you end up, but on what you live from. The contrast with Paul is significant. With Paul, the perspective gradually shifts from the Kingdom lived to the final destiny of the person. Paul speaks of "salvation," "loss," "eternal death," and "eternal life" (Romans 6:23) within a more juridical frame. Although Paul does not describe hell in detail as a place of torture, he introduces a binary logic: in Christ there is life; outside of him there is death.

With Paul, the idea of a final judgment and of definitive separation appears clearly (2 Corinthians 5:10). The emphasis falls on the ultimate destiny, not on the present state. Likewise, salvation becomes something that is "received" through correct faith, not merely an awakening of consciousness. This shift would later permit the development of a detailed eschatology of Heaven and Hell. Later institutional Christianity would amplify this direction.

Heaven and Hell become clear places, described in imagery, used as pedagogical instruments and, often, as instruments of control. Hell becomes eternal punishment; Heaven becomes eternal reward. Present life is reduced to a test for the future life. Fear and hope are used as engines of religious conformity. This use would be foreign to Yeshua. He never says, "Do this so you do not end up in Hell." He says, "Follow me," "Do not be afraid," "The Kingdom is here."

For Yeshua, God is not the judge who decides final destinations, but the Father who calls to life. Judgment, when it appears, is revelation rather than condemnation: the truth brings to light what is alive and what is dead in a person (John 3:19–21). Thus, we can state the difference in perspective very clearly: Yeshua speaks of states of being. Paul begins to speak of final destinies.

Today's Christianity often speaks of places of reward and punishment. Yeshua does not motivate us through the fear of Hell or the desire for Heaven. He calls us to the Kingdom as life lived in truth, here and now. Therefore, Yeshua does not preach Heaven and Hell as cosmic systems of the afterlife. He speaks of directions of life, already lived in the present. Hell is life lived against the truth. The Kingdom is life lived in communion with God.

## "Today You Will Be with Me in Paradise" — The Kingdom, Death, and Yeshua's Language

One of the most frequently invoked objections, when someone affirms that Yeshua speaks of the Kingdom of God as a present reality, lived "here and now," is the reply addressed to the thief on the cross: "Truly I tell you, today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43). For many, this phrase seems to close the discussion. If Yeshua promises "paradise" after death, then the Kingdom can no longer be a present reality, but a future, postmortem one.

Yet this conclusion arises only if we read the sentence through the lens of later theology, not through the language, context, and intention of Yeshua. To understand this statement, we must do something essential: separate language from doctrine and return to the way Yeshua uses words, not to what they later came to mean. The word translated as "paradise" (paradeisos) is not a sophisticated theological term in the Gospels.

It does not appear frequently in Yeshua's discourse, and it is not systematically defined. In the Jewish context of the time, paradeisos evokes the idea of a garden, a place of rest, a space of God's presence, not a detailed map of the afterlife. What is important is that Yeshua never uses "paradise" as a central piece of his message.

The center of his message remains the Kingdom of God—a reality that "is near" (Mark 1:15), that "is within you" (Luke 17:21), and that does not come "in such a way as to strike the eye" (Luke 17:20). So when Yeshua uses the term "paradise," he does not change his message; he responds to an extreme situation, using language accessible to the man before him. The thief is not asking for a doctrine about the afterlife. He is not asking for explanations about salvation.

He is not asking for a theological scheme. He says only: "Yeshua, remember me when you come into your Kingdom" (Luke 23:42). This request is remarkable. The man does not ask to escape death. He does not ask to be taken down from the cross. He does not ask for the consequences of his deeds to be annulled.

He asks not to be forgotten. He asks for recognition. He asks for inclusion. He asks for relationship. Yeshua's response is not a lesson about heavens and hells, but an existential assurance: "Today you will be with me."

The emphasis falls not on "where," but on "with whom." Not on geography, but on presence. The decisive word in this phrase is not "paradise," but "today." "Today you will be with me…" does not move salvation into a distant future, but brings it into the absolute present of that moment. Yeshua frequently uses this "today" to break religious deferral: "Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:9). "Today these words have been fulfilled" (Luke 4:21).

For Yeshua, "today" is the time of the Kingdom. It is the moment in which the reality of God becomes accessible, regardless of the outer context. Even on the cross. So the statement to the thief does not contradict the message about the present Kingdom; it radicalizes it: if the Kingdom can be lived "today," then it can be lived even in death. Yeshua does not say, "Today you will reach a place called paradise." He says, "Today you will be with me." This is the essence.

In Yeshua's language, salvation is not a relocation, but a communion. To be "with Yeshua" means to be in the same reality of consciousness, in the same openness toward God, in the same trust without fear. Indeed, Yeshua defines eternal life not as duration or location, but as relationship: "This is eternal life: that they know you" (John 17:3). Knowledge here is not intellectual, but relational, intimate, alive. So "paradise" becomes a bridge-word, not a doctrine.

A language of nearness, not of metaphysical cartography. Yeshua does not tell the thief, "You are mistaken; there is no paradise after death; the Kingdom is only here." Why? Because Yeshua is not interested in winning conceptual debates. He is interested in freeing a person from fear. In that moment, a conceptual correction would have been irrelevant. What matters is that the man, in his final moments, passes from despair to trust.

Yeshua uses the language the man can receive. Just as he speaks in parables to bypass mental resistances, he speaks here in terms that can be heard by a man caught between life and death. The truth is not betrayed by symbolic language. It is betrayed only when the symbol is confused with the literal map. Something essential becomes visible here: for Yeshua, death does not interrupt the Kingdom. It does not suspend it. It does not defer it.

If the Kingdom were only a social or psychological reality, death would have annulled it. But Yeshua speaks of the Kingdom as a reality deeper than biological life. This is why he can say, without contradiction, that a person can enter this reality even while dying. We do not have here two doctrines—one for life and one for death—but a single reality that traverses both.

## What Repentance Means

For Yeshua, repentance (metanoia) is not a condemnation of the self, nor a moral self-flagellation. It is a change in the way of seeing, an awakening of consciousness that resettles a person in truth. Yeshua does not begin by saying "you are guilty," but "wake up," calling a person to come out of blindness and enter into the living reality of the Kingdom. This is why his inaugural message is not about punishment, but about nearness: "Repent, for the Kingdom of Heaven is near" (Matthew 4:17).

The nearness of the Kingdom does not demand payment, but inner availability; repentance is the living response to the presence of God, here and now. In Yeshua's logic, repentance is verified by fruit, not by declarations. The change of mind is seen in a life that aligns naturally with the truth: "Bear fruit worthy of repentance" (Matthew 3:8). In his encounters, repentance appears as a reorientation of the heart.

The tax collector, the woman caught in adultery, the prodigal son are not forced to hate themselves; they see differently and, seeing, they live differently. The words "Go and sin no more" (John 8:11) are not a threat, but a liberation, and the joy of the Father—"My son was dead and is alive again" (Luke 15:24)—shows that repentance, for Yeshua, is not the fear of punishment, but the coming out of inner death and the entering into life. With Paul, repentance takes on a more moral-theological form.

The person is seen first of all as a sinner in relation to the Law, and repentance becomes the awareness of this state and the turning toward God through faith in Christ. The diagnosis is universal: "All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23), and the call is general: "God now commands all people everywhere to repent" (Acts 17:30).

Paul preserves the idea of change, but binds it to the death of the old self and the receiving of a new identity through grace. Repentance is an act of passage: "Our old self was crucified with him" (Romans 6:6), and the result is a reconfigured existence: "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17). The emphasis shifts from the awakening of consciousness (central in Yeshua) to the resolution of the problem of sin through faith.

Repentance becomes the juridical gate of justification and grace, more than the living process of daily transformation. In contemporary Christianity, repentance is often reduced to the acknowledgment of guilt, the expression of remorse, and conformity to moral or ritual norms. Metanoia as a change of consciousness is frequently replaced by shame or fear.

Profound verses such as "A broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise" (Psalm 51:17) are sometimes used to cultivate an identity of permanent sinfulness, rather than a real transformation. Thus, repentance comes to mean "acknowledge that you are bad and accept forgiveness," without speaking any further about becoming, presence, and fruit. In practice, it becomes bound to confession without inner change, to declarations of faith without a transformed life, and to moralism without love.

Yeshua had warned precisely of this danger: "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me" (Matthew 15:8). When repentance stops at administered guilt, life remains unchanged. For Yeshua, repentance is awakening: you see the truth and, seeing, you change. For Paul, repentance is turning through faith: you acknowledge sin and receive a new identity through grace.

In today's Christianity, repentance often becomes managed guilt: you acknowledge, you are forgiven, but you remain the same. Yet Yeshua binds repentance to full life: "I came that the sheep may have life, and have it abundantly" (John 10:10). Repentance, as Yeshua lives it, does not diminish a person—it frees him. It does not fix him in the past, but brings him into the living present of the Kingdom. "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32) does not describe a burden, but an opening.

While Paul builds a theological framework of salvation, Yeshua calls to a living transformation, here and now. Today's Christianity is invited to return to the original meaning of repentance: the change of mind that gives birth to a new life, not merely a more burdened conscience. From this perspective, repentance takes on a meaning entirely different from the moralistic one.

In the language of the New Testament, the word translated as "repentance" is metanoia, which means a change of mind, an awakening of consciousness. Repentance does not begin with regret over the past, but with the moment in which a person begins to see what was previously hidden. It is not primarily sorrow for what you have done, but clarity about your own contribution within relationships.

In the moment a person sees the beam in his own eye, something fundamental changes: attention is no longer directed toward the guilt of others, but toward one's own heart. This inner change is described with remarkable simplicity in the parable of the prodigal son. The decisive moment is not the physical return of the son, but the instant in which the text says, "He came to himself" (Luke 15:17). In that moment, metanoia takes place.

The son ceases to interpret life through the lens of circumstances and begins to see the reality about himself. This is why he says, "I will rise and go to my father and say to him: Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you" (Luke 15:18). Repentance is not moral self-flagellation, but the lucid acknowledgment of one's own responsibility. Thus, repentance is not primarily a return to morality, but a return to oneself.

It is the moment in which a person ceases to flee from the truth and becomes capable of seeing himself without defense. In this light, relationships become the mirror in which the beam in our own eye becomes visible. Conflicts, wounds, and tensions are not merely external problems, but revelations of our interior. They bring to the surface what would otherwise remain hidden.

This is why sin, in the profound sense of Yeshua's teaching, is not only the wrong action, but the tendency to accuse others while remaining blind to one's own heart. And repentance is not only the abandonment of a behavior, but the abandonment of this blindness. It is the passage from judgment to consciousness, from accusation to responsibility. In the moment this change takes place, relationships can begin to be repaired.

Not because others have changed, but because the person has begun to see clearly. Exactly as Yeshua says: "First take the beam out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly" (Matthew 7:5). Inner clarity thus becomes the beginning of the healing of relationships. Repentance, therefore, is not a return to a list of moral rules, but a return to truth, to consciousness, and to oneself.

# 4. Discipleship

## Discipleship as a Process of Disidentification

Discipleship, in Yeshua's sense, is neither the accumulation of religious knowledge nor conformity to a set of rules. It is something far deeper and more destabilizing: disidentification. To follow Yeshua means to allow your ordinary identities — religious, moral, social — to be called into question and, little by little, left behind. Yeshua does not reinforce existing identities; he undermines them.

He tells his disciples that it does not matter who is "the greatest" (Mark 9:34–35), that titles confer no worth (Matthew 23:8–10), that religious belonging is no guarantee of nearness to God (Matthew 7:21). All these statements strike directly at the mechanisms by which people construct a solid, defensive self. Disidentification does not mean the loss of personality, but the loss of the illusion that our identity is what saves us.

Yeshua observes that people define themselves through roles: "I am righteous," "I am faithful," "I am chosen." Such definitions create separation, pride, and fear. Discipleship dismantles these labels not through direct criticism, but through experience: the disciples are constantly placed in situations where their image of themselves collapses. Yeshua does not explain this process in theory; he creates it. He sends them out without securities (Luke 10:4), exposes them to failure, lets them get things wrong.

Not in order to humiliate them, but to free them from the need to be "someone." True discipleship begins when a person no longer has anything to defend. In this sense, disidentification is the condition of freedom. When you no longer identify with your roles, you can love without controlling, you can speak the truth without defending yourself, you can lose without being destroyed. Yeshua says: "Remain in me" (John 15:4). To remain is not an attachment of identity, but a presence beyond identity.

Discipleship comes to an end, paradoxically, through the disappearance of the need for discipleship. Yeshua says it is to their advantage that he go away (John 16:7). A leader who set out to build identity would never say such a thing. But Yeshua does not want disciples dependent on him; he wants free people who live from the same source. Disidentification does not lead to emptiness, but to an identity that no longer needs to be asserted.

## Following Yeshua Beyond Mother and Father

Yeshua speaks some of the most disquieting words in the Gospels when he declares: "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me" (Matthew 10:37), and "If anyone comes to me and does not hate his father and his mother… he cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:26). These statements have often been read literally, moralized, or used to justify family ruptures. But Yeshua's language is not one of emotional abandonment, but of disidentification. He is not calling for the absence of love, but for a love freed from dependence. In the Semitic context, "to hate" does not mean to detest, but to relativize a priority. Yeshua does not ask for the severing of blood ties, but for moving beyond the stage in which your identity, your values, and your decisions are dictated by them.

"Let the dead bury their own dead" (Luke 9:60) is not an invitation to cynicism, but a line drawn between a life lived out of inertia and a life lived out of consciousness. To follow Yeshua presupposes stepping out of unconscious loyalties: inherited patterns, the fears of one's parents, beliefs passed on without discernment. "No one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the Kingdom of God" (Luke 9:62).

Looking back is not memory, but dependence on the past as a source of identity. When he is told that his mother and brothers are looking for him, Yeshua replies: "Who is my mother, and who are my brothers?… Whoever does the will of my Father is my brother and sister and mother" (Matthew 12:48–50). Here a radical mutation takes place: family is no longer defined biologically, but relationally and consciously. It is not blood that creates belonging, but inner alignment.

This statement does not diminish the family; it enlarges it. It does not deny the family; it removes it from exclusivity. To follow Yeshua means to cease living your life as an extension of your parents' history and to begin living out of the truth of the present. For the first time, Yeshua clearly separates love from unconscious submission. "Whoever does not give up all that he has cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:33).

"All that he has" includes emotional identities as well: "the good son," "the daughter who never disappoints," "the child who fixes everything." Following is not a new obedience, but a freedom that can love without losing itself. That is why, paradoxically, Yeshua reaffirms the command to honor your parents (Mark 7:10), but he does so from a higher level: not out of fear or duty, but out of maturity. True honoring appears only when you no longer need approval.

Yeshua's call is not an escape from relationships, but full presence within them. "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32) — free even from the family roles that suffocate you. This freedom does not destroy bonds; it cleanses them of manipulation, guilt, and fear. When he says "Come, follow me" (Mark 1:17), Yeshua does not offer a path of substitution, but a path of alignment. To follow him means to live from the center, not from the periphery of other people's expectations.

It means loving your parents not because "you have to," but because you can.

## What "Losing Your Life" Means for Yeshua

Yeshua's statement, "Whoever loses his life for my sake will gain it" (Mark 8:35), is one of the most badly misunderstood in the Gospels. Read literally, it has been used to glorify suffering, pointless sacrifice, or even physical death. But in Yeshua's language, the "life" he speaks of is not biological existence, but the identity a person constructs in order to protect himself from fear.

The word used in the Greek text is *psyche*, which points rather to "the soul," "the self," "the center of identity." To lose your life does not mean to cease existing, but to let that self dissolve — the self built out of defense, comparison, status, and control. It is a matter of relinquishing the false life in order to discover the real one. Yeshua observes that most people live in a constant effort of self-preservation. They defend their image, their position, their rightness, their security.

This life is tense, anxious, fragile. Any threat destabilizes it. That is why he says that whoever tries to "save" this life will lose it. The more tightly you cling to your defensive identity, the more of a prisoner you become. To lose your life, in Yeshua's sense, is an act of detachment. It is the relinquishing of the false center of the self so that another center may emerge, one that no longer needs protection.

"Whoever remains in me and I in him bears much fruit" (John 15:5). Fruit appears not from the effort to become someone, but from giving up the defense of what you are. This loss is not a dramatic moment, but a process. The disciples live through it gradually, through failures, misunderstandings, shames, and fears laid bare. Peter "loses his life" when his image of himself as a courageous disciple collapses through denial. Yet it is precisely there that a truer relationship appears.

The life that is gained is not more spectacular, but freer. For Yeshua, true life is not the life that defends itself, but the life that gives itself without calculation. Not in order to be martyred, but because it is no longer built on fear. "I came that the sheep may have life and have it abundantly" (John 10:10). Abundance is not quantitative, but qualitative: a life that no longer has to be saved at every moment.

## Does Yeshua Want to Be Defended?

From this arises an uncomfortable question: does Yeshua want to be defended by his followers? The Gospels show that he does not. He does not ask his disciples to protect him, but to follow him: "If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me" (Matthew 16:24). He does not tell them to fight for him, but to live as he lives. Before Pilate, he explains plainly why: "My kingdom is not of this world. If it were, my servants would have fought" (John 18:36).

If a fight is needed to defend the faith, then the source of that fight is not his kingdom. Truth, in Yeshua's vision, does not need protection, but living witness. The moment his followers begin to "defend" him through force, control, pressure, guilt, or fear, they are using the very instruments he refused. They defend him by means that contradict him. Thus the phrase "Get behind me" takes on its real meaning. It is not an insult. It is a realignment of places.

Do not try to tell me which way I must go; come behind me. Peter tries to lead Yeshua onto a safer road. Yeshua sets him back in the right position: that of a disciple. The deep meaning of this episode is clear: Yeshua does not want to be defended, but reflected. He does not seek people to protect him, but people who live without fear, as he does. Because, paradoxically, the moment you begin to defend Yeshua by force, you have already moved away from him.

To follow him means to walk the same road of truth — even when it is uncomfortable, vulnerable, and stripped of human protection.

## Why Yeshua Wants to Be Followed

If Yeshua refuses political power, another question immediately arises: why does he ask to be followed? At first glance, the call "Follow me" seems to create a personal dependence, a new center of authority. But a careful reading shows exactly the opposite: Yeshua asks to be followed not in order to be confirmed, but so that a person may be transformed. Yeshua never says: "Admire me," "Venerate me," or "Believe the correct things about me." He says: "Follow me" (Mark 8:34).

This call is not intellectual, but existential. To follow Yeshua means to enter a process of unlearning: the unlearning of fear, of attachment to status, of the security offered by power and control. That is why the call is not comfortable and is not addressed to everyone in the same way. Following presupposes movement. It is not a static adherence to a doctrine, but a change of direction.

"Come after me" does not mean "stay where you are and think differently," but "learn to live differently." That is why Yeshua offers no guarantees of safety: "Foxes have dens…, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head" (Luke 9:58). To follow him does not mean to gain protection, but to give up the illusions of control. Yeshua wants to be followed because the truth he lives cannot be handed over as information. It can be understood only through direct contact with a way of life.

That is why he calls his disciples "to be with him" (Mark 3:14). Transformation takes place through proximity, through observing a man who reacts without fear, who loves without controlling, who tells the truth without defending himself. Following is not an end in itself. Yeshua does not gather followers in order to consolidate his influence. The proof is that he constantly prepares them for his departure. "It is to your advantage that I go away" (John 16:7). A leader dependent on his followers would never say such a thing.

Yeshua does not want people bound to his person, but people who can live out of the same freedom. Following is temporary; the goal is inner autonomy. That is why Yeshua tells his disciples that they will do greater works than his (John 14:12). Such a statement would be absurd for someone seeking veneration. But it is perfectly coherent for someone who sees in being followed a process of maturation, not a permanent hierarchy.

The call to follow Yeshua is, in essence, the call to learn to live without fear in a world dominated by fear. "Whoever loses his life for my sake will gain it" (Mark 8:35). Here "life" is not biological existence, but the identity built on defense, status, and control. To follow Yeshua means to let this identity dissolve in order to discover one that no longer needs to defend itself.

Yeshua wants to be followed not in order to be the center of the world, but to show that the world can be lived from another center. Following is not submission, but initiation. It is not obedience, but learning. It is not the loss of the self, but its liberation from fear.

## "Get Behind Me, Satan" — The Disciple's Place and the Teacher's Road

Few moments in the Gospels are more unsettling than this one. Peter has just uttered the highest confession concerning Yeshua's identity: "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" (Matthew 16:16). Yeshua answers him with a rare blessing: "Blessed are you, Simon… for flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 16:17). In just a few moments, Peter is the bearer of a revelation that comes "from above."

And yet, only a few verses later, the same Peter hears one of the harshest replies recorded in Scripture: "Get behind me, Satan! You are a stumbling block to me, for your thoughts are not the thoughts of God, but of men" (Matthew 16:23). This abrupt shift is not about Peter's worth as a man, but about the mentality he expresses in that moment. The context explains the tension.

"From that time on, Jesus began to tell his disciples that he must go to Jerusalem, suffer much… be killed, and on the third day be raised" (Matthew 16:21). Peter takes him aside and rebukes him with friendly concern: "God forbid it, Lord! This shall never happen to you!" (Matthew 16:22). There is no malice here. There is love, loyalty, protectiveness — the natural reflex of someone who cannot bear the thought of loss.

Peter loves Yeshua with a human, protective love, born of the fear of suffering. It is precisely here that the thread breaks. Because, in that moment, Peter voices the same logic Yeshua had already encountered in the wilderness: "If you are the Son of God…" (Matthew 4). The temptation to avoid suffering, to use power to save yourself, to choose safety over truth.

In the language of the Gospels, this is "the logic of Satan": the logic of fear, of self-protection, of avoiding the cross. It is not Peter who is "Satan," but the perspective he brings: do not go down this road; protect yourself. Yeshua recognizes in these words the same vibration of fear that tries to turn him aside from the road of obedience to the very end. The irony runs deep.

Peter, who has just received the promise that he will be a "stone" in the building (Matthew 16:18), becomes in the next moment a "stumbling block." Why? Because he tries to align Yeshua with the human logic of survival rather than with the will of the Father. "Your thoughts are not the thoughts of God, but of men" (Matthew 16:23) is not an insult, but a diagnosis: man thinks in terms of safety, God thinks in terms of truth. The same theme reappears in Gethsemane.

When the soldiers come to arrest him, "Simon Peter… drew his sword, struck the servant of the high priest, and cut off his right ear" (John 18:10). Yeshua's reaction is immediate: "Put your sword back into its sheath. Shall I not drink the cup the Father has given me?" (John 18:11). And in Matthew he adds the clear warning: "All who take the sword will perish by the sword" (Matthew 26:52).

Yeshua refuses violent defense because it is born of fear and of the illusion that truth needs protection.

## "I Came That the Sheep May Have Life, and Have It Abundantly" (John 10:10)

"I came that the sheep may have life, and have it abundantly." (John 10:10) This statement is neither a material promise nor a moralizing metaphor. It is a declaration of existential intent. Yeshua does not speak of a life prolonged, protected, or conformed, but of a life intensely lived from within, a life that flows freely, unfragmented, unafraid. The "life" of which he speaks is not survival, but full participation in the real.

In Yeshua's language, life is not something that comes after death, but something that begins here, through awakening. "The Kingdom of God is within you." (Luke 17:21) To have life abundantly means to be alive before dying, present before being saved, whole before being correct.

## Why Does Yeshua Call People "Sheep"?

Yeshua calls people "sheep" not to humiliate them, but to describe a state of consciousness. The sheep is not an evil animal, but a vulnerable one — easily led, easily lost, dependent on a shepherd. The metaphor is not about inferiority, but about the lack of inner orientation when a person lives out of fear, conformity, and imitation. "We all wandered astray like sheep, each one going his own way." (Isaiah 53:6) The sheep follows the flock, not the truth.

It follows the loudest voice, not the truest one. Yeshua does not condemn people for this; he is noting a psychological and spiritual reality. That is why he says: "My sheep hear my voice; I know them, and they follow me." (John 10:27) His voice is not coercive, but recognizable. The sheep is not forced, but drawn. This is the key: Yeshua does not create dependence, but awakening. He does not want to be followed out of fear, but recognized from within.

## Why Does Yeshua Heal People?

Healings occupy a central place in the Gospels, but it is precisely this centrality that has produced one of the most persistent confusions: the idea that Yeshua heals in order to demonstrate his divinity. A careful reading, however, shows something else. Yeshua does not treat healing as a theological argument, but as a natural expression of the Kingdom of God.

He never says: "I heal you in order to prove who I am"; rather, he acts as though healing were what happens naturally when fear and exclusion are suspended. Consistently, Yeshua refuses to turn healings into spectacle or proof of authority. When he is challenged to perform signs to validate his identity, he refuses (Matthew 12:38–39). When he heals, he often asks people to tell no one (Mark 1:44).

This behavior is paradoxical if the aim had been the confirmation of his divinity. But it becomes coherent if the aim is the restoration of the human being, not the consolidation of a sacred image. For Yeshua, illness is not merely a biological problem. It is often bound up with social exclusion, shame, fear, and the loss of one's place in the community. Leprosy, blindness, paralysis remove people not only from health, but from relationship. Healing, in this sense, is an act of reintegration.

When Yeshua heals, he touches what was untouchable, draws near to those considered impure, speaks with the "invisible" ones (Mark 1:40–42). Healing is the sign that the Kingdom restores dignity, not merely the functioning of the body. It is significant that Yeshua frequently links healing to faith, but not in a dogmatic sense. "Your faith has healed you" (Mark 5:34) does not mean the acceptance of a doctrine, but openness to life, the stepping out of isolation and fear.

Healing is not a reward for faith, but the consequence of a reconnection. Yeshua creates a space in which a person can come out of the state of inner contraction that held him captive. That is why Yeshua's healings are often accompanied by forgiveness. "Your sins are forgiven," spoken to the paralytic (Mark 2:5), precedes his rising.

This is not a demonstration of divine power, but a shift of meaning: the fundamental problem is not only the body, but a person's relationship with himself, with God, and with the community. Healing appears as the effect of this realignment. The healings are not presented as logical proof of divinity, but as signs of the Kingdom. Even John the Baptist, when he doubts, receives not a definition but a descriptive answer: "The blind see, the lame walk…" (Matthew 11:4–5).

Not "I am," but "look at what is happening." Yeshua's identity is not proven, but recognized through its effects. In this light, healing does not confirm Yeshua's divinity in a metaphysical sense, but shows what life looks like when God is no longer perceived as a judge, but as a presence that restores. Healing is not an argument; it is revelation lived.

## Yeshua Did Not Ask to Be Followed Forever

Christianity turned "Follow me" into an eternal commandment. But in Yeshua's context, this call is temporary. It has meaning only until a person can walk alone. To follow Yeshua endlessly means, paradoxically, not to have understood him. Because Yeshua did not come to be the center of a crowd, but to kindle consciousnesses capable of standing alone in the truth. Discipleship ends not through abandonment, but through fulfillment. Not through betrayal, but through maturity.

Not through separation, but through interiorization. In this sense, Yeshua does not ask to be followed forever. He asks to be surpassed through ownership. And it is precisely here that his message becomes most uncomfortable — and most liberating. Yeshua speaks explicitly of his departure as something beneficial. "It is to your advantage that I go away." (John 16:7) This statement is impossible to digest within a theology of dependence. How could the departure of the teacher be an advantage?

Only if the goal is not attachment, but spiritual autonomy. His physical presence would have maintained the relationship of discipleship. His absence forces interiorization. Yeshua does not say: "Remain bound to me." He says: "You will know." Another key moment appears when he redefines his relationship with those who followed him. "I no longer call you servants, because a servant does not know what his master is doing; instead, I have called you friends." (John 15:15) The relationship of discipleship presupposes asymmetry.

The relationship of friendship presupposes equality. Through this move, Yeshua implicitly declares the end of discipleship as a structure. When you know, you no longer follow. When you see, you no longer believe. When you understand, you no longer need an intermediary.

## When Discipleship Ends

Discipleship, as it appears in the relationship between Yeshua and those who followed him, is not a permanent state. It is not a lifelong identity, nor a stable hierarchy, nor a sacralized dependence. Discipleship is a phase. A process. A road that has meaning only as long as there is a real need to be taught. Yeshua never asked to be followed endlessly. He asked to be understood. "I have told you these things while I am still with you." (John 14:25)

This formulation already contains the idea of parting. His presence is not conceived as a permanent center around which others are to gravitate forever, but as a catalyst. He appears, clarifies, shakes things up, heals — and then withdraws. Discipleship exists only as long as a person cannot yet stand alone in the truth. In the religious tradition, discipleship was turned into a supreme ideal: to follow, to imitate, to remain attached. But for Yeshua, discipleship is not the final goal. It is the transition.

"Teacher, where are you staying?" "Come and see." (John 1:38–39) Yeshua does not say "come and stay." He says "come and see." Seeing is the key. Once you see, you no longer need to be led by the hand. The disciple is not called to remain a disciple, but to become a whole person. That is why Yeshua does not create dependence. He does not position himself as an indispensable intermediary. On the contrary, he constantly shifts the center away from himself and toward the interior of the person.

"The Kingdom of God is within you." (Luke 17:21) If the kingdom is within, discipleship cannot be eternal. At a certain point, the search on the outside becomes pointless.

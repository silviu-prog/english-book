# 11. Yeshua in the Real World

## Was Yeshua a Conservative or a Progressive? A Socialist or a Capitalist?

The question of whether Yeshua was a conservative or a progressive, a socialist or a capitalist, arises naturally from the modern desire to place him within a familiar political grid. The problem is that these categories are historically anachronistic and ontologically insufficient for Yeshua's message. He does not come to adjust an existing social system, nor to propose a new one; he comes to change the meaning from which people live. From that changed meaning, social structures rearrange themselves—but not through ideology, rather through inner transformation. Seen superficially, Yeshua appears conservative. He quotes the Scriptures, speaks about the Law, moves within Jewish tradition, and declares: "I have not come to abolish the Law, but to fulfill it" (Matthew 5:17). This statement has often been used to align him with a conservative position, one of preserving order.

But the way he "fulfills" the Law undermines any classic conservatism: he shifts the center from the letter to the heart, from the norm to conscience, from control to lived truth (Matthew 5). Conservatism preserves the form; Yeshua preserves the meaning and abandons the form when it kills life. At the same time, Yeshua appears deeply progressive. He breaks down social barriers, speaks with women in a patriarchal culture (John 4), touches the "unclean" (Mark 1:40–42), eats with the marginalized (Mark 2:15–17), and proclaims radical reversals of hierarchy: "the last will be first" (Matthew 20:16). These gestures resemble a social progressivism *avant la lettre*. Yet Yeshua does not fight for rights through political confrontation, and he does not demand changes to the laws of the state.

His progressivism is not programmatic but ontological: he changes the way one sees the human being before changing any structures. When we shift the discussion to the economic plane, the temptation to call him a "socialist" arises quickly. Yeshua speaks harshly about wealth, warns against accumulation ("do not store up treasures on earth"—Matthew 6:19), demands renunciation where attachment becomes an idol (Mark 10:21), and describes communities of sharing (Acts 2:44–45, as a later effect).

But Yeshua does not propose imposed redistribution and does not create an economic system. He does not ask the state to confiscate and distribute; he asks the individual to detach. For Yeshua, solidarity is voluntary, born of freedom, not of coercion. On the other hand, Yeshua is not a capitalist. Capitalism presupposes the maximization of self-interest, competition, and accumulation as a virtue.

Yeshua says explicitly that life does not consist in an abundance of goods (Luke 12:15), that you cannot serve money as a master (Matthew 6:24), and that security built on accumulation produces fear (Matthew 6:25–34). In his parables, the critique is not against work or initiative, but against the reduction of life to profit (Luke 12:16–21). He does not demonize property; he demonizes identification with it. Yeshua constantly refuses political power.

After the multiplication of the loaves, when the crowd wants to make him king, he withdraws (John 6:15). To the question about taxes, he answers without legitimizing or contesting the system: "Give to Caesar what is Caesar's" (Mark 12:17). This refusal shows clearly that Yeshua does not want to be a political reformer. He does not change the world by seizing power, but by changing the center from which people live. This is why his language is consistently trans-ideological.

He does not say "change the system," but "do not be afraid" (Luke 12:32). He does not say "redistribute by law," but "sell, give, and follow me" (Mark 10:21). He does not say "revolt," but "blessed are the meek" (Matthew 5:5). These are not slogans of the left or the right; they are shifts of consciousness that make both camps uncomfortable. Conservatism feels threatened by the relativizing of religious form. Progressivism feels frustrated by the absence of a coercive program. Capitalism is undermined by the critique of the idol of money. Socialism is contradicted by the refusal of constraint. Yeshua aligns himself with no ideology, because he does not begin from the question of the distribution of power, but from the healing of the fear that makes power necessary. In conclusion, Yeshua is neither conservative nor progressive in the political sense. He is neither socialist nor capitalist. He is radical in another register altogether. He changes the human being before changing the world.

And when the human being changes, the world rearranges itself without any further need for labels. Yeshua did not come to choose a system. He came to show what happens when a person no longer lives out of fear.

## Yeshua and Money

The way Yeshua speaks about money and social status is one of the most radical and most uncomfortable aspects of his message. Not because he condemns possession in itself or the social order as such, but because he sees in money and status the most subtle forms of idolatry: mechanisms by which a person shifts the source of his security away from God and onto control, accumulation, and external validation.

Yeshua does not treat money as a mere economic instrument, but as a spiritual indicator. "Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21) is not a moral warning but an existential diagnosis. The heart follows whatever promises security. This is why the problem is not that a person has money, but that money comes to possess the person. This perspective explains one of Yeshua's harshest statements: "You cannot serve two masters… you cannot serve God and Mammon" (Matthew 6:24). Mammon is not merely money as an object, but money as a master, as a principle of life. Yeshua does not say that money is evil, but that it becomes incompatible with God when it occupies the place of the ultimate source of security. Here, economics becomes theology. Yeshua observes that attachment to money produces fear.

This is why his discourse about money is always tied to his discourse about fear: "do not be anxious about your life" (Matthew 6:25). The obsessive concern for accumulation is not prudence but the expression of a life lived out of lack. For Yeshua, true wealth is not the quantity of resources, but the absence of the need to defend oneself through them. The episode of the rich young man is essential. When Yeshua tells him, "sell all you have… and come, follow me" (Mark 10:21), he is not proposing a universal economic program. He identifies precisely the place where that man's heart is bound. The young man's sadness does not come from the loss of his goods, but from the impossibility of giving up his false source of identity. This is why Yeshua concludes: "How hard it will be for those who have wealth to enter the Kingdom of God" (Mark 10:23). The difficulty is not moral but inner. The same logic applies to social status. Yeshua lives in a world deeply organized by hierarchy: pure–impure, rich–poor, learned–unlearned, man–woman.

He does not deny the existence of these structures, but he constantly undermines them from within. He draws near to those without status, eats with the marginalized, praises the faith of the despised (Mark 2:15–17; Luke 7:44–50). Status is not a criterion of truth. Repeatedly, Yeshua inverts the social logic: "the last will be first" (Matthew 20:16).

This statement is not a promise of social revenge, but the disclosure of the fact that status is a fragile construction, without relevance in the order of the Kingdom. True worth is not given by position, but by presence and inner freedom. Yeshua is especially attentive to the danger of religious symbolic capital. The Pharisees are not criticized for being rich, but for drawing their identity from their moral and religious position. "Woe to you… for you love the place of honor" (Matthew 23:6). Status becomes an idol when it is used to feel "above" others. Here, religion and status meet in a refined form of possession. Another essential element is the way Yeshua refuses political power and mass appeal. After the multiplication of the loaves, the crowd wants to make him king, and he withdraws (John 6:15).

Yeshua understands that popularity is a form of symbolic wealth, and that power is a form of accumulation. Both create dependence and distort the message. This is why he prefers to lose followers rather than gain influence (John 6:66). In Yeshua's parables, the rich are not automatically condemned, but they are constantly warned. The rich man whose land produced a good harvest is called a "fool" not because he gathered, but because he reduced his life to material security (Luke 12:16–21). The rich man in the parable of Lazarus is not accused of active cruelty, but of an indifference born of comfort (Luke 16:19–31). For Yeshua, wealth numbs the senses. In the end, Yeshua's vision of money and status is not ascetic but liberating. He does not demand poverty as a virtue, but freedom from anything that promises security in place of God. Money and status are not evil in themselves, but they become dangerous when they replace trust.

So, for Yeshua, the problem is not how much you have, but what you live from. Money and status can be used. But they cannot be served.

## Yeshua on Sexuality

Although Christianity is often perceived as profoundly preoccupied with sexuality, Yeshua speaks surprisingly little about this subject. And when he does, he does not construct a detailed sexual morality, but shifts the attention toward the heart, relationship, and responsibility. Yeshua does not preach about sexuality as a central theme. He does not classify practices, he does not build a code of the permitted and the forbidden, he does not fix sexuality as a major theological problem.

In the Gospels, the dominant themes are: the Kingdom of God, fear, money, power, religious hypocrisy, love, and forgiveness. Sexuality appears rarely and always in relational rather than doctrinal contexts. This silence is not accidental. It shows that, for Yeshua, sexuality is not the center of the human problem. A person is not called to correct his biological impulses in order to be accepted by God, but to clarify the inner center from which he lives.

When Yeshua addresses sexuality explicitly, he does so to reveal an inner dynamic, not to institute rules. "Anyone who looks at a woman with lust has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matthew 5:28). This statement is often read as a moral tightening, but its intention is different: Yeshua shifts the problem from the act to objectification.

For Yeshua, sexuality becomes problematic when the other is reduced to an object of desire, when relationship is replaced by consumption. The sin is not eros, but dehumanization. This is why Yeshua speaks about the purity of the heart (Matthew 5:8), not about the obsessive control of the body. Yeshua does not moralize about the sexuality of the marginalized. His relationship with the woman caught in adultery (John 8:3–11) is emblematic: he refuses to condemn her, refuses to shame her publicly, and refuses to turn her sexuality into a permanent identity. "Neither do I condemn you" precedes any invitation to change. Likewise, Yeshua eats and sits at table with those considered "immoral" (Matthew 9:10–13). Sexuality does not become a criterion for exclusion from nearness to God. For Yeshua, the problem is not what you have done, but what you live from now.

When he speaks about marriage, Yeshua quotes Genesis: "the two will become one flesh" (Matthew 19:5). The emphasis is not on the sexual act but on relational unity. Sexuality is understood as the expression of a whole relationship, not as an autonomous zone that must be controlled. Even here, Yeshua avoids constructing a sexual legislation. He acknowledges human complexity ("not everyone can receive this word"—Matthew 19:11) and leaves room for discernment, not for rigid standardization. With Paul, sexuality becomes much more visible and much more regulated. Paul writes to young communities confronted with moral chaos and cultural confusion, and sexuality becomes a sensitive terrain. He introduces a new language: the body as a "temple of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 6:19).

This metaphor moves sexuality from the relational sphere into the sphere of the regulated sacred. Sexuality is no longer only about the other, but about right relation to God. Paul speaks about debauchery, sexual immorality, self-control (1 Corinthians 6–7) and even recommends abstinence as an ideal, with marriage as a pragmatic solution: "it is better to marry than to burn" (1 Corinthians 7:9). In Paul, a hierarchy clearly appears: virginity is preferable, marriage is a concession, sexuality is potentially dangerous. This hierarchy is absent in Yeshua. The Christianity that came after Paul would amplify this direction. Sexuality becomes: the principal ground of guilt, a criterion of purity, an instrument of moral control, a zone of religious obsession. Detailed rules develop about what is permitted and forbidden, and the body is often regarded with suspicion.

Desire is associated with sin, pleasure with danger, and sexuality becomes one of the principal sources of religious shame. This shift has profound effects: people who feel separated from God not because they live in hatred or injustice, but because they feel desire. This would be a grave distortion of Yeshua's perspective, for whom the real problem was fear, not eros. In the Gospels, sexuality does not appear as a central theme of Yeshua's teaching. He does not construct an obsessive moral discourse about the body, desire, or sexual behavior, but constantly shifts attention toward the heart and toward the way people relate to one another. When he addresses the subject, he does so not to shame, but to expose objectification and the absence of love.

"Anyone who looks at a woman with lust has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matthew 5:28) is not a condemnation of desire, but of the transformation of the other into an object of inner consumption. The problem is not attraction, but the loss of the living relationship and the reduction of the person to a function. Yeshua refuses to shame and to exclude. Before the woman caught in adultery, he does not feed the crowd's moralizing spectacle, but simply says: "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone" (John 8:7). When the accusers leave, his words are liberating: "Neither do I condemn you" (John 8:11). Yeshua does not treat sexuality as a dirty zone of the human being, but as a place where shame and judgment can be dissolved through truth and where dignity can be restored.

His emphasis remains on the interior, on the quality of the relationship, and on the purity of the heart: "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God" (Matthew 5:8). Purity is not rigid behavioral control, but an inner transparency in which the other is not used but loved. In this logic, sexuality is one of the expressions of life, not the center of the human problem. With Paul, however, the tone changes. Sexuality becomes a sensitive zone in the life of the Christian community, a space that must be regulated in order to preserve the cohesion and holiness of the group. "Flee from sexual immorality" (1 Corinthians 6:18) is a direct exhortation, and his argument is theological and bodily: "Your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 6:19). The body takes on a sacral dimension that demands discipline and control.

Paul idealizes abstinence as the preferable state: "It is good for a man not to touch a woman" (1 Corinthians 7:1), and he sees marriage as a solution for avoiding the burning of passion: "if they cannot control themselves, they should marry" (1 Corinthians 7:9). Sexuality thus becomes a domain that must be carefully administered, so as not to deviate from the spiritual calling. The emphasis is no longer only on the heart and relationship, but on discipline and communal order.

In later Christianity, this concern is amplified and often transformed into a moral obsession. Sexuality comes to be treated as the principal field of spiritual struggle, and shame and guilt become pedagogical instruments. The body becomes suspect, desire becomes dangerous, and fear is used to shape behavior. What in Yeshua was freed from shame becomes once again laden with it.

Thus a subtle but profound shift occurs: from the healing of the relationship to the control of behavior, from the purity of the heart to the surveillance of the body. Yeshua frees the person from shame and brings them back into dignity; later theology reloads the body with suspicion and fear. Yeshua did not come to correct people's sexuality, but the way people look at and use one another. For him, the problem is not desire but objectification; not the body but the heart that loses love. When sexuality becomes a religious obsession, it is a sign that attention has moved from truth and relationship to control and fear. Yeshua frees the body from shame; later interpretations loaded it again.

## Yeshua on Homosexuality

The subject of homosexuality has become one of the most emotionally charged in contemporary Christianity—not because Yeshua spoke about it at length, but precisely because he did not speak about it at all. While later theology transformed this subject into a major moral criterion, the Gospels preserve a complete silence. This silence is not an accidental omission, but a profound theological indication of what Yeshua considered truly central in human life.

In the Gospels, Yeshua speaks extensively about fear, about money and power, about religious hypocrisy, about judgment, about love, forgiveness, and compassion. By contrast, he does not discuss sexual orientations, he does not classify types of relationships, and he does not construct an ethics of desire based on gender. This absence is all the more relevant given that homosexuality was known in the Greco-Roman world of the time. If he had considered it a central problem of the human condition, he would have addressed it.

Instead, Yeshua constantly shifts attention from labels to the heart: "Do not judge, so that you will not be judged" (Matthew 7:1) and "Out of the heart come evil thoughts" (Matthew 15:19) show that, for him, the human problem is not whom one loves, but from what inner center one loves. And this completely shifts the discussion. When Yeshua speaks about morality, the supreme criterion is love: "Love your neighbor as yourself. On these two commandments hang all the Law and the Prophets" (Matthew 22:39–40). Love becomes the measure of all relationships, not conformity to a social or biological pattern. In his encounters with people, Yeshua constantly refuses to exclude on the basis of reputation or the perception of "immorality." He eats with those considered undesirable (Matthew 9:10–13), refuses public shaming, and restores the dignity of the stigmatized.

I know this is hard for many to accept, but any reading that turns Yeshua into an arbiter of sexual orientations comes into direct tension with his concrete behavior. It is often invoked that Yeshua quotes Genesis: "From the beginning, God made them male and female" (Matthew 19:4) as an argument for the exclusion of other forms of relationship. But the context is essential: Yeshua is answering a question about divorce and the superficiality with which men abandoned their wives. He uses Genesis to protect the fidelity and dignity of the relationship, not to create a doctrine of exclusion. His emphasis is on responsibility and permanence, not on limiting love to a single formula. With Paul, however, the discourse changes.

Paul explicitly mentions relationships between persons of the same sex in passages such as Romans 1:26–27 and 1 Corinthians 6:9. These texts appear in a cultural context marked by practices of sexual exploitation, pederasty, relationships of power, and idolatrous rituals in which sexuality was used as an instrument. Paul does not analyze orientation as a psychological identity—a concept that did not exist then—but behaviors that he sees as expressions of moral disorder in a world far from God. Still, by including these behaviors in a list of sins, sexuality enters into a juridical logic of guilt, different from Yeshua's relational approach. Later Christianity would take a further step and transform these verses into criteria of identity.

Homosexuality is no longer regarded as a behavior within a specific historical context, but as a sign of depravity, an ontological deviation, and a threat to the divine order. From this shift derive stigmatization, exclusion, and shaming, and love comes to be associated with guilt. In many traditions, homosexuality becomes graver than a lack of compassion, injustice, or hypocrisy—a hierarchy of sins foreign to Yeshua's priorities.

The difference in perspective is clear. In Yeshua there is no explicit discourse about homosexuality and no attempt to define or condemn it. He evaluates relationships by truth, fidelity, and the capacity to love without dominating or objectifying. In Paul, homosexuality appears as part of a list of behaviors within a logic of moral order. In later Christianity, it becomes a condemned identity, and fear and shame are amplified theologically. Yeshua did not come to classify people's sexual orientations, but to free relationships from fear, shame, and violence. If someone were to ask him what he says about homosexuality, his answer would not be a label, but a deeper question, in keeping with everything he says and does: "Do you love in truth?" and "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:16). For Yeshua, this is the measure. The rest are constructions that appeared later.

## How Yeshua Views Monogamy

Yeshua does not speak about monogamy as a social rule, but as a consequence of inner truth. When he is asked about marriage, he does not begin with norms but with unity: "the two will become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24, quoted in Matthew 19:5). For Yeshua, monogamy is not a contract of exclusivity but the expression of a wholeness. Where a person is fragmented, the relationship becomes possessive. Where a person is whole, exclusivity appears naturally, not by imposition.

Yeshua does not praise monogamy as a virtue in itself. He does not elevate it to the rank of a separate moral ideal. He speaks about truth and about the heart. "Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21). Monogamy, in Yeshua's logic, is the stability that appears when the heart is no longer pulled in opposite directions by fear, lack, or the need for validation. This is why Yeshua does not construct a discourse about fidelity as an obligation, but about presence. Betrayal, adultery, flight are not, for him, behavioral problems, but signs of a heart that can no longer remain. "Anyone who looks at a woman with lust has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matthew 5:28). Here Yeshua shifts the discussion from the exterior to the interior: the problem is not the act, but the inner detachment that precedes the act.

## What Yeshua Says About Divorce

Divorce is one of the few subjects on which Yeshua appears harsh. "What God has joined together, let no one separate" (Matthew 19:6). But the harshness is not juridical but ontological. Yeshua does not defend the institution of marriage; he defends the reality of the union. When two people have become "one flesh," separation is not neutral. It tears something real, not merely a contract.

Yet Yeshua introduces an essential nuance: "Moses permitted you to divorce your wives because of the hardness of your hearts" (Matthew 19:8). Here Yeshua explicitly acknowledges that there are situations in which the heart can no longer live the truth of the union. Divorce is not the ideal, but neither is it condemned as an absolute sin. It is a concession made to human fragility, not a command. What matters is that Yeshua never says: "stay together at any cost." He says: the truth of the union cannot be mimicked. When the relationship has become a place of fear, domination, lies, or inner death, preserving the form no longer serves life. Yeshua is radical not in the direction of preserving the institution, but in the direction of authenticity. Paul takes up Yeshua's words about marriage, but moves them into a different framework. He thinks in terms of the organization of the community, not of inner experience.

In 1 Corinthians 7, Paul says explicitly: "Each one should remain in the condition in which the calling found him" (1 Corinthians 7:20). Stability becomes a value in itself, in order to avoid communal chaos. For Paul, marriage is often a practical solution: "it is better to marry than to burn" (1 Corinthians 7:9). Here the major difference from Yeshua becomes visible. In Yeshua, the relationship is the expression of a whole heart. In Paul, the relationship becomes a mechanism for regulating desire. This is not wrong, but it is clearly a different level of approach. Regarding divorce, Paul introduces explicit rules, including situations of accepted separation (1 Corinthians 7:15). The emphasis falls on order, peace, functionality. Paul does not speak from the realm of existential revelation, but from the realm of administering reality. He does not directly contradict Yeshua, but neither does he remain at the same depth.

Institutional Christianity took up mainly the Pauline reading and transformed it into moral law. Monogamy became a criterion of belonging. Divorce became a label. The relationship was removed from the realm of inner truth and moved into the realm of religious status. Today, in many Christian contexts, marriage is defended even when it is inwardly dead. The form is defended at the expense of life. Divorce is condemned without asking what has already died inside.

This is exactly the inversion of Yeshua's message, who said that "the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27). The relationship was made for life, not life for the relationship. Today's Christianity speaks much about fidelity and little about presence. About covenant and little about truth. About sin and little about fear. Within this framework, monogamy becomes constraint, not expression. Divorce becomes shame, not the sign of a human limit. For Yeshua, monogamy is the truth of a whole heart. For Paul, it is a solution of order and stability. For today's Christianity, it has often become a norm of identity. Yeshua does not ask: "Did you stay?" His question is more radical: "Did you stay alive?"

## The Family in Yeshua's Vision

For Yeshua, the family is neither abolished nor absolutized. He does not attack it, but neither does he raise it to the supreme rank of human life. In his words and gestures a positioning appears that differs from the traditional one: the family is important, but it is not the ultimate authority over a person's conscience. Yeshua honors the old commandment: "Honor your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12). He rebukes those who empty it of meaning through religious artifices, showing that respect for parents must be real, not merely declared: "You have nullified the Word of God by your tradition" (Mark 7:9–13). For him, the family deserves genuine care and responsibility. But this honoring is not blind. It does not mean unconditional submission, nor the sacrifice of inner truth for the apparent peace of the family. Honoring does not cancel discernment.

The rupture appears when the family becomes an obstacle in the path of lived truth. "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me" (Matthew 10:37). The statement is not emotional but axiological: nothing external can take priority over truth. For Yeshua, blood ties can sustain life, but they cannot define it. When the family demands conformity at the expense of conscience, it must be relativized.

This relativization is seen clearly when he is told that his mother and brothers are looking for him. Yeshua answers: "Who is my mother, and who are my brothers?… Whoever does the will of my Father is my brother and sister and mother" (Matthew 12:48–50). He does not reject the biological family but extends the meaning of family. Belonging is no longer dictated by blood, but by the level of consciousness. The family becomes a community of meaning, not a structure of possession.

Yeshua also refuses the idea that duty to family can justify postponing one's personal calling: "Let the dead bury their own dead; you go and proclaim the Kingdom of God" (Luke 9:60). This is not a lack of compassion, but a delimitation between a life lived out of inertia and a life lived out of calling. When the family becomes an excuse for not living authentically, it ceases to be a support and becomes a chain.

In Yeshua's vision, relationships are not meant to preserve emotional childhood, but to facilitate maturation. "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32). This freedom includes liberation from unconscious loyalties, from inherited guilt, from imposed roles. The family that demands the sacrifice of the self is not supported by Yeshua. The family that allows becoming, yes. Is the family the most important thing for Yeshua? The answer is clear: no. The most important thing is lived truth and the Kingdom of God as an inner state: "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21). The family is important only insofar as it does not replace this calling. Paradoxically, it is precisely through this relativization that the family can be loved more purely. Not out of fear, not out of obligation, but out of freedom. "Seek first the Kingdom of God" (Matthew 6:33). In this order, the family is not lost—it is healed.

## Yeshua on Alcohol

Yeshua drank wine. He takes part in meals, at weddings, at feasts, and his religious contemporaries criticize him precisely for this freedom, saying that "the Son of Man came eating and drinking, and they say, 'Here is a glutton and a drunkard'" (Matthew 11:19). At the wedding in Cana, Yeshua not only accepts the presence of wine but turns water into wine, and the guests observe that "you have kept the good wine until now" (John 2:10). Such an episode would be incomprehensible if Yeshua had been against alcohol in itself. Along the same line, he affirms that "it is not what enters the mouth that defiles a person, but what comes out of the mouth" (Matthew 15:11). The problem is not the substance, but the inner state of the person. Yeshua does not preach abstinence and does not turn the spiritual life into an austere regimen. He shifts attention from the object to consciousness.

When he speaks about drunkenness, Yeshua does not treat it as a ritual violation, but as a loss of presence and inner watchfulness. He warns: "Watch yourselves, lest your hearts be weighed down with dissipation, drunkenness, and the cares of this life" (Luke 21:34). Drunkenness is, for him, a state of numbing of consciousness, the opposite of the call to lucidity. This is why he says: "Watch and pray" (Matthew 26:41). The call is not to puritanism but to sobriety. Yeshua clearly distinguishes himself from the radical asceticism of John the Baptist. "John came neither eating nor drinking… the Son of Man came eating and drinking" (Matthew 11:18–19). This opposition is essential: Yeshua's spirituality is not manifested through the refusal of life, but through the free and conscious way of living it. He shows that the Kingdom does not depend on dietary rules or prohibitions, but on the state of the heart.

If he were asked about drunkenness, Yeshua would not place the emphasis on quantity, but on motive. A pure heart, of which he says "blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God" (Matthew 5:8), is a lucid, unfragmented heart. Drunkenness does the opposite: it breaks contact with oneself, with others, and with God. The apostle Paul approaches the issue in a more normative way. He accepts the existence of wine, but draws clear lines against excess, saying: "Do not get drunk on wine, which is debauchery" (Ephesians 5:18) and "let us live decently… not in carousing and drunkenness" (Romans 13:13). For Paul, the body is a "temple," and behavior must be managed with discernment. This is why he affirms: "Everything is permitted to me, but not everything is beneficial" (1 Corinthians 6:12). Yet Paul is not a radical abstainer. He recommends to Timothy: "Stop drinking only water, and use a little wine because of your stomach" (1 Timothy 5:23). So he does not condemn alcohol, but the loss of measure and self-control.

Contemporary Christianity often oscillates between extremes. Some currents promote a moralizing abstinence and try to forcibly reinterpret the Gospel episodes; others adopt a freedom without discernment; and still others accept wine only in a liturgical context, but regard it with suspicion in real life. None of these positions fully reflects Yeshua's lucid freedom. His words "the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27) can be applied here as well: things are for the human being, not the human being for things. Consumption in itself is not the problem, but the relationship you have with it. From Yeshua's perspective, you can drink wine and remain spiritually awake if you remain present, free, and unattached. From Paul's perspective, you can do this if you remain in measure and self-control. From the rigid religious perspective, the rule tends at times to replace discernment. Yeshua drank wine, but he did not lose himself in it. He celebrated life, but did not let himself be absorbed by it. He says that he came "so that the sheep may have life, and have it abundantly" (John 10:10). The abundance he speaks of is not excess, but a living, conscious, and free presence in everything you do.

## How Yeshua Would Heal a Person Addicted to Drugs

The Gospels reveal a surprising pattern: Yeshua never begins with the symptom, but with the person. He does not treat the behavior before restoring belonging. He does not demand change before giving back dignity. In our modern language we would say that he does not begin with the "drug problem," but with the "problem of the lost son." This is why, if we ask ourselves how Yeshua would heal an addicted person, we must shift our gaze from the substance to the soul, from consumption to identity, from shame to belonging. In the parable of the prodigal son, the father does not demand explanations before the embrace. "While he was still far off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion; he ran and fell on his neck and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). This order is essential: first the nearness, then the conversation.

An addict often lives in an inner exile, in shame, self-contempt, and isolation. Yeshua would first restore belonging: "You still belong. You are still a son." Only afterward would the call to change come. The same thing happens when Yeshua eats together with tax collectors and sinners. "It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick" (Luke 5:31). The table is not a reward for virtue, but a medicine for alienation. His presence at the table is the declaration that the person is not reduced to his label. In the case of addiction, where identity compresses into "you are a junkie," Yeshua would break this reduction through communion, not through preaching. Another constant pattern is the separation of the person from the behavior. In the episode of the woman caught in adultery, Yeshua says: "Neither do I condemn you. Go, and sin no more" (John 8:11).

The order matters: first he removes the condemnation, then he calls to a new life. Addiction feeds on shame; shame sustains the cycle. By removing the condemnation, Yeshua would cut the inner fuel of addiction before speaking about renunciation. The Gospels show something else subtle: around him, people grow calm. The storm subsides: "He commanded the wind and said to the sea, 'Be still!' The wind ceased, and there was a great calm" (Mark 4:39). The possessed man of Gadara comes to be "clothed and in his right mind" (Mark 5:15). In contemporary language, we would say that his presence regulates the nervous system. Addiction is often a desperate search for regulation through chemical shortcuts. Yeshua would offer what the substance promises and cannot give: security without escape, peace without numbness. Yeshua does not first offer new rules, but a new identity. "You are no longer slaves… but sons" (Galatians 4:7).

When identity changes, behavior begins to follow. The world tells the addict: "You are weak, you are broken, you are a problem." Yeshua would say: "You are a son. You are a daughter." Not: "Stop the drugs so that you may be worthy," but: "You are worthy; that is why you no longer need them." His call is an invitation, not a constraint: "Come, follow me" (Matthew 4:19). Addiction is compulsion, the loss of choice. Yeshua restores inner freedom through nearness and meaning. When a person feels once again that he can choose, he begins to step out of the circle in which he was trapped. What would this healing look like, concretely? It would not begin with detox, with prohibitions, or with moralizing. It would begin with sitting together, with the shared meal, with the gaze that does not recoil in disgust. It would be followed by the word that names who you are, not what you do. It would continue with the calm that settles into the body, with the breath that slows, with the shame that melts away.

And only then would the simple sentence come: "You no longer need this." And the person, for the first time, might be able to believe it. The essence is this: Yeshua would not first try to remove the drug from the person, but the reason the drug became necessary. When the cause disappears, the symptom loses its power. This is why healing in the Gospels is not a program for correcting behavior, but a restoration of the person. And where the person is restored, addiction no longer has anywhere to dwell.

# 5. The Commandments of Yeshua

## Why Yeshua Gave Commandments

At first glance it may seem paradoxical that Yeshua, the one who criticizes legalism and religious rigidity, should nevertheless give commandments. But to understand why Yeshua gives commandments, we must first clarify what the commandments are for him and what they are not. The commandments of Yeshua are not instruments of moral control, nor legal conditions for being accepted by God. They are descriptions of a life lived out of truth.

Yeshua does not begin from the idea that human beings must be coerced into being good, but from the conviction that a person, once he sees the truth, lives differently in a natural way. This is why the commandments of Yeshua are neither arbitrary nor detached from inner experience. They flow from a certain state of the heart. "If you love me, you will keep my commandments" (John 14:15) is not a condition but an observation: love produces a certain way of living.

Yeshua gives commandments because people were living out of fear, confusion, and wandering — not because God needed additional rules. The commandments are for human beings, not for God. He says so explicitly: "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27). This statement is the key to the whole of Yeshua's ethics. The Law exists in order to serve life; when it no longer does so, it must be reinterpreted.

When he is asked which is the greatest commandment, Yeshua invents nothing new. He quotes two texts from the Old Testament: "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart…" (Deuteronomy 6:5) and "you shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18). Then he makes the decisive statement: "On these two commandments depend all the Law and the Prophets" (Matthew 22:40). With this statement, Yeshua completely rearranges the architecture of religion.

He does not add a new commandment alongside the others; he offers the unifying principle. All the other commandments are valid only insofar as they express love. The Law is no longer a set of disparate obligations but a coherent expression of love. Where there is no love, the literal observance of the commandments becomes emptied of meaning. It is essential to notice that Yeshua does not say, "love replaces the Law," but that love fulfills it.

"I have not come to abolish the Law but to fulfill it" (Matthew 5:17). To fulfill, for Yeshua, does not mean to observe the letter perfectly, but to bring the Law to its real purpose. This is why he can break the Sabbath, touch the "unclean," and forgive sins without ritual, without considering himself outside the will of God. The commandments of Yeshua are few, but radical.

They do not regulate minor external behaviors; they touch the inner center: "Love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44), "Do not judge" (Matthew 7:1), "Forgive seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22), "Do not be afraid" (Luke 12:32). These are not rules that are easy to apply; they are descriptions of a transformed consciousness. Yeshua does not give them to be checked off, but to show what life looks like when a person no longer lives out of fear and separation.

When we turn to Paul, we notice a shift of emphasis. Paul states explicitly that a person is not justified by the works of the Law (Romans 3:28). In this sense, he seems to continue Yeshua's critique of legalism. But at the same time, Paul introduces other normative centers: faith in Christ, belonging to the community, obedience to certain structures and teachings. Paul does not annul the moral commandments, but he reforms them.

Instead of the commandments of Yeshua being the central doctrine, Paul places at the center: correct faith ("if you confess with your mouth…" — Romans 10:9), identity "in Christ," life "according to the Spirit" as opposed to "according to the flesh" (Romans 8). Although Paul says that "love is the fulfilling of the Law" (Romans 13:10), in practice Pauline Christianity will shift the emphasis from living like Yeshua to believing about Yeshua.

The commandments of Yeshua often become lofty moral ideals, but not the central criterion of salvation. In their place appear formulas of faith, communal rules, and doctrinal norms. Later Christianity will go even further, transforming the "commandments" into: dogmas to be accepted, moral rules to be obeyed, conditions of religious belonging.

Thus, what for Yeshua was a single simple commandment — the love that manifests itself in life — becomes a complex system of norms, beliefs, and obligations. Yeshua gave commandments in order to liberate, not to constrain. He gave the great commandment in order to unify everything that was fragmented. For Yeshua, love is the criterion of all the commandments.

For Paul and for later Christianity, the commandments of Yeshua are often secondary to faith, doctrine, and belonging. To understand this difference is not to reject Paul, but to see clearly that the commandments of Yeshua were not conceived as a new law, but as the expression of a life lived out of truth.

## Why Yeshua Says "Do Not Be Afraid" So Often

One of the most recurrent phrases in Yeshua's discourse is this: "do not be afraid." It is not a slogan of consolation, nor an emotional calming technique. Yeshua does not speak about fear in a superficial sense, as a passing emotion, but as the fundamental structure of human existence. Fear, in his view, is not merely a reaction to danger but the foundation on which attachments, violence, moralism, and the thirst for power are built.

Yeshua observes that people do not live led by truth, but by the fear of loss: the fear of losing life, status, security, belonging. This is why he says "do not be afraid" precisely in those moments when the logic of the world would say the opposite: when he speaks of the lack of material security (Matthew 6:25–34), of persecution (Matthew 10:28), of the unknown (Matthew 14:27), of death (Luke 12:4). The message is not "there is no danger," but "the danger is not what you think it is."

For Yeshua, fear is the principal barrier on the path to the Kingdom. It narrows consciousness, produces rigid attachments, and justifies moral compromises. People lie because they are afraid. They accumulate because they are afraid. They judge because they are afraid. They control because they are afraid. In this sense, "do not be afraid" is not a moral exhortation but a diagnosis and an invitation: if fear disappears, the structure of life reorganizes itself.

This is why Yeshua does not propose to eliminate fear through external promises, but through a change in the center of security. "Do not be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the Kingdom" (Luke 12:32). Security no longer comes from what you can lose, but from what cannot be taken. The Kingdom is not a future reward but a present foundation that renders fear useless. Yeshua understands that fear cannot be conquered by willpower.

This is why he does not say "you should not be afraid," but "do not be afraid," as a declaration of reality: fear loses its power when a person sees something more real than the threat. "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32) is not an intellectual metaphor but an existential affirmation: freedom appears when fear is no longer the meaning of life. In this sense, "do not be afraid" is the gateway into the whole of Yeshua's message.

Without this defusing of fear, love remains an ideal, forgiveness remains impossible, and following him remains theory. Yeshua does not demand heroism; he asks for a shift of trust. When fear no longer leads, the Kingdom becomes visible.

## "Do Not Be Afraid" — From Religious Fear to Filial Relationship

It is not merely a formula of spiritual consolation. It is a direct intervention upon a person's inner state. Fear contracts, closes, and isolates. Love relaxes, opens, and unites. This is why, every time people enter the presence of Yeshua, the first thing he does is to regulate this state of fear in them. When the disciples are terrified on the sea, he tells them: "Take heart, it is I; do not be afraid!" (Matthew 14:27).

When the women come to the tomb, seized by dread, the message is the same: "Do not be afraid" (Matthew 28:10). And when he sends the disciples out into the world, he tells them again: "Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul" (Matthew 10:28). Looked at closely, these words are not merely theological teaching. They are emotional regulation. Yeshua does not begin by demanding moral performance or abstract belief from people. He begins by calming their fear.

Because a person dominated by fear cannot love, cannot see clearly, and cannot enter into an authentic relationship. Fear keeps consciousness on the defensive. Love opens it. This is why, when he teaches the disciples how to relate to God, he does not tell them to fear him, but to pray like this: "Our Father who is in heaven…" (Matthew 6:9). Not "Our Judge," not "Our fearsome Master," but our Father.

In this single formulation, the religious paradigm of fear is already transformed into a paradigm of relationship. And yet, within the biblical tradition there is also the famous statement from Proverbs: "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom" (Proverbs 1:7). This sentence has often been interpreted as if the relationship with God ought to be permanently based on fear. But the text itself says something else: fear is the beginning, not the destination.

In the spiritual psychology of Scripture, fear can be the first step by which a person breaks free from arrogance and self-sufficiency. A person entirely absorbed in his own ego seeks nothing beyond himself. At times, the awareness of one's limits and vulnerability can begin in the form of a sacred fear. But if the spiritual path remains stuck there, it no longer evolves. Yeshua seems to carry this movement to the next level.

If wisdom begins with the fear of God, spiritual maturity ends with trust in his love. In the Sermon on the Mount, he says: "Do not be anxious about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink… Your heavenly Father knows that you need them" (Matthew 6:25–32). Notice the change of tone: God is no longer the figure you must fear, but the Father who already knows what you need. Fear belongs to the beginning of the road. Trust belongs to maturity.

This transformation also appears in the way Yeshua describes the relationship with God in his parables. In the parable of the prodigal son, the father is not a figure who inspires fear but one who runs to meet the returning son: "While he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion; he ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). If God were first of all an authority to be feared, this scene would make no sense.

But if God is first of all a fatherly heart, then the whole parable becomes revelatory: fear belongs to the son who returns trembling; love belongs to the father who receives him. From this perspective, the phrase "do not be afraid" takes on a far deeper dimension. Yeshua is not merely preaching a moral ideal. He is trying to lift a person out of a way of living based on fear and defensiveness.

Religious fear can control behavior, but it cannot produce authentic love. Love appears only when a person's inner system is no longer dominated by fear. This is why he says: "Do not be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the Kingdom" (Luke 12:32). In this sentence, God is no longer the source of fear but its antidote. This also explains why, for many people, the message of Yeshua is difficult.

A relationship of filial love with God is far more intimate and more vulnerable than a religion based on fear. Fear creates distance and control. Love creates closeness and inner responsibility. For some, the fear of God is more bearable than nearness to him. It is easier to submit to an authority than to enter into a living relationship.

But the direction in which Yeshua leads is clear: from fear to trust, from submission to relationship, from religion to sonship. What begins as "fear of the Lord" can be the first step of awakening. But the destination is not fear. The destination is love. As another profound biblical statement puts it: "There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear" (1 John 4:18). If fear is the beginning of wisdom, then love is its fulfillment.

And the call of Yeshua seems to be precisely this: to move from the religion of fear to the living relationship with the Father.

## How Yeshua Sees "the Fear of the Lord"

The phrase "the fear of the Lord" runs through the whole Old Testament as the foundation of wisdom: "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom" (Proverbs 9:10). For many, this formulation has become synonymous with dread, religious anxiety, or fear of punishment. But when we look at Yeshua and at the way he reveals God, we notice a profound shift of perspective. He does not abolish "the fear of the Lord," but he transforms it radically.

For Yeshua, the relationship with God begins not with panic but with filial trust. The prayer he offers as a model begins with "Our Father" (Matthew 6:9). This formulation is revolutionary. God is not introduced as a threatening authority but as a Father who is near. In the same discourse, Yeshua says: "Your Father knows what you need before you ask him" (Matthew 6:8). Anxious fear dissolves into a relationship of security.

If the Father knows and cares, fear can no longer be the foundation of spirituality. Yeshua constantly repeats the exhortation: "Do not be afraid" (Luke 12:32). He does not merely say "do not fear people," but "do not be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the Kingdom." Here the essential difference becomes visible. "The fear of the Lord" cannot mean living in terror of God, because God is the one who gives with delight.

Fear is associated with insecurity; the Kingdom is associated with gift. There is, however, a seemingly tense passage: "Do not fear those who kill the body… rather fear him who can destroy the soul as well" (Matthew 10:28). At first glance, it seems that Yeshua is reaffirming religious fear. But the context shows otherwise. He relativizes the fear of human power and reorients it toward the seriousness of the spiritual life.

He does not invite panic before God but lucidity: there is a reality deeper than physical survival. "Fear" here is awareness, not anxiety. In the Sermon on the Mount, Yeshua moves the center of morality from the outside to the inside: "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matthew 5:8). Purity of heart is not the product of fear but of integrity. If the fear of the Lord were terror, the result would be hiding, not clarity.

Instead, Yeshua affirms that the truth sets free: "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32). Freedom and fear cannot coexist as a foundation. Yeshua criticizes a religiosity based on dread and appearance: "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites" (Matthew 23). These people kept the rules out of fear of the Law and of judgment, but their hearts remained far away. Their fear produced rigidity, not compassion.

This is why Yeshua says: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13). Mercy does not grow out of fear but out of closeness. In John 15:15, Yeshua takes a decisive step: "No longer do I call you servants… but friends." The servant lives in fear; the friend lives in trust. If "the fear of the Lord" is the beginning, friendship is maturity. Yeshua does not deny reverence, but he transforms it into a living relationship. God is no longer a distant Master but a Father and a Friend.

On the cross, in the extreme moment of vulnerability, Yeshua does not react out of fear but out of compassion: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). If he had lived out of the fear of God, he would have invoked judgment. But he lives out of unity with the Father, and this unity produces forgiveness. "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30) does not describe superiority but integration. In this integration, fear has no place. So, how does Yeshua see "the fear of the Lord"?

Not as religious terror, nor as moral anxiety, but as the beginning of awakening — the moment in which a person realizes that life is greater than his own ego. It is reverence before reality, not panic before punishment. It is the awareness of truth, not the fear of sanction. For Yeshua, the beginning may be wonder and humility before God. But the end is not fear, but love. "Take heart; I have overcome the world" (John 16:33).

Where love becomes mature, fear loses its function. "Do not be afraid" is not merely consolation; it is the invitation to a relationship in which God is no longer perceived as a threat, but as life.

## What "Taking Up Your Cross" Means

For Yeshua, the phrase "taking up your cross" is not a religious metaphor, nor an ideal of suffering, and still less an invitation to resignation. In the historical context in which he speaks, the cross is the unmistakable symbol of the end of one's social identity. The one who took up his cross was already a man dead to the world: without status, without rights, without a negotiable future. Yeshua uses this symbol to show that discipleship begins precisely where the life lived out of fear comes to an end.

"If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me" (Mark 8:34). "Self-denial" is not self-annihilation but the disidentification from the false self — that self built out of the need for control, validation, and belonging. To take up your cross means to live no longer in order to be accepted, but in order to be true.

This is why Yeshua immediately links the cross to the losing and gaining of life: "For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake will gain it" (Matthew 16:25). The life that is lost is not biological life but the life of the ego — the identity maintained through compromise. The cross is the moment when a person stops trying to save his image, even if this brings conflict, loss, or solitude.

"Whoever does not bear his own cross and come after me cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:27). The cross, for Yeshua, is the assuming of truth all the way to the end, even when truth breaks relationships, destabilizes systems, and exposes the falseness of social peace. Suffering is not sought, but the consequence of truth is accepted. In Paul, the cross remains central, but the emphasis shifts.

For him, the cross is no longer merely an inner process of disidentification; it becomes the theological center of salvation. Paul speaks of an inner crucifixion, but he links it explicitly to the act of faith: "I have been crucified with Christ, and I no longer live… but Christ lives in me" (Galatians 2:20). In Paul, the cross is the place where the "old self" dies, but this death is understood through the lens of a saving event already accomplished by Christ.

From here arises a subtle but major difference: in Yeshua, the cross is the path you walk; in Paul, the cross is the reality you believe in. "The word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God" (1 Corinthians 1:18). Although Paul speaks of real transformation — "our old self was crucified" (Romans 6:6) — in the theology that develops afterward the emphasis falls more and more on faith in the cross, not on the daily living of the cross as the death of the false self.

The cross begins to be venerated, explained, and defended more than it is existentially assumed. In contemporary Christianity, the phrase "taking up your cross" often ends up completely severed from its original meaning. The cross becomes synonymous with an unavoidable suffering, with a toxic relationship accepted, with a diminished life lived out of obligation. "That is my cross" comes to mean resignation, not truth. Suffering is no longer the consequence of courage but the justification of the fear of change.

This interpretation does not come from the words of Yeshua but from a confusion between patience and self-annulment. And yet, Yeshua says clearly: "You will know the truth, and the truth will set you free" (John 8:32). The cross is not meant to keep you captive in a false life but to let that life die. It is not a call to psychological martyrdom but to freedom through truth. Looked at together, the three meanings separate clearly.

In Yeshua, the cross is the death of the false self so that true life may begin. In Paul, the cross is the foundation of salvation, interiorized through faith. In contemporary Christianity, the cross is often reduced to suffering that is tolerated, without transformation. In its original form, the cross is not punishment and it is not virtue. It is the end of negotiation with the ego.

It is the point at which a person ceases to protect himself through lies and accepts the loss of everything that is false, so as not to lose what is alive. To take up your cross does not mean "suffer more," but: stop living against your own truth.

## Loving Your Enemies: What It Dissolves in Us

One of the most radical statements of Yeshua is the call to love one's enemies. "Love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44) is not an exaggerated moral ideal, nor a demand for spiritual performance. It is a direct blow struck at the mechanism by which a person constructs his identity. The enemy is not merely a hostile person; he is the mirror of your defensive identity. He exists because there is an "us" and a "them," a "good" and an "evil," a "righteous" and an "unrighteous."

Yeshua understands that human identity is often constructed through opposition. We know who we are because we know whom we are against. Morality becomes an instrument of demarcation: I am good because someone else is evil. The enemy is necessary in order for the self to feel coherent. When Yeshua says to love him, he is not asking for an artificial feeling but for the dissolution of the structure that needs an enemy. Loving your enemies is not about tolerating abuse or denying evil.

Yeshua does not say that evil becomes good. He says that evil must not define your identity. "If you love those who love you, what reward do you have?" (Matthew 5:46). This is not about recompense, but about freedom: conditional love does not set you free, it confirms you within the same circle. When love extends beyond the boundaries of identity, something breaks within: the need for superiority, the need to be right, the need to win.

This is the true function of the commandment: not to create moral saints, but to disidentify a person from his defensive self. Loving the enemy is impossible as long as the self must be defended. This is why it becomes possible only when fear is no longer the center of life.

## "Follow Me"

The call "Follow me," spoken by Yeshua, is not, first of all, an invitation to doctrinal adherence, but to a radical change of perspective on life. When he says, "Come after me, and I will make you fishers of men" (Matthew 4:19), he is not describing enrollment in a system of ideas but the beginning of an existential journey. To follow him means to enter a movement of transformation in which life acquires a different source.

This is why Yeshua speaks of remaining: "Abide in me" (John 15:4). This abiding is not loyalty to a name, but letting his life become the form of your own, until behavior flows naturally out of a new consciousness. In this process, Yeshua points to a profound disidentification: the renunciation of ego, of fear, of status, and of the attachments that define the old "I."

He says without circumlocution: "If anyone comes after me and does not deny himself, he cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:27). To follow him means to lose the old life in order to gain the true one: "Whoever loses his life for my sake will gain it" (Matthew 16:25). Yeshua promises not only forgiveness but freedom; he asks not only for belief but for living. His method remains simple and alive: "Come and see."

Yeshua does not present himself as an inaccessible ideal, reserved for a divine nature impossible to replicate. On the contrary, his words presuppose that his state is, in a sense, possible for human beings as well. "Whoever believes in me will also do the works that I do; and greater works than these will he do" (John 14:12) makes no sense if Yeshua considered his own life to be unique and impossible to follow.

Likewise, the call "You therefore must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect" (Matthew 5:48) is not a moral irony but an invitation to maturity of consciousness. When he affirms, "The Kingdom of God is within you" (Luke 17:21), he moves the center from the outside to the inside. To follow him means to discover what is already sown within you.

In this light, sin no longer appears as a legal list of prohibitions but as a state of separation, of not-seeing, of inner bondage. When he says to the woman, "Go, and sin no more" (John 8:11), Yeshua is not pronouncing an impossible ideal but affirming the possibility of coming out of this state. He explains the mechanism: "Everyone who commits sin is a slave to sin" (John 8:34), but he also shows the solution: "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32).

To live without sin, for Yeshua, does not mean moral perfection without fault, but living out of unity with the Father, where separation — the source of sin — no longer operates. In the Apostle Paul, the perspective on "following" takes on a different emphasis. Paul did not follow Yeshua during his earthly life but encountered him in a mystical experience after the cross. Hence a different lens.

"It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me" (Galatians 2:20) describes a profound identification, and "By grace you have been saved, not by works" (Ephesians 2:8) introduces dependence on gift. For Paul, to follow Yeshua means to identify with his death and resurrection and to recognize the human inability to save oneself. Paul honestly describes the inner conflict of the human being: "I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want" (Romans 7:19).

In this perspective, following is lived as a continuous struggle, sustained by grace. On the one hand, he affirms the universality of sin: "All have sinned" (Romans 3:23). On the other hand, he proclaims the possibility of a life freed from its dominion: "Sin will have no dominion over you" (Romans 6:14) and "Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh" (Galatians 5:24).

For Paul, life without sin is not a stable state attained definitively, but a permanent dynamic, between fragility and grace. In the practice of today's Christianity, the call "Follow me" has often been transformed into an invitation to belonging, to correct belief, and to the defense of a religious identity. Much is said about Yeshua, but less is lived from his way of being.

Yeshua himself speaks of this rupture: "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me" (Matthew 15:8). And he asks directly: "Why do you call me 'Lord, Lord,' and not do what I tell you?" (Luke 6:46). For him, following is not a title but a life that is lived. Here three different emphases appear. In Yeshua, to follow him means to become — to live out of the same consciousness, to be free of sin through truth.

In Paul, to follow him means to believe and to lean on grace in a continuous struggle. In contemporary Christianity, to follow him often comes to mean to belong. But the final question remains the one spoken by Yeshua to the sick man: "Do you want to be healed?" (John 5:6). Not "Do you want to be forgiven?" but "Do you want to be whole?"

## "The Father's Will" in Yeshua

For Yeshua, the phrase "the Father's will" does not designate a hidden plan that a person must discover with fear and effort, nor a list of commandments demanding execution. It is a state of alignment of life with truth, with love, and with life itself. When he says that "not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the Kingdom of Heaven, but the one who does the will of the Father" (Matthew 7:21), Yeshua is not shifting the emphasis onto religious conformity, but onto authentic living.

To "do" the Father's will means to live out of the same source from which he himself lives. This is why, when he affirms that "whoever does the will of God is my brother and sister and mother" (Mark 3:35), Yeshua is not creating spiritual hierarchies but abolishing the boundaries between people. The Father's will does not separate the "chosen" from "the rest"; it unites those who live out of the same heart. It is not the criterion of belonging to a group but of inner likeness.

This divine will is not a fixed doctrine but a living work. When he says, "My Father is working until now, and I too am working" (John 5:17), Yeshua shows that the Father's will is a continuous movement of life that expresses itself through healing, forgiveness, truth, and freedom. It is not something learned as information but something that manifests itself through a person. The obedience Yeshua speaks of is not fearful submission but inner unity.

The statement "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30) shows that the Father's will and his own are not two separate realities that must be harmonized, but the same life lived without division. This is why he can say that "my food is to do the will of him who sent me" (John 4:34): the Father's will is nourishment, not constraint; it is what sustains life, not what limits it.

When he declares, "I seek not my own will but the will of him who sent me" (John 5:30), Yeshua is not speaking about the annulment of the self but about the dissolution of the ego. The Father's will becomes natural when a person no longer lives out of fear, self-interest, or the need for control. It is not a loss of identity but the liberation of identity from everything that deforms it. For Yeshua, the Father's will is inseparable from the reality of the Kingdom of God.

In the prayer "Your Kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven" (Matthew 6:10), what is requested is not the imposition of a divine law but the manifestation of a state: that what is true on the inner plane should become visible in concrete reality. The Father's will is the coincidence between inner and outer, between truth and deed. In the Apostle Paul, the phrase "the will of God" takes on a different, more normative and more theological emphasis.

When he exhorts, "Do not be conformed to this age, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that you may discern the will of God" (Romans 12:2), the will of God becomes something that must be discerned and applied. The emphasis shifts from unity to conformity, from living to moral evaluation. The same direction appears when Paul says clearly: "This is the will of God, your sanctification" (1 Thessalonians 4:3).

Sanctification becomes the goal, and the will of God acquires a moral-disciplinary dimension. It is no longer so much a natural state of the heart as a standard a person must reach. Contemporary Christianity carries this shift even further. "The will of God" often comes to be perceived as an external authority, unpredictable and at times rigid, which says what must be done, what is permitted, and what is forbidden.

Expressions such as "It was God's will" are used to close off questions and to justify suffering or the lack of clarity. Yeshua, however, never uses "the Father's will" to close off life. On the contrary, he links it to fullness when he says: "I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly" (John 10:10). The Father's will is always associated with full life, not with restriction.

The fundamental difference thus becomes clear: in Yeshua, the Father's will is a state of unity with truth and love; in Paul, the will of God is plan, discernment, and moral order; and in today's Christianity, it often becomes an external authority that demands submission, not awakening. This is why Yeshua never says, "Find out what God wants from you," but shows: live out of the Father, and his will will be done through you.

When he affirms, "If you abide in me, and my words abide in you, ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you" (John 15:7), he suggests that when a person is in this unity, his desire and the Father's will are no longer different things. The Father's will is not imposed. It manifests itself. It is not executed. It is lived.

## "Your Sins Are Forgiven"

When Yeshua speaks the words "your sins are forgiven," he is not performing a deferred legal act, nor invoking an invisible heavenly procedure. In his encounters with people, forgiveness is an act of immediate liberation from the state of separation, shame, and fear. When he says to the paralytic, "Take heart, my son, your sins are forgiven" (Matthew 9:2), and to the sinful woman, "Your faith has saved you; go in peace" (Luke 7:50), forgiveness does not appear as the result of a future event but as a present reality that changes the person on the spot.

Yeshua forgives because he sees the heart, not because a price has been paid. This forgiveness does not validate sin; it dissolves it. When he says to the woman caught in adultery, "Neither do I condemn you; go, and sin no more" (John 8:11), the meaning becomes clear: forgiveness is not a declaration that the deed does not matter, but a liberation that makes a new life possible. The person is not left in the state in which he was, but is drawn out of it.

For Yeshua, sin is not an accounting debt that demands punishment but a wandering of consciousness. The person is lost, not condemned. This is why his parables speak of being found again, not of payment. The father in the parable of the prodigal son does not demand compensation but says: "This my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found" (Luke 15:24). Forgiveness is a return to life, a turning back (metanoia), not a legal acquittal.

Yeshua does not change the Father's attitude toward man; he reveals a love that is already present. Even on the cross, in the midst of violence, Yeshua says: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). Forgiveness does not wait for conditions to be met and does not depend on formal repentance. It flows from the nature of love. When Yeshua affirms "your faith has saved you," faith is not adherence to a doctrine but the opening of the heart that allows healing.

This is why he says that "unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter the Kingdom" (Matthew 18:3). Forgiveness appears at the moment when a person sees the truth about himself without defense and without shame. In this seeing, sin loses its power. The Apostle Paul introduces a major shift of perspective. The forgiveness of sins becomes linked to the death and resurrection of Yeshua, understood as an objective substitutionary act.

He writes that "in him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins" (Ephesians 1:7) and that "Christ died for our sins" (1 Corinthians 15:3). Sin is no longer merely a wandering that calls for being found again, but an objective problem that requires an objective resolution. In this paradigm, forgiveness is no longer spoken in the living encounter between Yeshua and a person but becomes a benefit of faith in a past event.

Paul affirms that "we are justified by faith" (Romans 5:1). The emphasis shifts from healing to justification, from living to status. This shift creates a visible tension. Whereas in Yeshua forgiveness produces change on the spot, in the Pauline paradigm a person can be forgiven "in principle," even if his life remains unchanged. This is why Paul must add moral exhortations: "Do not go on living in sin" (Romans 6:1–2).

Where Yeshua liberates and the person changes, Paul explains forgiveness and then demands change. Contemporary Christianity inherits this explanation and often simplifies it. "Your sins are forgiven" comes to mean the acceptance of a doctrine, a ritual declaration, or a legal assurance for the life to come. Forgiveness becomes something you have, not something you live.

In this context, the words of Yeshua take on a new weight: "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me" (Matthew 15:8). Much is said about forgiveness, but less about the inner liberation it ought to produce. The consequences are visible: chronic guilt, the identity of the "forgiven sinner," and little real transformation of life. The fundamental difference becomes clear.

In Yeshua, "your sins are forgiven" means: you are liberated now and you can live differently. In Paul, "sins are forgiven" means: you have a new status through faith in the cross. In today's Christianity, forgiveness often becomes a theological guarantee, not a healing that is lived. In the spirit of Yeshua, forgiveness does not manage guilt; it dissolves separation. It is not a paper signed in heaven but a door opened in the heart. This is why he says: "The truth will set you free" (John 8:32).

When Yeshua speaks the words "your sins are forgiven," the deep meaning is: you are whole again; return to life.
